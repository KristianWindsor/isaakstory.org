# Chapter One

## The Alde Kerke

Soest, Westphalia — November 29, 1760

---

The bells of St. Petri began at half past nine, their iron voices cracking the frozen air above the rooftops of Soest. They had been ringing over this town for six hundred years — through plagues, through sieges, through the burning of the Thirty Years' War that had emptied the streets of half its people and never quite let them fill again. Now they rang for a wedding, and the sound carried across the Hellweg, the ancient east-west road, and over the walls of a town that had once been the beating heart of Westphalia and was now little more than a garrison village of three thousand souls.

It was the kind of late November morning when the cold has a smell to it — damp stone and wood smoke and the faint mineral tang of the salt springs that had made Soest famous a thousand years ago. Frost clung to the green sandstone of the church walls. The bare lindens in the Petrikirchhof stood black against a sky the color of pewter. Across the narrow lane, so close you could nearly reach out and touch its massive tower, the Catholic Dom of St. Patrokli loomed in silence — its own bells still. Two churches, two faiths, separated by a few meters of cobblestone.

Inside, St. Petri was cold enough that you could see your breath.

The *Alde Kerke* — the Old Church, as the people of Soest called it — was the oldest parish church in Westphalia, and it looked it. Its Romanesque nave was a forest of thick stone pillars, their surfaces worn smooth by centuries of shoulders brushing past. The vaulted ceiling above was painted — Christ in majesty, saints with faded gilt halos, patterns of vines and stars that disappeared into the shadows where the candlelight could not reach. The windows were narrow, as Romanesque windows are, and on a morning like this they admitted only a thin, grey wash of light that barely reached the flagstone floor.

Thirty people were gathered in the nave. In better times, in the Soest that had been a Hanseatic capital with ten thousand citizens, this church would have been full for a wedding. But those times were two centuries gone. Half the young men of Soest were somewhere to the east with Frederick's army, or buried in the Saxon mud, or simply gone. French troops had marched through Westphalia that summer. There had been a battle at Warburg in July, not eighty kilometers to the north, and the older men in the congregation had heard the distant rumble of the guns.

And yet here they were. A wedding.

Anna Elizabeth Wittenberg stood at the door of St. Petri and tried not to shiver.

She was twenty years old. She had been born far from here — in Lubelskie, in the Polish lands, more than a thousand kilometers to the east, in a German-speaking community that clung to its language and its Lutheran faith amid a sea of Slavic Catholicism. She had come west along the migration routes that connected the scattered German communities of Poland to the heart of the old country, and somewhere along the way — in Brandenburg, in the flat, sandy country east of Berlin — she had met Andreas Micheal Isaak.

They had fallen in love, or come to an understanding, or been matched by families who knew each other through the invisible web of kinship and confession that linked German Lutherans across half of Europe. However it happened, by the winter of 1757 Anna Elizabeth was pregnant, and in 1758 she gave birth to a son. They named him Johann Michel.

They were not married.

In another time, this would have been a simple thing to remedy. A betrothed couple who conceived a child before the wedding was a common enough story in the German lands — embarrassing, subject to a fine and perhaps a public penance before the congregation, but ultimately resolved by a hasty marriage with a dispensation to skip the banns. The pastor would have frowned. The neighbors would have counted the months on their fingers. And then life would have gone on.

But 1758 was not another time. It was the third year of the Seven Years' War, and Brandenburg was burning.

That summer, fifty-two thousand Russian soldiers under General Fermor crossed the Oder and laid siege to the fortress town of Küstrin, not far from the Polish border. On August 25, Frederick threw thirty-five thousand Prussians against them at Zorndorf in one of the bloodiest battles of the war. The fields east of Berlin became a killing ground. Towns were razed. Civilians fled westward with whatever they could carry — and among them, almost certainly, were a young man named Andreas Isaak, a young woman named Anna Elizabeth Wittenberg, and an infant boy.

They came to Soest. Whether it was Andreas's family's town, or simply a place behind the Anglo-Hanoverian lines where a Lutheran family from the east could find shelter, we do not know for certain. What we know is that for two years they lived here as an unmarried couple with a child — a status that carried real weight in a Lutheran community. The church required documentation before it would perform a marriage: proof of income, proof that the couple would not become a burden on the parish, and for Anna Elizabeth, a letter from her home congregation in Poland confirming that the banns had been read and there were no impediments. Getting that letter from Lubelskie while Russian armies occupied the roads between Westphalia and Poland was a feat of patience and luck. Two years of patience. Two years of waiting, with a child growing up unbaptized in his parents' marriage, in a town that was not quite home, in a country that was not quite at peace.

Now, finally, they stood at the door of St. Petri.

Anna Elizabeth wore black. Not mourning, but custom. A German bride wore her finest dark dress to her wedding, a tradition older than anyone could explain. Hers was heavy wool, practical, the kind of dress you wore again to christenings and feast days and, eventually, to funerals. At her throat, a scrap of white lace. On her head, a wreath of dried myrtle — the *Myrtenkranz* — its small, dark leaves woven into a crown that declared what the church required be publicly known: that she was entering this contract freely. Two-year-old Johann Michel was somewhere behind her, held by a relative or neighbor, too young to understand what was happening but old enough to be the reason it was happening at all.

Beside her stood Andreas Micheal Isaak. He was twenty-one — the oldest Isaak we can name with certainty in this place, though the name itself suggests roots that stretch further back into Brandenburg. He wore his best clothes: knee breeches, a waistcoat, a long coat that had been his father's. His hands were rough. He was not a wealthy man. But he had survived the flight from the east, found work, assembled the paperwork, endured whatever public penance the pastor had required for the sin of fathering a child outside of wedlock, and brought his family to this door. It had taken two years. He was here now.

The pastor stood before them in the doorway.

This was the old way, Luther's way, laid down in the *Traubüchlein* — the little marriage booklet that Martin Luther himself had written in 1529 and that had governed Lutheran weddings for over two hundred years. The vows were exchanged here, at the threshold, in the cold November air, so that the whole community could witness that this thing had been done freely and publicly.

The pastor had already read the banns on three consecutive Sundays from the pulpit of St. Petri. No one had objected. Anna Elizabeth had presented her papers — a letter from her home parish, obtained God knows how, confirming that she was free to marry. A woman from Poland, marrying in Westphalia, in the middle of a war. The paperwork alone was a small miracle.

Now the pastor spoke the words.

*"Andreas, do you desire to have Anna Elizabeth as your wedded wife?"*

*"Yes."*

*"Anna Elizabeth, do you desire to have Andreas as your wedded husband?"*

*"Yes."*

They exchanged rings. Plain bands. The pastor joined their hands.

*"What God has joined together, let no one separate."*

And with that, they were married. Legally, publicly, finally. Johann Michel Isaak — two years old, born in Brandenburg during a Russian invasion, carried westward across the breadth of Prussia — was now a legitimate son.

The congregation parted, and Andreas and Anna Elizabeth walked together through the Romanesque doorway and into the nave — into the cold, candlelit darkness of the oldest church in Westphalia, past the thick stone pillars, up the worn flagstones toward the altar. At the altar, the pastor opened his Bible and read the words from Genesis that Luther had prescribed.

*"Then the Lord God said, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper who will stand by his side.'"*

The pastor prayed. He blessed the marriage. The organ filled the vaults and rattled the old glass in the narrow windows.

It was done.

---

That evening, at an inn near the Marktplatz, there was a modest meal — bread, beer, what meat could be had. The night before, their friends and family had gathered in the street for the *Polterabend*, hurling stoneware and crockery against the cobblestones to shatter it — never glass, only earthenware — while the couple swept up the shards together, a ritual older than Christianity, meant to drive away evil spirits and prove that a marriage begins not with perfection but with broken things made whole by shared labor.

They had already proven that.

Somewhere to the east, the war ground on. Three weeks earlier, Frederick had thrown forty-five thousand men against the Austrian lines at Torgau and lost thirteen thousand of them in a single afternoon. The Russians had occupied Berlin in October. The Prussian treasury was empty, the silver melted, the currency debased. But in Soest, on this night, none of that mattered. A family that had been made in flight was now made whole in law.

Johann Michel would not be the last child. The Isaak family was just beginning, and the road ahead of them — eastward, always eastward, from Westphalia back across the German lands and into the Russian steppe of Bessarabia, and then, generations later, westward again across an ocean to the Dakota Territory and finally to California — was longer than any of them could have imagined.

It all began here. In the *Alde Kerke*. In the cold. In the candlelight. With a two-year-old boy in someone's arms, and two young people who had already learned that the world does not wait for you to be ready.

# Chapter Two

## The Warsaw Colonists

---

*In the decades following their wedding at the Alde Kerke, Andreas and Anna Elizabeth Isaak raised a family in Soest. Their eldest son, Johann Michel — born before their marriage, in Brandenburg, during the chaos of the Seven Years' War — grew into a young man in a town with no future. Soest was quiet, poor, and shrinking.*

*Andreas Micheal Isaak died in 1792, at the age of fifty-three. He did not live to see what came next.*

*Far to the east, the ancient kingdom of Poland was dying. In 1772, 1793, and 1795, three of Europe's great powers — Prussia, Austria, and Russia — carved the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth into pieces and erased it from the map. Prussia swallowed the western territories and renamed them South Prussia. The land was vast, fertile, and underpopulated. The Prussian king wanted German farmers to settle it.*

*Johann Michel Isaak answered the call. Sometime in the late 1780s, he left Westphalia and traveled east to the Prussian Polish territories, joining thousands of other German settlers — Lutherans, Swabians, Westphalians, Brandenburgers — who were promised land, tax relief, and the chance to build something of their own. He settled near a village called Blacka, in western Poland. He married. His first son was born in 1789, the same year a revolution in Paris began tearing down the old order of Europe. His mother, Anna Elisabeth — widowed, resilient, already in her fifties — came east with him.*

*For nearly twenty years, the German colonists in Poland lived under Prussian protection. They built farms, churches, and schools. They spoke German, worshipped as Lutherans, and kept to their own communities. It was not home, exactly — but it was theirs.*

*Then Napoleon came.*

*In 1806, the French emperor crushed the Prussian army and seized the Polish territories. From the wreckage, he created the Duchy of Warsaw — a French client state with a Polish government. Overnight, the German settlers lost their Prussian protections, their legal standing, and their security. They were now foreigners in someone else's country, surrounded by a Polish population that remembered who had colonized their land.*

*By 1812, the situation was catastrophic. Napoleon marched 615,000 soldiers through Poland on his way to invade Russia, stripping the countryside of food, horses, and men. Ninety thousand Poles were conscripted into the Grande Armée. Most of them never came back. The German colonists, caught between an empire that had abandoned them and an army that devoured everything in its path, could only watch and wait.*

*It was in these years — the years of Napoleon, of famine, of uncertainty — that Johann Michel Isaak's younger sons came of age.*

---

Blacka, Western Poland — June, 1812

The wheat was not yet ripe when the soldiers came.

Karl August Isaak was nine years old. He was in the east field with his older brother Johann Michael, pulling weeds from between the rows, when they heard it — a low sound, felt more than heard at first, like distant thunder on a clear day. Johann Michael straightened up and shaded his eyes, looking south toward the road. Karl August stood beside him, the pulled weeds still in his fist, and followed his brother's gaze.

The road was a kilometer away, visible as a pale line cut through the green where it crossed the low rise beyond the Kowalski farm. Something was moving on it. At first it looked like a dark stain spreading slowly across the landscape, too wide to be a wagon train, too steady to be a herd. Then the stain resolved into shapes — men on horseback, men on foot, wagons, cannon on wheeled carriages — and the low sound sharpened into something recognizable: the rhythmic crunch of thousands of boots on packed earth, the jangle of harness, the distant snap of pennants in the wind.

"Get down," Johann Michael said. He was fourteen, old enough to know what an army looked like, old enough to know you did not want an army to notice you. He put a hand on Karl August's shoulder and pulled him into a crouch between the wheat rows.

They watched from the field as the column passed. It took a long time. The cavalry came first — lancers with red and white pennants, their horses kicking up clouds of dust that drifted across the fields like smoke. Then the infantry, regiment after regiment, their blue and white uniforms smudged grey with road dirt, muskets on their shoulders, faces blank with the particular exhaustion of men who have been marching for weeks and know they will march for weeks more. Behind them came the artillery — heavy guns on iron-wheeled carriages, each one pulled by teams of horses that were already too thin. And behind the artillery, stretching back as far as Karl August could see, came the supply wagons, the ambulance carts, the sutlers, the camp followers, the women and children and dogs that trailed behind every army like a second army of their own.

Karl August had seen soldiers before. Small patrols, couriers, the occasional squadron of cavalry passing through the village on their way somewhere else. But he had never seen anything like this. The column filled the road from edge to edge and spilled over into the fields on both sides, and still it kept coming — an endless river of men and horses and iron flowing east, east, always east, toward Russia.

"How many?" Karl August whispered.

Johann Michael shook his head. He didn't know. Nobody in Blacka had ever seen this many people in one place. Later, they would learn that what they had seen was a fraction of Napoleon's Grande Armée — 615,000 men from twenty nations, the largest invasion force Europe had ever assembled, crossing the Duchy of Warsaw on its way to destroy the Russian Empire. Ninety thousand of those men were Poles, conscripted from the very fields and villages the column was now trampling.

"Will they take our wheat?" Karl August asked.

"They'll take everything," Johann Michael said.

They stayed in the field until the column had passed and the dust had settled and the sound had faded to a hum on the eastern horizon. When they walked back to the farm, they found their father standing in the yard with their grandmother, Anna Elisabeth, watching the last of the wagons disappear. Johann Michel was sixty-four years old. Anna Elisabeth was seventy-two. She had been born in Lubelskie, married in Westphalia, widowed in Soest or Poland — and now she stood in a village in the Duchy of Warsaw and watched Napoleon's army march past her door on its way to a war that would kill a million men in six months.

She said nothing. She had seen armies before.

Their father turned to the boys. "Did they come into the field?"

"No," Johann Michael said.

"They will," his father said. "Tomorrow, or the next day. They'll send foragers. We need to move the stores."

That night, the family buried half their grain in the root cellar beneath the barn, wrapped in oilcloth and covered with straw. It was a crime — requisition officers had the authority to search farms and seize supplies, and hiding food from the army was punishable by imprisonment or worse. Johann Michel did it anyway. He had been a Prussian subject, then a subject of the Duchy of Warsaw, and he knew that governments came and went but winter came every year, and if you did not have grain in the ground come November, your children starved.

The foragers came two days later, as he had predicted. They took a third of the visible stores, two of the family's four horses, and the good wagon. They left a requisition receipt stamped with Napoleon's eagle — a piece of paper that promised future payment from a government that would not exist in eighteen months.

---

Six months passed. The Grande Armée did not come back. What came back were rumors, then refugees, then the wreckage.

By December, the survivors began staggering west through the Duchy — gaunt, frostbitten men in rags, many of them barefoot in the snow, their eyes emptied of everything except the animal desire to keep moving. Of the 615,000 who had crossed the Neman in June, fewer than 100,000 came back. The rest were dead in the Russian snow, or captured, or simply gone. Behind the survivors came the Russian army, advancing steadily westward, occupying the Duchy of Warsaw without resistance.

Karl August watched them too — the defeated men shuffling through the village in ones and twos, begging for bread, sleeping in barns, some of them dying where they fell. He was nine years old and he understood, in the way that children understand catastrophe, that the world had broken and would not be put back together the same way.

By the autumn of 1813, the Duchy of Warsaw had ceased to exist in any meaningful sense. Russian troops controlled the territory. Napoleon was retreating across Germany toward France. And in the German colonists' villages, in the churches and farmhouses and meeting halls where they gathered to speak their own language, a new word was circulating.

*Bessarabien.*

---

Johann Michel first heard the Tsar's offer in the autumn of 1813, though the formal manifesto was issued on November 29 — the same date, to the day, as his parents' wedding at St. Petri, fifty-three years earlier. Tsar Alexander I was calling for German settlers to colonize Bessarabia, a vast territory between the Danube and the Dniester rivers that Russia had taken from the Ottoman Empire the year before. The terms were astonishing: sixty-six hectares of free land for every family. Freedom of religion. Exemption from military service. A ten-year tax holiday. Money for building a house. It was, in every respect, the same offer that had brought Johann Michel to Poland a generation earlier — only further east, and from a different emperor.

The German colonists in Poland listened with open ears. They had been promised security by Prussia and abandoned by Prussia. They had been promised independence by Napoleon and crushed by Napoleon. The land they farmed was about to change hands for the fourth time in twenty years. Their churches had been neglected, their schools underfunded, their legal standing uncertain. More than 1,500 German families would accept the Tsar's offer between 1814 and 1815 alone. They would leave behind farms they had spent decades building, because they had learned — in the hardest possible way — that a farm means nothing if the government above you does not want you there.

Johann Michael, at fifteen, understood this in the way that children of unstable countries understand it — not as politics or history, but as the texture of daily life. You planted crops in the spring without knowing whose flag would fly over the village by harvest. You learned Polish because you had to, but you spoke German at home because your father insisted. You watched soldiers come and go and you learned to stay out of their way. You grew up fast, because the world did not slow down for boys.

Karl August, at ten, understood less but felt more. He knew the farm. He knew the church. He knew his grandmother's face — Anna Elisabeth, eighty-three years old now, the woman who had stood at the door of St. Petri in a black dress and a myrtle crown more than half a century ago, who had outlived her husband by twenty-one years and crossed half of Europe and was still here, still watching, still quiet in the way of women who have seen too many armies pass. He knew the sound the wind made in the flat Polish fields in October, when the harvest was in and the sky was the color of iron. He did not yet know that he would leave this place and never come back.

But that was coming. The Tsar's recruiters were in the village. The older men were talking in low voices after church. Anna Elisabeth sat by the stove and said nothing, because she had heard offers like this before — in Soest, in Brandenburg, in every place the Isaak family had ever been — and she knew what came next.

And their father, Johann Michel Isaak — the son of Andreas and Anna Elisabeth, the boy who had been born in Brandenburg during a Russian invasion and carried west to Soest and then east to Poland — was already making up his mind.

The Isaak family had been moving for three generations. They were about to move again.

# Chapter Three

## The Steppe

---

*In January 1813, the Russian army occupied Warsaw. Napoleon's Duchy crumbled. The Congress of Vienna, convened the following year, divided what remained of Poland between Russia, Prussia, and Austria — the same three empires that had carved it up a generation before. For the German colonists who had settled in the Polish territories under Prussian protection, the Congress changed nothing and everything: the land beneath their feet was the same, but the government above them had shifted once again, and with it any claim they had to belonging.*

*The Tsar, however, had plans for them.*

*On November 29, 1813, Alexander I issued a manifesto calling for German settlers to colonize Bessarabia — a vast, treeless steppe between the Danube and the Dniester rivers that Russia had seized from the Ottoman Empire just one year earlier. The terms were extraordinary: sixty-six hectares of free land per family, freedom of religion, perpetual exemption from military service, a ten-year tax holiday, government loans for houses and seed, and a daily food allowance of five kopecks per person until the first harvest. The settlers had to be debt-free and willing to leave behind everything they could not carry.*

*More than 1,500 German families accepted. They traveled in columns — Kolonnen — by horse and wagon, southeast across the breadth of Europe, a journey of roughly 2,000 kilometers that took between two and six months. They were the first Germans to arrive in Bessarabia, in 1814, and they were known ever after as the Warsaw Colonists.*

*Among them were the Isaaks.*

---

Blacka, Western Poland — Spring, 1815

They sold what they could not carry and buried what they could not sell.

Johann Michel Isaak spent the first weeks of April settling his accounts — paying off the last of his debts to the village miller, selling the plow and the heavier tools to a Polish neighbor for less than they were worth, trading the milk cow for a second horse because you could not pull a loaded wagon across two thousand kilometers of road with one animal. The farm itself — the house, the barn, the fenced kitchen garden that Anna Elisabeth had tended for a quarter century — could not be sold. It belonged to the land, and the land belonged to whoever governed it this year, and that was no longer a question Johann Michel cared to answer. He walked away from it.

Karl August watched all of this with the particular attentiveness of a twelve-year-old who understands that something enormous is happening but has not yet been told the full shape of it. He knew they were leaving. He knew the word *Bessarabien*, though he could not have found it on a map. He knew his father had been to the Russian recruiters' office in the nearest town and had come back with papers — a thick packet sealed with red wax — and that his grandmother, Anna Elisabeth, had held the papers in her hands for a long time without speaking, turning them over as if the weight of them might tell her something the words could not.

She was seventy-five years old. She had been born in Lubelskie, Poland. She had married in Soest, Westphalia. She had been widowed twenty-three years ago, when Andreas died in 1792\. She had followed her son to Poland and watched Napoleon's army march past her door and buried grain in the root cellar and waited, as she always waited, for the next thing to happen. Now the next thing was a wagon and a road that led southeast, toward a country she had never seen, and she was going.

Johann Michael — seventeen now, nearly a man — did the heavy work. He loaded the wagon in the grey April mornings while his father went from house to house in the German quarter, coordinating with the other families who were leaving. They would travel in a *Kolonne* — a column of wagons, twenty or thirty families together, for safety and for the simple human need to not be alone on a road that long. Johann Michael checked the axles, greased the wheels, fitted the canvas cover that would be their roof for the next four months. He packed the tools they were keeping — an axe, a saw, a set of chisels, a scythe — in oilcloth beneath the wagon bed. He packed the Bible and the hymnal in a wooden box lined with straw, and set them where they would not get wet.

Karl August's job was the animals. He fed and watered the two horses. He checked their hooves. He was responsible for the crate of chickens that would ride on the back of the wagon, and for the dog, a brown mutt named nothing in particular, who would walk alongside.

On the last evening, Karl August found his brother behind the barn, sitting on an upturned bucket, looking east across the fields. The wheat they had planted last autumn was coming up in pale green rows — wheat they would never harvest. Someone else would cut it, or no one would.

"What do you think it looks like?" Karl August said.

Johann Michael didn't answer right away. He picked up a clod of the dark Polish soil — rich, heavy stuff, the reason their father had come here in the first place — and turned it over in his hands.

"Father says there are no trees."

"None?"

"He says the grass is taller than a man and there is nothing else. No forest. No hills. Just grass and sky."

Karl August tried to imagine this and could not. He had never seen a landscape without trees. The woods beyond the village — birch and oak and linden, thick enough that you could lose sight of the road in summer — were the boundary of his known world. A country with no trees was as abstract to him as a country with no ground.

"What about the house?"

"There is no house. We build it when we get there."

"Out of what?"

Johann Michael looked at him. He was seventeen, and he had their father's face — the same long jaw, the same patient, slightly tired expression of a man who has learned not to expect too much from the world. "I don't know," he said. "Sod, maybe. Or whatever the Russians give us. Father says they give us money for building."

"How much money?"

"Enough, supposedly."

Karl August sat down on the ground next to the bucket. The spring air smelled like wet earth and new growth — the smell of Poland, the only country he had ever known, though it had been called three different things in his twelve years of life.

"Grandmother says the first ones who went last year are already there," he said. "She says they named the towns after battles. Borodino. Tarutino."

"Kulm," Johann Michael said. "That's where we're going. Named after the Battle of Kulm."

"Who won?"

"We did. The Russians, I mean. Against Napoleon."

Karl August pulled at the grass. "Is that a good sign? Living in a place named after a battle?"

Johann Michael almost smiled. "It's a better sign than living in a place named after nothing, which is what this place will be once we leave."

He tossed the clod of soil into the dark and stood up. "Get some sleep. We leave at dawn."

---

They left on a Tuesday in late April, when the roads had dried enough to hold a wagon's weight. The *Kolonne* assembled at the crossroads east of the village — twenty-two families, eighty-some people, a dozen wagons, twice as many horses and oxen, assorted livestock, and everything they owned in the world stacked and lashed and balanced on wooden frames that creaked with every rut in the road.

Anna Elisabeth rode in the wagon. She sat on a folded blanket on the bench seat beside Johann Michel, who drove. She did not look back at the village. She had done this before — left a place she knew for a place she did not — and she understood that looking back was a luxury for people who had a choice. Johann Michael and Karl August walked alongside, as most of the younger people did, because the wagons were too heavily loaded to carry anyone who could walk.

The route led southeast. From western Poland they crossed into Galicia — Austrian territory — skirting the northern edge of the Carpathian foothills. The roads were bad. In places they were not roads at all but mud tracks that swallowed wagon wheels to the hub, requiring the men to unload, lever the wagon free, reload, and continue. They averaged fifteen to twenty kilometers a day when the weather held, less when it rained, and it rained often.

The *Kolonne* developed its own rhythm. They rose before dawn, fed the animals, ate whatever bread or porridge could be managed, and were moving by first light. They stopped at midday to rest the horses and eat again, then pushed on until the light began to fail. Camp was made wherever the ground was relatively dry — in a field, beside a stream, under the lee of a hill if they were lucky. The women cooked over open fires while the men tended the animals and checked the wagons for damage. The children gathered wood and water and played in the margins of the road, the way children do even in the middle of a migration, because twelve-year-olds do not stop being twelve-year-olds just because the world is rearranging itself around them.

Karl August collected things. Stones, mostly — interesting ones, unusually shaped or colored — which he kept in his coat pockets until they wore holes in the lining and his mother made him throw them out. He collected feathers, too, and once a spent musket ball he found in a ditch near what must have been a battlefield, though there was no marker and no one could say which battle or which war. He showed it to Johann Michael, who turned it over in his palm and said, "There are enough of those where we're going. Don't keep it." Karl August kept it anyway.

Johann Michael walked mostly in silence. He was old enough to feel the weight of what they were doing — old enough to understand that his father was fifty-seven and his grandmother was seventy-five and that the journey itself might kill them, that people died on these migrations from dysentery and fever and simple exhaustion, that the wagons broke and the horses went lame and the food ran out before the road did. He watched the country change around them — the flat Polish farmland giving way to gentle hills, then steeper hills, then the long, rolling uplands of the Galician borderlands — and he tried to imagine what lay on the other side of all of it.

They crossed rivers whose names no one in the *Kolonne* knew. They passed through villages where the language changed from Polish to Ukrainian to something else — Moldavian, perhaps — and the churches went from Catholic to Orthodox, their onion domes strange and beautiful against the sky. They bought bread when they could and shot rabbits when they couldn't. One of the wagons broke an axle outside a town whose name Karl August never learned, and the whole column stopped for two days while a local wheelwright made the repair and charged them triple for it.

Anna Elisabeth endured. She did not complain, because complaining was not something she did, and because she understood — better than any of them, better than her son, better than her grandsons — that the road was the price of arrival, and that you did not argue with the price after you had already started walking.

---

They reached Bessarabia in late summer, after nearly four months on the road.

The steppe announced itself gradually, then all at once. The hills flattened. The trees thinned and then vanished. The sky, which had been a ceiling, became a dome — enormous, pale, pressing down on a landscape so flat and featureless that the horizon was a perfect unbroken line in every direction. The grass was waist-high and silver-green in the August heat, and it moved in the wind like water, rolling in long slow waves that made Karl August dizzy if he watched too long.

There was nothing else. No fences, no roads, no buildings, no trees. Just grass and sky and the sound of insects and the hot, dry wind that blew from the southeast and tasted like dust and distance.

Karl August stood in the back of the wagon and looked out over the steppe and felt, for the first time in his life, genuinely afraid. Not of anything specific — there was nothing to be afraid of, which was precisely the problem. There was *nothing*. The landscape offered no shelter, no shade, no landmark by which you could orient yourself. It was the largest emptiness he had ever seen, and they were going to live in it.

Johann Michael stood beside him. He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "We'll need to dig a well."

It was such a practical thing to say — so ordinary, so much like their father — that Karl August laughed, and the laugh broke something open in him, and after that the steppe did not seem quite so terrifying.

---

Russian officials met the *Kolonne* at a staging point near the Kogylnik River and directed them to their assigned parcels. The colony had been laid out according to plan — a central road with farm lots extending back on either side, each family receiving their sixty-six hectares of treeless steppe. The settlement was called Kulm, after the battle where the allied armies had defeated Napoleon's forces in August 1813\. It had been officially established the year before, in 1814, by the first wave of Warsaw Colonists, but when the Isaaks arrived, there was almost nothing to show for it — a few sod houses, some freshly broken ground, a well or two, and a wooden cross marking where the church would eventually stand.

The government provided what it had promised: money for building materials, seed for the first planting, a daily food allowance until the harvest came in. It was not much. The houses the colonists built that first year were *Erdhütten* — earth huts, dug partially into the ground, with walls of sod bricks and roofs of bundled steppe grass. They were dark, damp, and cold in winter. They leaked when it rained. They were home.

Johann Michel Isaak was fifty-seven years old. He had left Soest as a young man, built a farm in Poland, lost it to Napoleon, and walked two thousand kilometers to build another one in a country where the wind never stopped and there were no trees to break it. He would spend the rest of his life here, on this steppe, in this colony named after a battle he had not fought in, growing wheat in soil he had never seen before, speaking German to his neighbors and Russian to the officials and praying in a church that did not yet exist.

Anna Elisabeth was seventy-five. She had outlived her husband by twenty-three years. She had outlived the Electorate of Cologne, the Kingdom of Prussia as it had been, the Duchy of Warsaw, and Napoleon himself. She would live another twenty years, dying in Kulm in 1835 at the age of ninety-five — the oldest woman in the colony, or close to it, a living thread connecting the Alde Kerke in Soest to the sod houses of the Bessarabian steppe.

There was a saying among the Bessarabian Germans, passed down from the first generation to the last: *Die ersten fanden den Tod, die zweiten die Not, erst die dritten das Brot.*

The first generation found death. The second found need. Only the third found bread.

The Isaaks had just arrived. The dying had not yet begun. But the bread — the bread was three generations away, and every one of those generations would earn it.

# Chapter Four

## Black Earth

---

*By 1844, the colony of Kulm was nearly thirty years old. The sod huts of the first settlers had given way to whitewashed mud-brick houses roofed with thatch, each one set on a narrow lot that stretched back a quarter kilometer into the steppe. Acacia trees — planted by the colonists themselves, because the steppe had none — lined the main street and shaded the yards.*

*The first generation had found death. The second had found need. The third was beginning — just beginning — to find bread.*

*Karl Isaak was forty-eight. He had walked to Bessarabia as a nineteen-year-old and spent his entire adult life turning steppe grass into wheat fields. He had married Anna Elizabeth Louise Schimke in 1819 and given her more children than either of them could easily feed. The farm was sixty-six hectares of the blackest soil in Europe — so rich it needed no fertilizer, so flat you could watch your dog run away for three days.*

*Gottlieb Isaak, Karl's son, was ten years old.*

---

Kulm, Bessarabia — A day in late summer, 1844

The rooster woke him, as it always did, in the grey half-light before dawn. Gottlieb lay on the straw mattress he shared with his brother Andreas and listened to the sounds of the house coming alive — his mother at the stove, the iron clang of the door, water being poured into the kettle.

He dressed in the dark. Linen shirt, wool trousers that had been his brother Gottfried's before they were his, leather boots that were too big and stuffed with rags at the toes. The house was mud-brick — thick walls mixed from the black earth and straw, dried in the summer sun and whitewashed inside and out until they gleamed. Two rooms: the *Stube*, where the family lived and ate around the big clay stove, and the *Kammer* in the back, where his parents slept. The floor was packed earth, swept clean by his mother every morning before anyone else was awake.

His father was already in the yard. Karl Isaak rose before the rooster — a habit from the early years, he said, when the wolves still came at night.

"Feed the chickens," his father said. "Then come eat."

This had been a sore subject lately. Three weeks ago, something had gotten into the henhouse. Gottlieb had been the one to find them — feathers everywhere, blood on the straw, two hens dead and a third dying with her throat torn open. His father said it was a fox. Old Herr Flegel down the street said it was a wolf, the last wolf in the Budschak, come down from the hills to remind the Germans that the steppe had been here first. Karl had patched the henhouse with heavier boards, set a trap baited with offal, and told Gottlieb to check the coop every morning before dawn.

Gottlieb checked. The chickens were alive, irritable, and unimpressed. Four eggs today. He carried them inside in his cap, and his mother cracked two into the pan without comment. Breakfast was eggs, dark bread with lard, and chicory coffee. His father said grace — the same grace in the same German, the same words reaching back through the family like a rope to a church in Westphalia none of them had ever seen.

---

School was a low mud-brick building near the prayer house. One room, one teacher, one purpose: to make Lutheran Germans out of the children of Lutheran Germans.

The teacher was the *Küster* — the sexton — Herr Brauer, a thick-shouldered man with a voice that could fill the prayer house without effort. He also led the Sunday service most weeks, because the pastor lived in Tarutino, an hour's ride away, and could only come once or twice a month. The schoolbooks were the Bible, the hymnal, and Luther's Small Catechism. Reading was taught from Scripture. Writing was taught by copying Scripture. Arithmetic was taught because you needed it to measure land and sell wheat, and for no other reason.

What Gottlieb liked was the singing. The old Lutheran hymns — *Ein feste Burg*, *Nun danket alle Gott* — were the best part of school and the best part of Sunday and, if he was honest, the best part of being alive. When the whole village sang together in the prayer house, the sound was enormous, far bigger than the small building that contained it, and Gottlieb felt that he was part of something that could not be taken away — something that did not change when the borders changed or the government changed or the language on the official documents changed. The hymns were the same. The words were the same. The God was the same.

School let out at noon. Gottlieb walked home along the main street, kicking dust, past the whitewashed houses with their acacia trees and kitchen gardens, past the smithy and the prayer house and the cemetery where the oldest stones — the ones from the hunger years — were already sinking into the black earth as if the steppe were slowly swallowing them.

---

He was halfway home when he saw the camel.

It was standing in Herr Raugust's yard, tied to the fence post, chewing something with an expression of such magnificent contempt that Gottlieb stopped in the road and stared. He had seen camels before — the Nogai Tatars who grazed their flocks on the open steppe east of the colony sometimes brought them through the village, and a few of the Bulgarian traders used them to haul salt from the coastal lakes near the Black Sea. But he had never gotten used to them. They were ridiculous animals. They walked as if they had too many knees, looked at you sideways like they knew something you didn't, and made a noise — a deep, gargling groan — that sounded like a very old man complaining about his dinner.

This one was enormous, taller than a horse by half, its humped back piled with bundles of raw wool. A Tatar trader was sitting cross-legged in the shade of the fence, drinking something from a leather flask, while Herr Raugust examined the wool and shook his head and named a price that the Tatar rejected by spitting on the ground.

Gottlieb edged closer. The camel turned its head and regarded him with one enormous brown eye, its jaw working in a slow circle. It had the longest eyelashes he had ever seen on any creature, and its lips were curled back in what looked exactly like a sneer.

"Don't stand behind it," the Tatar said in broken German, without looking up. "It kicks."

Gottlieb moved to the side. The camel followed him with that one contemptuous eye. Then it groaned — that deep, wet, gurgling sound — and a string of green saliva swung from its lower lip like a pendulum.

Gottlieb laughed. He couldn't help it. The animal was absurd — too tall, too ugly, too proud of itself for something that looked like God had assembled it from leftover parts. His brother Gottfried had once said that God made the camel on the seventh day, after He was already tired, and Gottlieb believed it. No rested creator would have put that face on any living thing.

The Tatar and Herr Raugust were still arguing about the wool. Gottlieb watched for another minute, then walked home, grinning, the camel's offended expression stuck in his head like a song.

---

The afternoon was fieldwork. The wheat stood golden and heavy in the fields beyond the village, and Karl and his older sons cut it with scythes, working in long rows from dawn until the light failed, bending and straightening in a rhythm so ancient it preceded agriculture itself. Gottlieb followed behind with the rake, gathering the cut stalks into sheaves and binding them with twists of straw. Hot, repetitive, back-breaking work. Every sheaf was food for the winter, and winter in Bessarabia was not a thing you took lightly.

The steppe wind blew from the southeast, steady and warm, smelling of dry grass and distance. It never stopped. It was the one constant of life in Bessarabia — more reliable than the rain, more permanent than the government, more faithful than anything except the church. You did not fight the wind. You built your house with its back to it, planted your acacias to slow it, and eventually you stopped hearing it at all, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

They worked until the sun touched the western horizon — a horizon so flat and far that the sunset seemed to take an hour, the sky turning from blue to gold to copper to a deep, bruised red that spread across the entire dome of the heavens. It was the most beautiful thing about this country. The land was plain. The village was plain. The work was plain. But the sky was operatic, excessive, absurdly generous — a sky that belonged to a grander country than this one, a sky that had been here before the Germans and the Bulgarians and the Tatars and the Turks, and that would be here long after all of them were gone.

Gottlieb walked home in the blue dusk, shoulders aching, hands raw from the straw, his boots heavy with the black earth that stuck to everything and never fully came off. His mother had supper on the table — soup with potatoes and onions, bread, a glass of the thin wine that Karl made from his own vines. Grace was said. The same words. The same God.

After supper, his mother sewed by the door. His father smoked his pipe in the yard. The acacias rustled. From somewhere down the street came the sound of someone playing a fiddle, badly.

Gottlieb sat on the doorstep and looked at the sky. The first stars were out — the same stars that shone over Soest and Blacka and Brandenburg and every other place the Isaak family had ever been. He was ten years old. He had never been anywhere but Kulm. He had never seen a forest or a mountain or a city or the sea, though it was only a hundred kilometers to the south. He knew the steppe, the village, the church, the field, and the sky, and he did not yet know that the same restlessness that had driven his great-grandfather from Soest and his grandfather from Poland would, in time, drive his own children across an ocean to a place called Dakota, where the prairie looked almost exactly like this and the wind blew just as hard and the sky was just as big, and where, at last, after six generations of moving, the Isaak family would finally stop.

But that was decades away. Tonight, the wheat was almost ready. The stars were out. The wolf, if it was a wolf, had not come back. And Gottlieb Isaak — ten years old, great-great-grandson of a woman who had married in a black dress in a cold church in Westphalia — was home.

# Chapter Five

## The Contract

---

*By the 1850s, the mother colonies of Bessarabia were full. The original sixty-six hectares that each family had received from the Tsar were being divided among sons and grandsons, and the math was unforgiving — what had sustained one family could not sustain four. Young men who wanted their own land had two choices: leave Bessarabia entirely, or move to a daughter colony and rent from a Russian landowner.*

*Gottlieb Isaak chose the second.*

---

**Kulm, Bessarabia — December 17, 1854**

Gottlieb married Louisa Schulz on a cold Tuesday in the prayer house at Kulm. She was seventeen. He was twenty. The pastor from Tarutino performed the service — the same Lutheran rite, the same vows at the door, the same reading from Genesis — and afterward there was a meal at his mother's house, because his father, Karl, had died the year before at fifty-seven, and the house belonged to the family now, which meant it belonged to too many people at once.

That was the problem, and Gottlieb knew it before the wedding wine was finished. Karl had left the farm to his sons, as every man in Kulm left his farm to his sons, but the sixty-six hectares that had fed one family in 1815 were now expected to feed several. The land could not be stretched. It could not be conjured. It was the same black earth it had always been — deep and rich and generous — but there was simply not enough of it, and every year there were more children and fewer hectares per child, and the quiet arithmetic of inheritance was slowly strangling the colony.

Gottlieb and Louisa stayed in Kulm for five years. He would later describe those years in a single sentence: *troubled and burdened, primarily because of the shortage of land.* He did not elaborate. He did not need to. Every young couple in every mother colony in Bessarabia understood what those words meant — the cramped quarters in a family house, the negotiations with brothers over which strip of field belonged to whom, the knowledge that no matter how hard you worked, you were farming a fraction of what your grandfather had farmed, on the same soil, with the same tools, for less return.

In 1859, they left.

---

**Sophiental, Bessarabia — 1860s**

Sophiental was not Kulm.

Kulm sat in the Bessarabian black earth belt — the richest farmland in southern Russia, soil so dark and deep you could push your arm in to the elbow. Sophiental was a daughter colony on rented land, and the soil was light and sandy, pale as flour compared to the heavy black loam Gottlieb had grown up with. The rent was cheap, which was why they had come. The harvests were poor, which was why the rent was cheap.

Gottlieb understood this trade-off in the way that farmers understand all trade-offs — not as an abstraction, but as a physical fact that announced itself every spring when the wheat came up thin and yellow instead of thick and green, and every autumn when the yield was short, and every winter when you counted what was in the granary and what was owed to the landlord and tried to make the first number cover the second.

Most years, it didn't. *Most of the time, cattle and horses had to be sold to pay for the rent,* he would write later, and the sentence has the flatness of a man who has said the same thing so many times that the anger has gone out of it and only the fact remains. You sold a horse to pay the rent. Then you plowed with fewer horses. Then the harvest was worse because you had plowed with fewer horses. Then you sold another horse.

Louisa bore children. The house filled. The farm did not grow.

And then, because the steppe had not yet finished with Gottlieb Isaak, the village elected him mayor.

---

**Sophiental — 1873**

The crop failed completely in 1873\.

It was not only Sophiental. The entire region — the sandy country south and east of the mother colonies, where the daughter settlements scratched at soil that gave grudgingly in good years and gave nothing at all in bad ones — went dry that summer. The rain did not come. The wheat did not come. The corn withered in the fields. By August it was clear that there would be no harvest, and by September it was clear what that meant: no feed for the animals, no seed for next year's planting, no money for the rent, and nothing to live on.

Gottlieb was thirty-eight years old, the father of a growing family, and the mayor of a village that was about to starve.

The contract with the landowner — the Russian who owned the soil they farmed — had a clause for this. Gottlieb had read it carefully, because he was the kind of man who read contracts carefully. In the event of a crop failure, the clause said, the rent payment would be delayed and rolled into the following years. It was there in writing. It was clear. It was, he believed, the law.

He believed wrong.

The administrators — the Russian officials who managed the estate — came to Sophiental in the autumn and demanded the rent. Gottlieb showed them the contract. They told him to submit intelligence reports on the village's financial condition. He submitted them. They asked again for the money. He pointed again to the clause. The correspondence went back and forth through the fall and into the winter, the letters growing shorter and colder on both sides, until one day a letter arrived that ended the conversation.

The contract, it said, had been sold to the Crown's broker. The rent was due immediately. If the money was not paid, the colony would be cleared away and removed. They had thirty days.

Gottlieb read the letter twice. Then he folded it and put it in his coat and walked out into the yard and stood there for a long time, looking at the flat, failed fields and the empty sky, and thought about what it meant to be told that the paper you had signed and the words you had agreed to and the clause you had trusted meant nothing — that the contract existed not to protect you but, as the court would shortly explain, *so you will know how to behave yourselves.*

---

**Akkerman — Thirty days later**

The justice court in Akkerman was a stone building in a town that smelled like the sea, which was only a few kilometers away. Gottlieb had never been to Akkerman. He had never been to court. He wore his best clothes — the same coat he wore to church, brushed and pressed by Louisa — and he carried the contract in his inside pocket, folded into quarters, the paper already soft from handling.

The judges sat behind a long table. There were three of them. Gottlieb stood.

He knew, before anyone spoke, that it had been arranged. The judges did not look at him the way men look at someone whose case they are weighing. They looked at him the way men look at someone whose case has already been decided, with the particular boredom of officials performing a formality. He had seen that look before — on the faces of the Prussian officials who had processed his grandfather's papers in Poland, on the faces of the Russian recruiters who had promised his family free land in Bessarabia. It was the look of power addressing inconvenience.

"Submit the money or leave," they said, voting as one.

Gottlieb requested that the contract be read aloud. This was his right, and they could not refuse it, and so the clerk read the contract, line by line, in a droning voice, while the judges waited.

When the clerk reached the paragraph about crop failures — the clause that specified, in plain language, that rent payments would be delayed in the event of a failed harvest — Gottlieb stopped him.

"Is this as expressed in the contract?" he asked.

"It has been decided," the judge said. "There is no other way."

"For what purpose is the contract?"

The judge looked at him. It was not an unkind look, exactly — it was worse than unkind. It was the look of a man explaining something to a child who is too slow to understand it on his own.

"So you will know how to behave yourselves," the judge said. "And that settles the matter."

Gottlieb stood in the stone courtroom in Akkerman and felt the words land on him like a physical weight. He thought of things he could say. He thought of the German word *Gerechtigkeit* — justice — and the Russian word for prison, which he also knew. He thought of his grandfather Karl, who had walked two thousand kilometers to build a farm on this steppe because a tsar had made him a promise. He thought of his great-grandfather Johann Michel, who had done the same thing in Poland because a king had made him a different promise. He thought of his great-great-grandparents, Andreas and Anna Elisabeth, who had stood in the doorway of a church in Soest and promised each other something, and who had, against all odds, kept that promise.

The promises of governments were not like the promises of people. Gottlieb knew this now. He had known it, perhaps, since boyhood — since the first time he understood that the colony existed at the pleasure of an empire that could revoke that pleasure at any time, for any reason, or for no reason at all. The contract was not a shield. It was a leash. It told you how to behave.

He did not say what he was thinking, because saying it would have meant prison. *"Wotnigimo kidei jim Ostrok"* — bring him in. He had heard the phrase before. Everyone in the colonies had heard it.

# Chapter Six

## The Crossing

---

*On June 4, 1871, Tsar Alexander II signed a decree that changed everything.*

*For over a century, the German colonists in Russia had lived under a separate peace — exempt from military service, free to govern their own villages, free to worship in their own language, free to educate their children as they saw fit. These were the privileges that had drawn their grandparents and great-grandparents east from Germany, from Poland, from Westphalia. These were the terms of the deal.*

*Alexander II revoked them all.*

*Russia had lost the Crimean War and was rebuilding itself as a modern nation-state. The Tsar emancipated the serfs, reformed the courts, modernized the army — and decided that the German colonies, with their separate laws and foreign language and stubborn refusal to become Russian, were an obstacle to progress. Under the new decree, the colonists lost their self-governance. Their children would be taught in Russian. Their young men would be drafted into the Russian army. The Germans in Russia were to become, simply, Russians.*

*They had ten years to comply.*

*The colonists did not comply. They sent scouts to America.*

*In 1862, Abraham Lincoln had signed the Homestead Act, offering 160 acres of free land to anyone willing to settle and improve it for five years. By the early 1870s, railroad companies were distributing pamphlets across Europe and Russia, advertising the vast, cheap, empty grasslands of the American Great Plains. The Northern Pacific Railroad reached Dakota Territory in 1872\. The delegates came back and said the land looked almost exactly like the Bessarabian steppe — flat, treeless, deep-soiled, and enormous. They said: yes. Let's move.*

*The first German-Russian families arrived in Dakota Territory in 1873\. By 1878, families from Kulm, Bessarabia were homesteading near Parkston in southern Dakota. By 1886, enough of them had settled in the northern part of the territory that they named their new town after the old colony — Kulm, North Dakota. By 1920, more than 300,000 first- and second-generation German-Russians lived in the United States, 70,000 of them in North Dakota alone.*

*Gottlieb Isaak watched the exodus for years. He was not a young man anymore. He had fought the Russian courts and lost. He had borrowed from his neighbors to pay a rent that his contract said he did not owe. He had watched his sons grow into men on sandy soil that could not feed them. He knew — in his bones, in his blood, in the inherited memory of a family that had been moving since 1760 — that it was time again.*

---

**Sophiental, Bessarabia — 1886**

The letter came from Dakota.

It was written in German — the Swabian-inflected German of the Bessarabian colonies, familiar as a hymn — and it was from a man Gottlieb knew, a former neighbor from Kulm who had emigrated two years earlier. The letter described the land. It described the soil — black, the letter said, *black*, like the real Kulm, not the sandy nothing of Sophiental. It described the Homestead Act and what 160 acres meant for a family that had been farming rented sand. It described the town they were building. They had named it Kulm, because what else would you name it.

Gottlieb read the letter twice. Then he handed it to Louisa.

She was forty-nine. He was fifty-two. They had been married for thirty-two years. They had raised their children on soil that betrayed them and under a government that lied to them, and they had survived it. Now here was a letter from the other side of the world saying there was room.

Louisa set the letter on the table and looked at him.

"How?" she said.

---

First, you sell everything. The farm, the animals, the tools, the furniture. You keep the clothes on your backs and the Bible and the hymnal and whatever money the sale produces, which is less than you hoped because everyone in the colony is selling at the same time and the buyers know it. You pay off your debts, because the Russian authorities will not issue an emigration passport to anyone who owes money. This takes weeks.

Then the passport. You go to the district office in Akkerman — the same stone building where Gottlieb had stood before the judges thirteen years earlier — and you fill out the papers and pay the fee and wait. The bureaucracy is slow and deliberate, because the Russian government does not want you to leave. But they cannot stop you. The right to emigrate is in the law, grudgingly, and eventually the passport comes.

Then the train.

By the 1880s, a railway connected Odessa to the European rail network, and from Odessa you could reach Germany in days — a journey that had taken Gottlieb's grandfather four months by wagon. The family boarded a train heading west. They crossed the border out of the Russian Empire — for the last time, though they could not have known that — and passed through Austria-Hungary and into Germany, the country their ancestors had left a hundred years ago, the country they had never stopped calling the Fatherland, though none of them had ever set foot in it.

Germany was not what they expected. It was green. It had trees. The towns were close together and built of brick, and there were factories, and the trains ran on time, and the language on the station signs was the same language they spoke at home but also somehow different — faster, sharper, full of words they didn't know. The Bessarabian Germans were country people, steppe people, a century behind the industrialized nation that Germany had become. They passed through it like visitors from another age.

Bremen. The Norddeutscher Lloyd — North German Lloyd — operated out of Bremerhaven, where passengers were processed, housed, and medically examined before boarding. The steamship company handled everything with German efficiency: you bought a through-ticket that covered the train to the port, the Atlantic crossing, and the railway journey from New York to your final American destination. The ticket was your lifeline. It was the most expensive thing most emigrants had ever purchased, and it represented the entire proceeds of a liquidated life.

The ship was the SS *Mosel*.

She was an iron-hulled steamer, 349 feet long and 3,200 tons, built in Greenock, Scotland, and operated by Norddeutscher Lloyd on the Bremen-Southampton-New York route. She carried ninety first-class passengers, 126 second-class, and six hundred in steerage. The Isaaks were in steerage.

The *Mosel* departed Bremerhaven and steamed down the coast to Southampton, on the southern shore of England, where she took on additional passengers and coal. For Gottlieb, who had never seen the sea until a few days earlier, the English Channel was a revelation — grey-green water stretching to every horizon, dotted with the sails and smokestacks of more ships than he had imagined existed in the world. At Southampton, passengers from London arrived by special train from Waterloo Station — eighty miles in a couple of hours, straight onto the quay, from train to tender to the deck of the steamer.

London. They did not see London, not really — not the way a tourist sees it. But they were close enough to know it was there, the largest city on earth, the capital of an empire that spanned the globe, and the *Mosel* sat in its shadow at Southampton while the English passengers boarded and the coal was loaded and the final cargo was stowed. Some of the Bessarabian families had relatives or contacts in London's German community, and a few may have disembarked briefly to meet them. For most, Southampton was simply the last piece of solid European ground they would ever stand on.

Then the open Atlantic.

Steerage was below the waterline, in compartments fitted with rows of narrow wooden bunks stacked three high. The air was close — a permanent fog of body heat, damp wool, engine grease, and the sweetish stench of seasickness. The engines throbbed through the iron hull day and night, a vibration you felt in your teeth and your spine. When the ship entered the open ocean, the swells began, and the compartment filled with the sounds of retching and prayer, which were sometimes the same thing. Six hundred people in a space designed for efficiency, not comfort, rolling west across two thousand miles of water at thirteen knots.

The crossing took roughly two weeks. Gottlieb had never seen the ocean. He had never seen a body of water larger than the Kogylnik River, which was mostly dry in summer. The Atlantic was incomprehensible — not just in its size but in its indifference, the way it moved without reference to anything human, the way it could be calm one hour and violent the next, the way it went on and on and on without a single landmark, without a tree or a hill or a road, just water and sky and the thin line where they met, endless and empty and terrifying and beautiful.

He was fifty-two years old. He had been born on the steppe. He had never been more than a hundred kilometers from his birthplace until this year. Now he was in the middle of an ocean.

---

New York Harbor. No Statue of Liberty — not yet. Lady Liberty would not be dedicated until later that year, or the year after, depending on when the Isaaks arrived. What they saw instead was the harbor itself: the forest of masts and smokestacks, the ferries and tugs and barges crisscrossing the water, and beyond them the skyline of lower Manhattan — not the skyscrapers of the twentieth century, but a dense wall of brick and stone that rose from the waterline like a cliff, taller and thicker and more crowded than anything Gottlieb had ever imagined a city could be.

Castle Garden. The old circular fort at the southern tip of Manhattan, converted into America's immigrant processing station in 1855, had been receiving newcomers for over thirty years by the time the Isaaks walked through its doors. It was not Ellis Island — that would come later, in 1892\. Castle Garden was smaller, older, more chaotic. Yiddish immigrants had coined the term *Kesselgarden* to describe it — a word meaning any space that was noisy, confusing, and overwhelming — and the name fit.

The Isaaks entered through the main doors into a vast circular hall that echoed with the sound of a thousand simultaneous conversations in a dozen languages. Clerks sat behind long wooden counters, recording names, ages, countries of origin, occupations, final destinations. Interpreters circulated — German, Polish, Russian, Czech, Norwegian, Italian — shouting questions and translating answers. New arrivals sat on damp trunks nursing children or smoking pipes. Agents from labor exchanges worked the crowd, offering jobs. Currency exchangers called out rates that may or may not have been honest. The air smelled like disinfectant and sweat and wood polish and the sea.

They registered. They stated their names, their ages, their country of origin — Russia, they said, because that was what Bessarabia was, officially, though they had never felt Russian and never would. They stated their occupation: farmer. They stated their destination: Dakota Territory. A clerk wrote it down in a ledger. A doctor checked their eyes and their throats and their hands for signs of disease. They were healthy. They were admitted.

The whole process took hours. Then they were out — out of Castle Garden, out onto the streets of New York, into the noise and heat and impossible density of the largest city in the Western Hemisphere. They did not linger. They had no reason to linger. New York was not for them. New York was for people who wanted to disappear into crowds, and the Isaaks had spent their entire lives in crowds of exactly one kind — German Lutheran farmers — and that was the only crowd they were interested in joining.

They found the railway station. They bought their tickets — or rather, they presented the through-tickets they had purchased in Bremen, which covered the entire journey from Bremerhaven to their final stop in Dakota. They boarded a westbound train.

---

The train ride was four days.

Four days across the breadth of a continent so vast it made the Bessarabian steppe look like a garden. The train left New York and plunged into New Jersey, then Pennsylvania — green, rolling country, thick with forests and rivers, a landscape so lush and well-watered that Gottlieb pressed his face to the window and stared. He had never seen this many trees in his life. In Bessarabia, a single tree was an event — something you planted and watered and protected from the wind and pointed out to visitors. Here, trees grew in uncountable millions, covering the hills like fur on an animal's back, and no one seemed to notice them.

The train crossed rivers wider than anything in Bessarabia, spanned by iron bridges that were themselves miracles of engineering. It passed through cities — Pittsburgh, with its furnaces and smokestacks glowing orange against the night sky; Chicago, where the train changed stations and the platform swarmed with more people than lived in the entire Akkerman district. Louisa held the children close in Chicago. The noise was extraordinary. The buildings were extraordinary. Everything was extraordinary, and none of it was for them.

West of Chicago, the country began to change.

The forests thinned. The towns grew smaller and further apart. The hills flattened. The green gave way to gold, and then to the pale, straw-colored blond of prairie grass in autumn. The sky, which had been a ceiling in the eastern states — held down by trees and hills and buildings — opened up, wider and wider, until it was the whole world again, a dome of pale blue pressing down on a landscape so flat and featureless that the horizon was a perfect unbroken line in every direction.

Gottlieb sat up straight. He knew this. He *knew* this landscape. The train was carrying him through country that looked almost exactly like the Budschak — the same treeless plain, the same wind-bent grass, the same enormous sky. Louisa saw it too. She put her hand on his arm and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. They had crossed an ocean and half a continent to arrive at a place that looked like the place they had left.

The train pushed west and north through Minnesota and into Dakota Territory. The stops grew further apart — every thirty, forty, fifty miles, a platform and a water tower and a cluster of wooden buildings that could barely be called a town. At each stop, families disembarked — Germans, Norwegians, Swedes — carrying trunks and bundles, stepping off the train into the grass and the wind and the nothing, met by a man with a wagon or sometimes by no one at all, just the prairie and the sky and the knowledge that somewhere out there, 160 acres of sod were waiting to be broken.

The train reached the end of the line. Yankton, South Dakota.

Gottlieb stepped down onto a wooden platform. The wind hit him immediately — the same wind, the same direction, the same steady, warm, grass-scented pressure that had blown through every day of his childhood in Kulm, Bessarabia. He stood on the platform with Louisa and the children and their trunks, blinking in the afternoon light, and looked out at the Dakota prairie.

Then he saw his brother.

Johann — John, as he called himself now — was standing at the far end of the platform next to a wagon and a pair of horses, squinting against the sun. He had emigrated years earlier, had been one of the first Kulm families to make the crossing, and had written the letters that brought the others west. He was older than Gottlieb remembered — weathered by Dakota winters, browned by Dakota sun — but he had the same Isaak jaw, the same patient eyes, the same unhurried way of moving that their father Karl had carried from Poland to the steppe.

Johann walked over and picked up the heaviest trunk without a word, as if his brother had been gone for an afternoon instead of a lifetime. Then he looked at Gottlieb and said something in the Swabian-accented German of the Bessarabian colonies — something ordinary, something about the road or the weather or the wagon — and the sound of that voice, speaking that language, in this place, broke something open in Gottlieb's chest.

He was home.

Not because he had been here before. He had never been here. No Isaak had ever been here. But the land knew him. The sky knew him. The wind knew him. And his brother was here, speaking German, standing next to a wagon, ready to take him to a place where other families from Kulm and Leipzig and Tarutino had already broken sod and built churches and named their new town after the old colony, because what else would you name it.

They loaded the trunks into the wagon. Louisa climbed up onto the bench. The children settled in the back among the bags. Johann took the reins.

The wagon rolled west, into the prairie, into the grass, into the wide open nothing that looked almost exactly like the Budschak — the same treeless plain, the same enormous sky, the same wind that had known Gottlieb's father and his grandfather and his great-grandfather, all the way back to the flat country of Westphalia where it all began. The Isaak family had spent 126 years looking for a landscape that felt right, and they had finally found it — not in Germany, not in Poland, not in Bessarabia, but here, on the other side of the world, at the end of a wagon road in the middle of a continent that none of their ancestors could have imagined.

Gottlieb sat in the back of the wagon, his legs hanging over the tailgate, watching the prairie unfold behind them like a wake. The wind blew. The grass moved. The sky was enormous.

He had crossed an ocean. He had crossed a continent. And his brother had been waiting for him at the platform with a wagon, the way family does, the way family always has.

# Chapter Seven

## Wide Open Spaces

---

*The Homestead Act of 1862 promised 160 acres of free land to anyone who would settle it, build on it, and farm it for five years. There was one condition the German-Russians had not anticipated: you could not live in a village. The law required each family to reside on its own claim, separated from its neighbors by a quarter mile or more of open prairie. For a people who had lived in tight-knit colonies for a hundred years — who had shared a single street, a single church, a single schoolteacher — the isolation of the American homestead was a kind of shock. But the land was free, and free land was something no Isaak had ever had.*

---

**Near Parkston, Dakota Territory — 1886**

Johann's wife had made Bierocks.

They were sitting around his brother's table — Gottlieb, Louisa, the children, Johann and his family — eating the stuffed bread rolls that every German-Russian woman made slightly differently and every German-Russian man considered a matter of serious importance. Johann's wife filled hers with beef and cabbage and onion, the dough golden and tight, and Gottlieb ate three of them before he said a word, because he had been eating train food and ship food and emigrant-hall food for six weeks and his body recognized this meal the way a man recognizes his own language after months of listening to someone else's.

"The soil," Gottlieb said finally, wiping his mouth. "Tell me about the soil."

Johann grinned. He was older than Gottlieb but looked younger — the Dakota sun had baked him brown and lean, and he moved with the loose confidence of a man who owned his own land and owed nothing on it. "Black," he said. "Like Kulm. The real Kulm."

"Not like Sophiental."

"Nothing is like Sophiental. Sophiental was God's punishment for something we did in Poland."

Gottlieb laughed. It was the first time he had laughed since Bremen, and the sound surprised him. Louisa looked over from the other end of the table, where she was helping Johann's wife clear the dishes, and something in her face softened.

"How deep?" Gottlieb asked.

"Deep enough. You can push a rod in to your elbow and not hit clay. It's never been farmed. It's never been anything. Just grass, since the beginning of the world."

"And the rain?"

"It rains. Not like Bessarabia — you don't pray for it the same way. It comes." Johann leaned back in his chair. "The winters are hard. Harder than the steppe. But the land is good, and it's yours, and nobody comes to your door asking for rent."

That word — *rent* — landed on the table between them like a stone. Johann knew the story of Akkerman. Every Isaak knew the story of Akkerman.

"No rent," Gottlieb said quietly.

"No rent. No landlord. No contract that teaches you how to behave." Johann reached across the table and tore a Bierock in half. "Welcome to America, brother."

---

Gottlieb's son David sat at the far end of the table and listened.

He was twenty-six years old — born in Kulm, Bessarabia, on April 17, 1860, the same year the colony got its first proper church building. He had been christened and confirmed in the Lutheran faith, had grown up on the steppe, and had come to America with the family in 1877, when he was just seventeen. The Isaaks had settled first at Scotland, South Dakota, entering upon agricultural pursuits — farming, as the family had always farmed, as every generation of Isaaks had farmed since Soest.

But David was not entirely like his father. He was quiet — *a quiet and retiring disposition*, his obituary would say — but beneath the quietness was an ambition that the older Isaaks did not quite share. He had watched his father fight the Russian courts and lose. He had watched him farm rented sand and sell cattle to pay the landlord. He had crossed an ocean and broken sod alongside his brothers near Parkston. But David had also noticed something about America that his father, who thought in hectares and wheat yields, had not fully grasped: in this country, you did not have to be a farmer. There were towns. There were stores. There were businesses that did not depend on whether the rain came.

He kept this to himself for now. He was a dutiful son — *a true and devoted servant of the church*, they would say of him later — and when his father needed hands in the field, David's hands were there. But he was watching. He was thinking.

Three years earlier, in 1883, David had married a woman named Hanna Hetzler. She had been born in Missouri in 1861 — an American, technically, though her parents were as German as sauerkraut. They had emigrated from Germany in 1852, when they were barely more than children themselves, sixteen and seventeen years old, crossing the Atlantic a full generation before the Bessarabian exodus. Hanna spoke English without an accent. She understood how things worked in this country in a way that the Bessarabian families, with their colony German and their steppe habits, were still learning. David had married well, and he knew it.

In 1887, Hanna gave birth to their second child — a boy they named Albert Friedrich Isaak. He was born in Dakota Territory, an American from his first breath, and he would carry the Isaak name into the twentieth century and beyond. But that was decades away. For now, he was an infant in a sod house on the prairie, and his grandfather Gottlieb held him the way old farmers hold babies — carefully, with rough hands, as if the child were something that had come up unexpectedly from good soil.

---

The Parkston years were the best years Gottlieb ever had.

He would not have said it that way — Gottlieb was not a man who ranked his years — but the autobiography tells the story in spite of him. They had no money when they arrived, but every member of the family hired out for wages, and American wages were a revelation. *The wages received amounted to more than three times the amount I had to pay in Russia for expenses,* he wrote, and the sentence has the quiet shock of a man who has just discovered that the world is not, in fact, arranged to cheat him.

Everything the children earned came into his hands. He broke twenty-five acres of virgin prairie. He bought a pair of oxen, a cow, swine. They planted a *Baschtam* — cucumbers, pumpkins, and watermelons — and that first summer's garden was *the likes of which we never again had.* The soil had never been farmed, and it gave back everything they put into it with a generosity that bordered on the absurd.

The old debt from Russia — 330 rubles, the money he had borrowed in 1873 to pay the rent the Akkerman court had demanded — he paid it off. The favorable exchange rate between dollars and rubles made it possible. He paid the debt, the interest, built the house, broke the land, bought the animals, and after four years he stood in his own yard and said what he had never been able to say in Bessarabia:

*"Now this is ours."*

*Oh, how lucky each one of us could consider ourselves, in that we were rescued out of servitude.*

In 1880, Louisa gave birth to their youngest son, Emanuel, right here in Parkston. A Dakota baby. An American.

But even in the good years, the old world reached across the ocean. Sometime in 1882 — by letter, weeks or months after the fact, the way news traveled between Bessarabia and Dakota — Gottlieb learned that his mother had died. Anna Elisabeth Schimke, his wife's mother? Or his own mother, back in Kulm? The records are unclear, but the news came late, as it always did, arriving in an envelope that had crossed the same ocean the family had crossed, carrying the kind of information that could not be answered or undone. In 1883, his brother Daniel also died, back in Bessarabia. The old colony was sending him obituaries now instead of letters.

In 1886, Louisa bore a daughter — Wilhelmina. The family was still growing, even as Gottlieb pushed past fifty. The Parkston farm was producing. The debts were paid. For a few years, the world held still.

Then the sons wanted land of their own, and there was none to be had.

---

**The Move North**

Every quarter section around Parkston had been filed on. The colonization had been that fast. The sons would have to go north, to the open country of central Dakota, where the railroad was still pushing into unsurveyed land.

Gottlieb could have stayed. But he was Gottlieb Isaak.

*"Children, if you are going to leave, I too do not wish to remain here."*

He found a buyer and sold the farm. The route north was roundabout — south to Yankton, east to St. Paul, Minnesota, then west again on the Northern Pacific Railroad to Hebron, North Dakota. David and Hanna came along, with little Albert on Hanna's hip and their older children holding tight to her skirts on the platform at every transfer.

Hebron. The hills.

Gottlieb and Louisa stepped off the train and saw, for the first time in either of their lives, a landscape that was not flat. The country around Hebron rolled and buckled into rough, broken hills — buttes and coulees and eroded badlands that looked nothing like anything they knew.

*Then my wife and I first perceived the hills; we were of one mind: "In this place we do not want to die."*

But they had provisions, feed, seed, and a herd of swine, and they were not the kind of people who turned back. They planted potatoes — a huge amount — and broke fifty acres and built a sod house spacious enough for the family. David worked alongside his father and brothers, breaking the same sod, eating the same dust, his quiet ambitions folded away for the time being.

Then they waited for the rain.

The rain did not come.

*It was real dry, no rain. The time arrived for haying, no grass, and what was planted and seeded, did not come up. The potatoes remained in the ground and not a penny was derived as an income. We were fearful that everything, man and beast, would have to die from hunger during the next winter.*

---

**Mercer County, North Dakota**

Solomon and Johann Klein rode sixty miles west from Hebron and found what the family needed: flat, open, grass-covered land along the Knife and Missouri rivers. A few Irish and English families lived there, mostly hunters. A handful of Germans from Hannover had settled nearby. The higher ground was empty.

*This was exactly what we wanted.*

Gottlieb and Klein bought railroad land. The sons homesteaded government claims around them. David filed his own claim — his first land, his own name on the deed.

The first winter was brutal. The sod house overflowed with visitors — other settlers arriving daily, drawn by the same open land. *During every meal two servings had to be made,* Gottlieb wrote. At night, the visitors slept on hay spread across the floor, body to body, while the wind howled and the snow piled against the walls.

*It was a very hard winter, with more snow than we had normally experienced. We were, however, able to console ourselves with the feeling that we had good land, which made us all feel more content.*

Good land. It was always good land. The promise that came from the earth itself, the only promise that had ever been kept.

---

**County Commissioner**

Then came politics, and with politics came trouble of a kind Gottlieb had not expected.

He was elected county commissioner — the only German on a board of five, the other four being English-speaking Americans who had been in the territory longer and who regarded the German-Russian settlers with the particular suspicion that established people reserve for newcomers who work too hard and complain too little.

*Oh, what a time I had.*

The county was badly in debt, despite having almost no population. The bills came in thick and heavy — contracts and requisitions and expenses that Gottlieb could see were inflated, padded, designed to extract money from a treasury that had no money to extract. The four English commissioners voted yes on everything. Gottlieb voted no.

They did not appreciate this.

He was a German farmer with an accent and a Bible and a deep, Bessarabian suspicion of any official who told you to submit the money or leave. He had stood before the Akkerman court and watched judges vote as one. He knew what arranged outcomes looked like. And he said no — *most of the time I said "no,"* he wrote — until finally the flow of inflated bills began to slow, not because the other commissioners agreed with him but because it is exhausting to steal from a treasury when one man at the table keeps asking to see the contract.

*Eventually more Germans became county commissioners and the county was released from the burden of heavy debts, but not during my time. I was only there for six years.*

Six years of being the only German in the room, saying no in a language the others pretended not to understand, fighting the same fight he had fought in Akkerman — the fight of a man who believes that words on paper should mean what they say. He served his time. He did not enjoy it. But he did it, because someone had to, and because the Isaaks had never been the kind of people who let other men handle the money.

David watched his father navigate American politics the way he had watched him navigate Russian courts — with a mixture of admiration and the quiet conviction that there had to be a better way. David's way would not be politics. David's way would be business.

# Chapter Eight

## The Hardware Store

---

*By 1892, Gottlieb Isaak was fifty-eight years old. He had homesteaded near Parkston, broken sod at Hebron, settled in Mercer County, served six years as county commissioner, and survived every crop failure, hard winter, and crooked official that Dakota Territory could throw at him. He was tired. His bones ached in the cold. His hands, which had gripped plow handles and scythe handles and wagon reins since he was old enough to walk, were stiff in the mornings and did not always close the way he told them to.*

*His son David had a proposition.*

---

**Eureka, South Dakota — 1892**

The hardware store was David's idea.

He had been thinking about it for years — since the Parkston days, maybe longer, since the first time he walked into an American general store and understood that the man behind the counter worked indoors, year-round, and did not depend on the rain. David was thirty-two, married to Hanna, father to a growing family that included five-year-old Albert Friedrich and his older siblings. He had farmed his homestead in Mercer County and done well enough, but farming was his father's life, not his. David wanted walls. He wanted a building with his name on it.

The proposition came through a man named Mewing, who was already in the hardware business in Eureka. The deal was a partnership: Gottlieb, David, and Mewing would run the store together, pooling their capital and their labor. Gottlieb had misgivings. He said so. *I had misgivings, wondering if the wife and I had the proper determination for this.*

He put the question to Solomon and Klein — his son and son-in-law, the men he trusted most after Louisa. Their answer surprised him.

*"Do you think we want to remain here? At the first opportunity that comes along, we are going to leave."*

Even his own children were ready to move on from Mercer County. The land was good, but the winters were savage and the isolation was real — sixty miles from the nearest proper town, surrounded by empty prairie and a handful of English-speaking hunters who had no interest in building the kind of community the German-Russians needed. Eureka, in McPherson County, was different. It was a growing town with a railroad connection, German-speaking settlers pouring in, businesses opening, churches organizing. It was the kind of place where a man could make a living without betting everything on the weather.

Gottlieb took the deal. After six years in Mercer County, the Isaak family moved to Eureka in the fall of 1892\. That same fall, the Adam Sailer family — Gottlieb's son-in-law — followed. In 1893, Solomon, Klein, and Netzer came too. The whole clan, reassembling in a new town, the way they had reassembled in every new place since Soest.

---

The hardware store stood on the main street of Eureka, and for a few years it was the center of the Isaak family's life.

David ran the day-to-day operations. He had a knack for it — the quiet disposition that made customers trust him, the head for numbers that farming had never required, the ability to stand behind a counter for ten hours and treat every man who walked through the door as if his purchase of a box of nails were the most important transaction of the day. Hanna helped with the books. Gottlieb handled what he could, though he was a farmer in a shopkeeper's apron and never entirely comfortable with the arrangement.

The store sold hardware, tools, implements — everything a homesteading family needed to build a house, break sod, fence a claim, and repair a wagon. The shelves were stacked with nails, hinges, rope, chain, plowshares, axe heads, saw blades, stove parts, and the hundred small iron necessities that held a prairie life together. It smelled like metal and oil and sawdust, and the bell above the door rang all day long.

The trouble was credit.

The settlers who walked through that door were, almost without exception, desperately poor. They were German-Russians and Scandinavians and Americans, all of them homesteading on the same empty prairie, all of them one bad harvest from ruin. They needed tools. They needed supplies. They did not have cash.

*Each time they purchased something, it would be said, "Of course, you will have to write this up on a charge."*

Gottlieb knew this voice. He had been this voice — the voice of a man who needs something he cannot pay for, who promises to settle up after the harvest, who means it sincerely and cannot always keep the promise. He had bought seed on credit in Sophiental. He had borrowed 330 rubles from his neighbors in 1873\. He understood the arithmetic of need.

But understanding it did not make it profitable.

*After three or four years, we usually received the information that this or that customer had moved away from the area. Because of this, many of the notes we had received came up with no value.*

They moved away. That was the cruelest part — not that the settlers couldn't pay, but that they left, vanishing into the vast American interior the way people vanished, heading further west or further north, chasing the next piece of open land, and the debts they left behind were as worthless as a Russian requisition receipt stamped with Napoleon's eagle. The pattern was so familiar it must have made Gottlieb sick with recognition.

The losses mounted. The partnership strained. There was not enough work and not enough income for all of them. Sailer left for Mannhaven, North Dakota, where he built a good business of his own. Solomon went into business with a man named Raab — Eureka Implements and Feed. The partnership dissolved, and when the accounts were settled, Gottlieb found himself on the losing end.

*Neither of the two, nor I could demand the return of our investment. The two did however receive a few notes receivable and a small portion of their partnership share, but I had the loss of even that, and left the business empty handed.*

David kept the store. He would manage it alone until his death in 1916 — *a quiet and retiring disposition,* his obituary would say, *but despite his lack of forwardness was at all times a true and devoted servant.* The hardware store on the main street of Eureka was David's life's work, the thing he had been building toward since he first walked into an American store and saw a different kind of future. It never made him rich. But it was his, and it was indoors, and it did not depend on the rain.

---

**The Eureka Lutheran Church**

When David arrived in Eureka, there was no Lutheran congregation. For a family that had carried Luther's Small Catechism from Soest to Brandenburg to Poland to Bessarabia to Dakota — a family for whom the church was not just a place of worship but the organizing principle of community, the keeper of records, the school, the court of first resort, the place where you married and christened your children and buried your dead — the absence of a congregation was not a minor inconvenience. It was a crisis.

David helped fix it. He was one of the founders of the Evangelical Lutheran Zion Church in Eureka — not the pastor, not the loudest voice, but one of the men who signed the charter and raised the money and built the building and showed up every Sunday, year after year, until the church was as much a part of Eureka as the railroad depot or the grain elevator. He attended services regularly and was unfailing in his attendance at the Lord's Supper. The faith that had been carried across an ocean in a hymnal and a Bible had found, at last, a permanent home.

Little Albert Friedrich — seven years old when the family moved to Eureka, growing up behind the counter of the hardware store and in the pews of the Lutheran church — absorbed all of it. The work ethic. The faith. The quiet ambition. The understanding that you build things not for yourself but for the people who come after you. He was an Isaak, and he was watching, the way David had watched Gottlieb, the way Gottlieb had watched Karl, all the way back.

---

**A Peaceful Life**

Gottlieb left the hardware store with nothing except the house. He was in his sixties now, too old for the counter, too old for the field, and for the first time in his life he had no occupation and no land and no plan.

He bought a smaller lot and a smaller house, on which he paid interest. And then something happened that had never happened to Gottlieb Isaak before: he became a landlord.

The larger house — the one he had bought when the family first moved to Eureka — he rented out. The income was steady. It was modest. And it was more reliable than anything he had ever earned from farming or shopkeeping.

*We had, thank God, a more tranquil life than we had while in business. Too, from the larger house we had a steady rent income, which took better care of our needs than did the business.*

A more tranquil life. The sentence is almost startling in the context of Gottlieb's autobiography, which up to this point has been a catalog of crop failures, court battles, forced moves, and dissolved partnerships. Tranquility was not something the Isaak family specialized in. But here, finally, in a small house in Eureka, South Dakota, with rent checks arriving on schedule and no inventory to manage and no judges to face and no rain to pray for, Gottlieb and Louisa found it.

They also received help from the Bruntschs and the Sailers — their sons-in-law, their daughters' husbands, the extended family that had followed them from Mercer County to Eureka and that now encircled them like a fence around a garden. The children took care of the parents. This was the way of it. This was what the family was for.

---

**The Golden Wedding — December 17, 1904**

Fifty years.

Gottlieb and Louisa had been married for fifty years. They had exchanged vows in the prayer house at Kulm, Bessarabia, on December 17, 1854, when she was seventeen and he was twenty and the steppe wind blew through the cracks in the walls. They had farmed rented sand at Sophiental and fought the Akkerman court and crossed an ocean and broken sod in Dakota and survived every winter and every drought and every broken promise the world had offered, and they were still here. Still married. Still together. Still alive.

The Golden Wedding Celebration was held at the Evangelical Lutheran Zion Church — David's church, the congregation he had helped to found. It was the first fiftieth anniversary celebration ever held in Eureka, and the entire town took part. The pastor, Reverend H. Reinhardt, presided. The couple was presented with a new Bible.

A new Bible. Think about that for a moment. The Isaak family had been carrying Bibles across Europe and across oceans for 144 years — from the *Alde Kerke* in Soest, through Poland, through Bessarabia, across the Atlantic to Dakota. The Bibles wore out. The pages thinned and tore. The bindings cracked. But the words did not change, and the family did not stop reading them, and now, at the end of a long life in a small town in South Dakota, the church placed a new Bible in Gottlieb's hands, as if to say: the story continues. The book is new. The words are the same.

Gottlieb was seventy. Louisa was sixty-seven. They sat in the pew together, the way they had sat in the prayer house in Kulm, the way they had sat on the wagon bench crossing Europe, the way they had sat at every table in every house they had ever built. The congregation sang. The hymns were the same hymns — *Ein feste Burg*, *Nun danket alle Gott* — the same words in the same German, reaching all the way back to the schoolhouse in Kulm where a ten-year-old boy had discovered that singing was the best part of being alive.

---

**May 1, 1905**

Louisa had been in pain for years.

Gottlieb did not describe the illness in his autobiography — only that it had caused her suffering for a long time, and that it had finally become almost intolerable, and that she was confined to her bed. The woman who had walked beside wagons across the Bessarabian steppe, who had stood on the deck of the SS *Mosel* and watched the Atlantic roll beneath her, who had broken sod and planted *Baschtam* and raised children on three continents, was dying in a bed in Eureka, South Dakota, and there was nothing anyone could do.

*In 1905, our loving God released her from her suffering on the first of May.*

She was sixty-eight years old. She had been married to Gottlieb for fifty years and four months. She had borne him children in Bessarabia, in South Dakota, and in the years between. She had followed him to Sophiental and to Parkston and to Hebron and to Mercer County and to Eureka without — so far as the record shows — ever once refusing to go. She had eaten Bierocks at Johann's table and slept on hay on the floor of a sod house and stood beside her husband in the Akkerman court and ridden a train across a continent and watched the Dakota prairie appear outside the window like a memory of the steppe.

Gottlieb buried her in Eureka. He sold the small house. He paid the debts.

And then, at the age of seventy, alone for the first time since he was twenty years old, Gottlieb Isaak began what he called, with the dry humor of a man who had moved too many times already, *the beloved journeys.*

*Every one of the children wanted me to live with them.*

He moved from child to child — Solomon, David, Sailer, Bruntsch, Emanuel — staying for a while in each home, a guest in the houses his children had built, loved and welcomed and quietly miserable, because Gottlieb Isaak had spent his entire life building things, and living in someone else's house was not something he knew how to do.

He would live another sixteen years, dying on January 20, 1921, at the age of eighty-six. But the autobiography — the document he had been writing since the night after the Akkerman court, the record he kept for his children and their children and theirs — effectively ends with Louisa's death. The rest is coda. The rest is an old man in a rocking chair, talking to God at midnight, asking to be relieved of his pain and finding, to his astonishment, that the prayer was answered.

The story of Gottlieb and Louisa Isaak is the story of the deal that every immigrant makes with the future: you give up everything you know in exchange for everything you don't. They made that deal in 1886, when they sold the farm in Sophiental and boarded a train heading west. They kept making it — at every train station, at every platform, at every new piece of land — for the rest of their lives. And the autobiography that Gottlieb left behind is the receipt. It is the proof that the deal was made, and the record of what it cost, and the testimony of a man who believed, against all evidence, that the next place would be better than the last.

Six generations later, his great-great-great-grandson is writing a book about it. The Bible the church gave him in 1904 is gone. The hardware store is gone. The sod house is gone. But the words are still here. They have always been here. And they are, as Gottlieb would have insisted, the truth.

# Chapter Eleven

## The Corporal

---

*By the turn of the century, the German-Russian communities of McPherson County, South Dakota, had become a world unto themselves. Eureka — originally platted as St. Petersburg, before someone thought the Greek word for "I have found it" sounded more American — was the commercial heart of this world. Its grain elevators handled millions of bushels of wheat each year, making it one of the greatest primary wheat markets in the country. Its streets were lined with general stores, saloons, and churches. Nearly seventy percent of the county's population was German-speaking.*

*But the wider world was watching.*

*Since 1914, a war had been consuming Europe. Germany, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire were locked in a death struggle against Britain, France, Russia, and a growing list of allies. The United States had stayed out of it. President Woodrow Wilson won reelection in 1916 on the slogan "He Kept Us Out of War." Most Americans — especially those with family in Germany — wanted it to stay that way.*

*Then, in the early months of 1917, three things happened in rapid succession. Germany announced it would resume unrestricted submarine warfare in the Atlantic, sinking any ship — military, merchant, or passenger — that approached British waters. German U-boats began torpedoing American cargo vessels, killing American sailors. And British intelligence intercepted and decoded a secret telegram from German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann to the German ambassador in Mexico, proposing a military alliance: if the United States entered the war, Germany would help Mexico reclaim Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. When Zimmermann publicly confirmed the telegram was genuine, American outrage became impossible to contain.*

*On April 2, 1917, President Wilson stood before Congress and declared that "the world must be made safe for democracy." Four days later, Congress voted for war. On April 6, the United States officially entered the fight against Germany.*

*For the German-Russian families of the northern plains, the declaration hit like a blizzard in June. Overnight, the language they spoke at home, the hymns they sang in church, the names they had carried across an ocean and a century became marks of suspicion. They were Americans — born here, raised here, buried here — but America was no longer sure about them.*

---

On a Sunday morning in the spring of 1905, the Isaak family walked to church as they always did — David and Hanna in front, Josephine and Albert and Edward trailing behind in their good clothes. The service was in German. The sermon was in German. The hymns were in German, sung from hymnals printed in the old Gothic script that Albert's generation could still read but that their children's children would not recognize. After the service, the men stood in the churchyard and talked — about wheat prices, about the weather, about a new piece of equipment at the hardware store — in German. The women talked too, about the Kuchen they were baking for the church supper, about the baby that Frau Keller was expecting, about the letter that had come from a cousin still in Russia — in German.

Albert was eighteen that spring — five feet eleven, brown-haired, blue-eyed, with the broad shoulders and quiet bearing of a young man who had spent his life hauling grain sacks and stocking hardware shelves. He had just finished his first year of high school. It would be his last. In rural South Dakota at the turn of the century, this was not unusual — most farm children finished eighth grade and went to work, and the fact that Albert had completed a year of high school put him ahead of most of his neighbors. There was no shame in it. Boys were needed in the fields and in the family business, and the education that mattered most — how to run a store, how to read a contract, how to judge the quality of a plow blade — was learned at the counter, not in a classroom.

He understood English perfectly — he had been educated in American schools, could read American newspapers, could do arithmetic in English and argue in English and tell jokes in English. But when he dreamed, the dreams were in German. When he stubbed his toe, the curse that came out was in German. When he thought about his grandfather Gottlieb — the old man who had crossed an ocean at forty-three and built a life from nothing — the thoughts arrived in German, because Gottlieb had never spoken anything else. German was not a second language for Albert Friedrich Isaak. It was the first language, the underneath language, the one that lived in his body.

Nobody in Eureka would have thought twice about this. In McPherson County, where seven in ten people spoke German, it was the air.

---

Twelve years later, the air turned poisonous.

The anti-German hysteria that swept the United States in 1917 and 1918 was swift, organized, and often vicious. Sauerkraut was renamed "liberty cabbage." Hamburgers became "liberty steaks." Libraries pulled German books from their shelves. Orchestras struck Beethoven and Bach from their programs. The Red Cross barred anyone with a German last name from joining. German-Americans changed their names — Schmidt to Smith, Müller to Miller — hoping to disappear into the Anglo-Saxon mainstream.

In South Dakota, the state Council of Defense went further. In 1918, it issued a decree banning the use of the German language over the telephone and in any public gathering of three or more persons — including church services. The order meant, effectively, that elderly immigrants who spoke little English could no longer pray in the only language they knew.

The violence, when it came, was real. In Collinsville, Illinois, a German-born man named Robert Prager was dragged from a jailhouse basement by a mob and lynched. His crime was having a German accent. In Luverne, Minnesota — barely across the South Dakota border — a German-American farmer named John Meints was kidnapped, whipped, tarred, and feathered, then driven to the state line and told that if he returned, he would be hanged.

Albert Isaak was thirty years old. He worked in the family hardware store. He attended the Lutheran church his father had helped to found. He spoke English — but his English carried the weight of his mother tongue. The vowels were broader than they should have been, the consonants harder. When he said *water*, the *w* sounded like a *v*. In Eureka, where seven in ten people spoke the same way, nobody noticed.

Outside of Eureka, it was different.

---

He had gone to Leola for hardware supplies — a trip he'd made a dozen times before the war. The store owner knew him, or had known him. This time the man behind the counter looked at him the way you look at something you've stepped in.

"Isaak. That's a German name."

"It is," Albert said.

"You people still talking German up there in Eureka?"

Albert set the box of nails on the counter. "We talk English. And German."

The man did not pick up the box. "My son's over in France right now. Fighting Germans. You understand that? Fighting *your* people."

Albert felt the heat climb up the back of his neck. He wanted to say: *My people came from Russia. Before that, from Poland. Before that, from Brandenburg. My grandfather walked across a continent to get away from wars like this.* He wanted to say: *We are not those Germans. We have never been those Germans.* But he knew that the distinction between a German from Hamburg and a German from Kulm, Bessarabia, was invisible to the man behind the counter. To him, German was German. The accent was the accent. The name was the name.

"My people are from South Dakota," Albert said.

The man pushed the box of nails back across the counter. "I think you'd better buy your supplies somewhere else."

Albert picked up the box. He set it back on the shelf himself, because he was not the kind of man who left a mess for someone else to clean up. He walked out. He drove the wagon home in silence.

---

On May 18, 1917, six weeks after the declaration of war, Congress passed the Selective Service Act. All men between twenty-one and thirty were required to register for the draft. Albert, at thirty, was right at the upper edge of the window. He had no choice. Registration Day was June 5, 1917\. Every eligible man in McPherson County walked to the local board and put his name on the list.

On September 21, 1917, Albert Friedrich Isaak entered the United States Army. Service number 2193197\.

No Isaak, in any generation anyone could remember, had ever served in a military. This was the whole point of the family's history. Johann Michel had left Brandenburg during the Seven Years' War. His sons had watched Napoleon's Grande Armée march through Poland and chosen not to follow it. Gottlieb's generation had left Russia in 1878 precisely to avoid conscription — the Tsar had revoked the military exemption that had been the centerpiece of Catherine the Great's original invitation. The Isaaks were not fighters. They were leavers. When a war came, they packed their belongings and moved.

But Albert could not leave. He was an American. This was the last country. There was nowhere further west to go. And the draft was the draft. The Selective Service Act did not allow substitutes, did not allow you to buy your way out, did not care whether your grandfather had crossed an ocean to avoid exactly this. If your number came up, you went.

---

Albert was sent to Camp Funston, at Fort Riley, Kansas, where the 89th Division — known as the "Middle West Division" — was being assembled from National Army drafts out of Kansas, Missouri, South Dakota, and Colorado. He was assigned to Company A, 340th Machine Gun Battalion. The 340th was the division's machine gun unit — the men who operated the Hotchkiss guns, the heavy automatic weapons that could shred a line of advancing infantry or pin down an enemy position for hours. The guns weighed over fifty pounds each, not counting the tripod, the ammunition boxes, the water cans. A machine gun crew did not charge across no-man's-land. They hauled their weapon into position, set it up, and held the line while other men moved forward. It was brutal, mechanical, essential work.

Albert trained through the winter and spring. In late May 1918, the 89th Division moved to Camp Mills, on Long Island — the staging ground for troops shipping out to France. From the Dakotas to Kansas to New York: each train took him further from everything he knew, further into a country that was enormous and loud and nothing like the prairie. The farmland of the Midwest gave way to factory towns, and the factory towns gave way to cities, and then the cities stacked up against one another until they became something Albert had never seen — the eastern seaboard, dense and vertical and roaring.

On June 4, 1918, Corporal Albert Isaak boarded the SS *Khyber* in New York Harbor. The *Khyber* was a British transport — part of a convoy of thirteen ships carrying roughly thirty thousand troops to the war in France. Albert stood on the deck as the ship pulled away from the pier, and he watched the harbor open before him — the tugs, the ferries, the long waterfront of Manhattan, the wharves and cranes and rooftops of a city he had barely imagined.

And then he saw her. The Statue of Liberty, standing on her island in the harbor, the torch held high above the water.

His father and grandfather had not seen her. When Gottlieb and David passed through Castle Garden in 1878 — the old fort at the tip of Manhattan where immigrants were processed before Ellis Island existed — the Statue of Liberty had not yet been erected. She would not be dedicated until 1886\. But Albert saw her now — the copper woman who represented everything this country promised and everything it sometimes failed to deliver — growing smaller as the *Khyber* headed for the open Atlantic.

The irony was not lost on him. His family had come *from* Europe, fleeing war. Now he was going *back* to Europe, to fight one.

The convoy sailed a zigzag course to avoid German submarines. Somewhere off the Irish coast, another British ship in the convoy, the SS *Justicia*, was attacked and sunk by a U-boat — just fifty miles ahead of the *Khyber*. After thirteen days at sea, the convoy docked at Liverpool. From there it was trains to Southampton, a Channel crossing, and then France.

---

While Albert was training at Camp Funston, his father died.

David Isaak passed away on the morning of Friday, December 21, 1917 — three months after Albert's enlistment. He was fifty-seven. His heart had been failing for years, ever since a severe illness around 1912 from which he never fully recovered. His doctor had warned the family that death could come suddenly, at any time.

It came on a Friday morning, two days before Christmas. Death found David, as the pastor would say at the funeral, "in midst of duty."

The funeral was held on Sunday, December 23rd, at the Lutheran church David had helped to found. Reverend Seehase spoke. The congregation filled the pews — the same German-Russian families who had bought nails at the hardware store, who had worshipped beside David for decades, who had known him as a quiet man and a faithful one. Hanna sat in the front row with Josephine and Edward.

Albert's chair was empty. He was in Kansas, learning to operate a machine gun. Whether he received the news by telegram or by letter, whether he was given leave to grieve or simply told to keep drilling — we do not know. We know only that the funeral was held without him.

---

The 89th Division arrived in France in June and July of 1918\. They trained under French supervision in the Lucey Sector in Lorraine — learning the landscape of a war that had been grinding on for four years without them. Then came the fighting.

On the night of September 11, 1918, the word came down: they were going in. Saint-Mihiel. A German salient that had bulged into Allied lines since 1914 — four years of trenches and wire and fortified positions that no one had been able to crack. Now the Americans were going to try.

Albert's crew set up before dawn — the Hotchkiss gun, the tripod, the ammunition boxes, all of it hauled forward through a communications trench that smelled of wet earth and cordite. The gun weighed over fifty pounds without the tripod. The tripod weighed another thirty. The ammunition cans were forty pounds each, and you needed a lot of them. A machine gun crew did not charge across the field. They dug in, they set up, and they held the line while the riflemen moved.

"Isaak, you good?"

"Good."

"Keep your head down when the barrage starts."

The barrage started at one in the morning. The ground shook. The sky turned white. And then the infantry went forward into the rain and the mud, and Albert's crew laid down covering fire — the Hotchkiss chattering in short bursts, the brass casings piling up around his boots, the barrel so hot you could smell the oil burning off. The salient was cleared in two days.

Two weeks later, the 89th Division was redeployed to the Meuse-Argonne.

It was worse. The Argonne Forest was a tangle of ravines, ridges, and fortified hills, and the Germans defended every inch of it. The fighting lasted forty-seven days — from September 26 until the armistice on November 11\. Machine gunners were high-value targets. The enemy knew that if they knocked out the guns, the infantry was exposed. Albert's crew set up in shell craters and ruined farmhouses, broke down the gun and moved it forward when the line advanced, set it up again, laid down fire, broke it down, moved again. By the end, the 89th Division had lost over a thousand men killed and more than seven thousand wounded.

Albert survived. He was not wounded. He did not talk about it afterward — not to Eva, not to his daughters, not to anyone. Whatever he saw in the Argonne, he buried it in the same place he buried everything else: deep, and quiet, and alone.

---

The armistice came on November 11, 1918\. The guns stopped. But the Army did not send its men home. There were two million American soldiers in France, and not nearly enough ships.

Albert was assigned to the Saint Aignon Casual Company No. 416, Regional Section No. 7\. Saint-Aignan-sur-Cher was a small town in the Loire Valley where the Army had established a massive replacement depot — a processing center where soldiers waiting to go home were gathered into temporary units and assigned to ships. On January 7, 1919, Albert boarded the USS *Pueblo* at the port of Brest. The *Pueblo* was an armored cruiser — 504 feet long, originally christened the USS *Colorado* — now repurposed as a troop transport. She would carry more than ten thousand men home from France before she was finished.

The crossing took thirteen days. The North Atlantic in January was brutal — gray seas, freezing wind, the ship rolling in the swells. The soldiers were packed below decks in bunks stacked three and four high.

On the morning of January 20, 1919, the *Pueblo* entered New York Harbor.

The men came up on deck. They crowded the railings in their olive-drab overcoats, and there she was again — the Statue of Liberty, standing on her island, the torch held high. Albert had seen her from the other side, going out. He had watched her shrink behind the ship as the *Khyber* headed for the Atlantic. Now he saw her from the front, growing larger. When his father and grandfather had passed through Castle Garden in 1878, she hadn't existed yet. She was younger than they were.

Every soldier coming home through Hoboken saw her. Some cheered. Some wept. Albert, who was not the cheering kind, stood at the railing and looked.

The *Pueblo* eased into the dock at Hoboken, New Jersey. Corporal Isaak walked down the gangplank.

He was thirty-one years old. He was home.

---

Albert returned to Eureka, to the hardware store, to the church. He came home to a community that was learning, painfully, that the price of belonging in America was the surrender of the most visible marker of difference: the language. Before 1917, German had been spoken freely in the streets of Eureka, in the stores, from the pulpit. After 1918, it retreated indoors — a kitchen language, whispered between parents, fading with each generation. The German-language newspapers closed. The churches switched to English. A world that had been carried intact across an ocean and a century was dismantled in the space of two years.

Albert made his decision. He would not pass the language on.

But first, he had other business.

---

She was standing in the vestibule of the Evangelical Lutheran church in Edmunds County, holding a hymnal she did not need because she already knew the words.

Eva Roesch was nineteen years old, and she was beautiful in a way that had nothing to do with fashion or ornament. She was small-framed and dark-haired, with clear eyes and an open, guileless face — the kind of face that made you trust her before she said a word. There was a stillness about her, a composure that made her seem older than she was, as though she had been born knowing things that other people had to learn. She moved through the world without apology and without pretense. The old women in the congregation watched her and nodded to each other. *That one*, they said. *That one will make a good wife.*

Her family's story was, in its broad strokes, identical to Albert's. Her parents had been born in South Russia — the same Black Sea German world of wheat fields and Lutheran steeples and Kuchen baked in big ovens. They had immigrated in 1898, settling near Roscoe, in Edmunds County. The Roesch family was well known in the German-Russian communities. Their letters from relatives still in Russia, their German-language books, their photographs from the old country would one day be donated to an archive — a small monument to a world that no longer exists.

Albert had come to this particular church for a particular reason. Someone — a sister, an aunt, a well-meaning neighbor — had told him about the Roesch girl. This was how it worked in the German-Russian communities. You did not meet a girl at a dance hall. You met her at church, or someone simply told you: *There is a girl in Edmunds County. Her people are from Russia. She is a good Lutheran. You should go.*

He went.

She noticed him because he was older than the other unmarried men — thirty-two to their early twenties — and because he was quiet in a way that suggested he had earned the right to be quiet. She noticed his hands, which were the hands of a man who worked with tools. She noticed that he sang the hymns without looking at the book.

After the service, they spoke. He told her he was from Eureka. She said she knew. He told her about the hardware store. She said her father had bought nails there. He did not tell her about France. He did not tell her about the Meuse-Argonne or the machine guns or the man in Leola who would not sell him supplies. He told her about the store, and the church, and the prairie in the spring when the wheat came up green, and she listened — and something in her listening told him that she already understood the parts he was leaving out.

On November 11, 1919 — exactly one year after the armistice — Albert Friedrich Isaak married Eva Roesch. Whether he chose this date deliberately, as a private commemoration, or whether it was simply the date that worked, we cannot know. But a man who had served in France and come home to a country that had tried to erase his heritage might have found something fitting in beginning his married life on the anniversary of the day the war ended.

---

Albert and Eva settled in Eureka. The 1920 census records them there, in McPherson County, beginning their life together.

On January 20, 1921 — exactly two years after Albert's ship had docked in Hoboken — Gottlieb Isaak died in Tripp, South Dakota, at the age of eighty-six. The old man who had written his autobiography in German, who had crossed an ocean and broken the prairie and prayed away his rheumatism at midnight — was gone. He was the last who remembered Kulm, the last who could tell you what the steppe looked like in October and how the wheat grew in Bessarabia so high it reached a man's chest.

Four months later, on May 8, 1921, Albert and Eva's first daughter was born. They named her Alice Estelle Isaak.

Six years after that, on July 2, 1927, in Aberdeen, South Dakota, their second daughter arrived. They named her Edith Allegra Isaak.

---

The names were not accidental.

Albert Friedrich Isaak — the man who had been mocked for his accent and questioned for his loyalty, who had hauled a machine gun through the Argonne Forest and come home to a country that had banned his mother tongue — had named his daughters after a poem.

It was one of the most beloved poems in the English language, memorized by generations of American schoolchildren, taught in third through sixth grade across the country. It was, in every sense, an *American* poem — written by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, the poet of *Paul Revere's Ride* and *The Song of Hiawatha*. Albert would have encountered it in school — every American child did — and he would have remembered the names. The poem was called "The Children's Hour":

 

Between the dark and the daylight,  When the night is beginning to lower, Comes a pause in the day's occupations,  That is known as the Children's Hour.

I hear in the chamber above me  The patter of little feet, The sound of a door that is opened,  And voices soft and sweet.

From my study I see in the lamplight,  Descending the broad hall stair, Grave Alice, and laughing Allegra,  And Edith with golden hair.

A whisper, and then a silence:  Yet I know by their merry eyes They are plotting and planning together  To take me by surprise.

A sudden rush from the stairway,  A sudden raid from the hall\! By three doors left unguarded  They enter my castle wall\!

They climb up into my turret  O'er the arms and back of my chair; If I try to escape, they surround me;  They seem to be everywhere.

They almost devour me with kisses,  Their arms about me entwine, Till I think of the Bishop of Bingen  In his Mouse-Tower on the Rhine\!

Do you think, O blue-eyed banditti,  Because you have scaled the wall, Such an old mustache as I am  Is not a match for you all\!

I have you fast in my fortress,  And will not let you depart, But put you down into the dungeon  In the round-tower of my heart.

And there will I keep you forever,  Yes, forever and a day, Till the walls shall crumble to ruin,  And moulder in dust away\!

 

*Grave Alice, and laughing Allegra, and Edith with golden hair.*

Albert's first-born was Alice. His second-born was Edith Allegra — both of Longfellow's other daughters compressed into a single name, as though he could not bear to leave either one out.

He did not teach Alice or Edith Allegra to speak German. He chose not to pass on the language of his parents and grandparents, the language that Gottlieb had carried from Kulm and that Johann Michel had spoken in Brandenburg before him. He broke the chain so that his daughters would never hear what he had heard — so that no one would ever push a box of nails back across a counter at them and say, *I think you'd better buy your supplies somewhere else.* When it came time to name them, he reached not for a Louisa or a Magdalena from the family rolls of Kulm, but for the most American poem he knew.

---

In Eureka, Albert and Eva built their life. They ran Isaak's Variety Store on one of Eureka's modest commercial blocks — the kind of general merchandise shop that small prairie towns depended on: thread and buttons, kitchen utensils, penny candy, school supplies, household goods arranged on wooden shelves and in glass cases. Albert and Eva worked it together, side by side, the way German-Russian couples had always worked — as partners, as equals, as a single unit. They hired a local girl named Rosina Lindemann, born in 1912, to help behind the counter.

The store was their livelihood and their daily rhythm. Albert handled the ordering, the inventory, the heavy lifting. Eva handled the customers, the displays, the gentle art of knowing what a woman needed before she asked. Between them, they kept the doors open through the lean years of the late 1920s and into the grinding poverty of the Depression, when wheat prices collapsed and the prairie dried up and half the storefronts in Eureka went dark.

---

But on a summer evening in 1927, in the small house in South Dakota, with Eva in the kitchen and Alice playing on the floor and the new baby sleeping in her crib, Albert Friedrich Isaak could sit for a moment in his chair and feel something he had not felt in a long time.

The war was over. His father was at rest. His grandfather was at rest. The old country, with its steppe and its Tsars and its long, slow dismantling of German hopes, was behind them — behind *all* of them, now, forever. He had a wife who was good and beautiful and strong. He had two daughters — grave Alice, and laughing Allegra, Edith with golden hair. He had a store and a church and a community that knew his name.

The Isaaks had been running for two hundred years — from Brandenburg to Poland, from Poland to Bessarabia, from Bessarabia to Dakota Territory — always fleeing something, always looking for a place that would let them stay.

Albert was the first Isaak in six generations who was not running from anything.

He was home. And the house was full.
