## Chapter One

# The Alde Kerke

Soest, Westphalia — November 29, 1760

---

In the autumn of 1760, the world was burning.

It had been burning for four years. Some called it the Seven Years' War, though no one knew yet how many years it would last or how many millions it would consume. On three continents, the great powers of Europe were tearing each other apart — and tearing apart everyone who had the misfortune to live between their armies. In North America, the British had just seized Canada from the French, and the fleur-de-lis was coming down over Quebec and Montreal for the last time. In India, the armies of rival trading companies fought pitched battles in the Tamil dust while empires older than Europe watched and waited to see which set of foreigners would swallow them whole. At sea, the Royal Navy hunted the remnants of the French fleet from the African coast to the Caribbean, and Spain — the great sleeping empire — was stirring, preparing to throw its weight into the war on France's side, which meant it would only get worse.

But nowhere was the killing worse than in Europe itself. The continent had become a charnel house. Frederick of Prussia — Frederick the Great, though greatness was a hard word to use in 1760 — was fighting for the survival of his kingdom against half the world: France, Austria, Russia, Sweden, and a dozen German states who smelled blood. His capital, Berlin, had been sacked by Russian and Austrian troops in October. His treasury was empty. His currency was worthless. His army was filling its broken ranks with teenagers and prisoners of war. Three weeks before this story begins, he had thrown forty-five thousand men against the Austrian lines at Torgau and won the field — but at the cost of seventeen thousand dead and wounded, a victory so ruinous that Frederick himself, knocked unconscious by a spent musket ball, spent the night on the steps of a church altar, waiting to learn whether he still had an army at all.

He did. Barely. And now both sides had crawled into winter quarters, too shattered to fight, while the frozen fields of Saxony were still being picked over for the dead.

To the west, a French army a hundred thousand strong was camped across the German heartland. They had been there for three years — marching, burning, requisitioning, and occasionally being beaten back, only to return. The towns of Westphalia, the old country between the Rhine and the Weser, had changed hands more than once. Soest had seen French soldiers in its streets. The old King of England — who was also the Elector of Hanover, and whose money paid for the army that was supposed to keep the French out — had dropped dead in his palace five weeks ago, and his grandson, a young man who had never set foot in Germany, now wore the crown. No one knew if the soldiers would keep coming. No one knew if the money would hold. The only thing anyone knew for certain was that the war was not over, and that winter, which stopped the armies, did not stop the fear.

And in the town of Soest, on the morning of November 29, 1760, a woman in a black dress stood at the door of a church, and waited to be married.

---

The bells of St. Petri began at half past nine, their iron voices cracking the frozen air above the rooftops of Soest. They had been ringing over this town for six hundred years — through plagues, through sieges, through the burning of the Thirty Years' War that had emptied the streets of half its people and never quite let them fill again. Now they rang for a wedding, and the sound carried across the Hellweg, the ancient east-west road, and over the walls of a town that had once been the beating heart of Westphalia and was now little more than a garrison village of three thousand souls.

It was the kind of late November morning when the cold has a smell to it — damp stone and wood smoke and the faint mineral tang of the salt springs that had made Soest famous a thousand years ago. Frost clung to the green sandstone of the church walls. The bare lindens in the Petrikirchhof stood black against a sky the color of pewter. Across the narrow lane, so close you could nearly reach out and touch its massive tower, the Catholic Dom of St. Patrokli loomed in silence — its own bells still. Two churches, two faiths, separated by a few meters of cobblestone.

Inside, St. Petri was cold enough that you could see your breath.

The *Alde Kerke* — the Old Church, as the people of Soest called it — was the oldest parish church in Westphalia, and it looked it. Its Romanesque nave was a forest of thick stone pillars, their surfaces worn smooth by centuries of shoulders brushing past. The vaulted ceiling above was painted — Christ in majesty, saints with faded gilt halos, patterns of vines and stars that disappeared into the shadows where the candlelight could not reach. The windows were narrow, as Romanesque windows are, and on a morning like this they admitted only a thin, grey wash of light that barely reached the flagstone floor.

Thirty people were gathered in the nave. In better times, in the Soest that had been a Hanseatic capital with ten thousand citizens, this church would have been full for a wedding. But those times were two centuries gone. Half the young men of Soest were somewhere to the east with Frederick's army, or buried in the Saxon mud, or simply gone. French troops had marched through Westphalia that summer. There had been a battle at Warburg in July, not fifty miles to the north, and the older men in the congregation had heard the distant rumble of the guns.

And yet here they were. A wedding.

Anna Elizabeth Wittenberg stood at the door of St. Petri and tried not to shiver.

She was twenty years old. She had been born far from here — in Lubelskie, in the Polish lands, more than six hundred miles to the east, in a German-speaking community that clung to its language and its Lutheran faith amid a sea of Slavic Catholicism. She had come west along the migration routes that connected the scattered German communities of Poland to the heart of the old country, and somewhere along the way — in Brandenburg, in the flat, sandy country east of Berlin — she had met Andreas Micheal Isaak.

They had fallen in love, or come to an understanding, or been matched by families who knew each other through the invisible web of kinship and confession that linked German Lutherans across half of Europe. However it happened, by the winter of 1757 Anna Elizabeth was pregnant, and in 1758 she gave birth to a son. They named him Johann Michael.

They were not married.

In another time, this would have been a simple thing to remedy. A betrothed couple who conceived a child before the wedding was a common enough story in the German lands — embarrassing, subject to a fine and perhaps a public penance before the congregation, but ultimately resolved by a hasty marriage with a dispensation to skip the banns. The pastor would have frowned. The neighbors would have counted the months on their fingers. And then life would have gone on.

But 1758 was not another time. It was the third year of the Seven Years' War, and Brandenburg was burning.

That summer, fifty-two thousand Russian soldiers under General Fermor crossed the Oder and laid siege to the fortress town of Küstrin, not far from the Polish border. On August 25, Frederick threw thirty-five thousand Prussians against them at Zorndorf in one of the bloodiest battles of the war. The fields east of Berlin became a killing ground. Towns were razed. Civilians fled westward with whatever they could carry — and among them, almost certainly, were a young man named Andreas Isaak, a young woman named Anna Elizabeth Wittenberg, and an infant boy.

They came to Soest. Whether it was Andreas's family's town, or simply a place behind the Anglo-Hanoverian lines where a Lutheran family from the east could find shelter, we do not know for certain. What we know is that for two years they lived here as an unmarried couple with a child — a status that carried real weight in a Lutheran community. The church required documentation before it would perform a marriage: proof of income, proof that the couple would not become a burden on the parish, and for Anna Elizabeth, a letter from her home congregation in Poland confirming that the banns had been read and there were no impediments. Getting that letter from Lubelskie while Russian armies occupied the roads between Westphalia and Poland was a feat of patience and luck. Two years of patience. Two years of waiting, with a child growing up unbaptized in his parents' marriage, in a town that was not quite home, in a country that was not quite at peace.

Now, finally, they stood at the door of St. Petri.

Anna Elizabeth wore black. Not mourning, but custom. A German bride wore her finest dark dress to her wedding, a tradition older than anyone could explain. Hers was heavy wool, practical, the kind of dress you wore again to christenings and feast days and, eventually, to funerals. At her throat, a scrap of white lace. On her head, a wreath of dried myrtle — the *Myrtenkranz* — its small, dark leaves woven into a crown that declared what the church required be publicly known: that she was entering this contract freely. Two-year-old Johann Michael was somewhere behind her, held by a relative or neighbor, too young to understand what was happening but old enough to be the reason it was happening at all.

Beside her stood Andreas Micheal Isaak. He was twenty-one — the oldest Isaak we can name with certainty in this place, though the name itself suggests roots that stretch further back into Brandenburg. He wore his best clothes: knee breeches, a waistcoat, a long coat that had been his father's. His hands were rough. He was not a wealthy man. But he had survived the flight from the east, found work, assembled the paperwork, endured whatever public penance the pastor had required for the sin of fathering a child outside of wedlock, and brought his family to this door. It had taken two years. He was here now.

The pastor stood before them in the doorway.

This was the old way, Luther's way, laid down in the *Traubüchlein* — the little marriage booklet that Martin Luther himself had written in 1529 and that had governed Lutheran weddings for over two hundred years. The vows were exchanged here, at the threshold, in the cold November air, so that the whole community could witness that this thing had been done freely and publicly.

The pastor had already read the banns on three consecutive Sundays from the pulpit of St. Petri. No one had objected. Anna Elizabeth had presented her papers — a letter from her home parish, obtained God knows how, confirming that she was free to marry. A woman from Poland, marrying in Westphalia, in the middle of a war. The paperwork alone was a small miracle.

Now the pastor spoke the words.

*"Andreas, do you desire to have Anna Elizabeth as your wedded wife?"*

*"Yes."*

*"Anna Elizabeth, do you desire to have Andreas as your wedded husband?"*

*"Yes."*

They exchanged rings. Plain bands. The pastor joined their hands.

*"What God has joined together, let no one separate."*

And with that, they were married. Legally, publicly, finally. Johann Michael Isaak — two years old, born in Brandenburg during a Russian invasion, carried westward across the breadth of Prussia — was now a legitimate son.

The congregation parted, and Andreas and Anna Elizabeth walked together through the Romanesque doorway and into the nave — into the cold, candlelit darkness of the oldest church in Westphalia, past the thick stone pillars, up the worn flagstones toward the altar. At the altar, the pastor opened his Bible and read the words from Genesis that Luther had prescribed.

*"Then the Lord God said, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper who will stand by his side.'"*

The pastor prayed. He blessed the marriage. The organ filled the vaults and rattled the old glass in the narrow windows.

It was done.

---

That evening, at an inn near the Marktplatz, there was a modest meal — bread, beer, what meat could be had. The night before, their friends and family had gathered in the street for the *Polterabend*, hurling stoneware and crockery against the cobblestones to shatter it — never glass, only earthenware — while the couple swept up the shards together, a ritual older than Christianity, meant to drive away evil spirits and prove that a marriage begins not with perfection but with broken things made whole by shared labor.

They had already proven that.

Somewhere to the east, the war ground on. Three weeks earlier, Frederick had thrown forty-five thousand men against the Austrian lines at Torgau and lost thirteen thousand of them in a single afternoon. The Russians had occupied Berlin in October. The Prussian treasury was empty, the silver melted, the currency debased. But in Soest, on this night, none of that mattered. A family that had been made in flight was now made whole in law.

Johann Michael would not be the last child. The Isaak family was just beginning, and the road ahead of them — eastward, always eastward, from Westphalia back across the German lands and into the Russian steppe of Bessarabia, and then, generations later, westward again across an ocean to the Dakota Territory and finally to California — was longer than any of them could have imagined.

It all began here. In the *Alde Kerke*. In the cold. In the candlelight. With a two-year-old boy in someone's arms, and two young people who had already learned that the world does not wait for you to be ready.

## Chapter Two

# The Warsaw Colonists

---

In the decades following their wedding at the Alde Kerke, Andreas and Anna Elizabeth Isaak raised a family in Soest. Their eldest son, Johann Michael — born before their marriage, in Brandenburg, during the chaos of the Seven Years' War — grew into a young man in a town with no future. Soest was quiet, poor, and shrinking.

Andreas Micheal Isaak died in 1792, at the age of fifty-three. He did not live to see what came next.

Far to the east, the ancient kingdom of Poland was dying. In 1772, 1793, and 1795, three of Europe's great powers — Prussia, Austria, and Russia — carved the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth into pieces and erased it from the map. Prussia swallowed the western territories and renamed them South Prussia. The land was vast, fertile, and underpopulated. The Prussian king wanted German farmers to settle it.

Johann Michael Isaak answered the call. Sometime in the late 1780s, he left Westphalia and traveled east to the Prussian Polish territories, joining thousands of other German settlers — Lutherans, Swabians, Westphalians, Brandenburgers — who were promised land, tax relief, and the chance to build something of their own. He settled near a village called Blacka, in western Poland. He married. His first son was born in 1789, the same year a revolution in Paris began tearing down the old order of Europe. His mother, Anna Elisabeth — widowed, resilient, already in her fifties — came east with him.

For nearly twenty years, the German colonists in Poland lived under Prussian protection. They built farms, churches, and schools. They spoke German, worshipped as Lutherans, and kept to their own communities. It was not home, exactly — but it was theirs.

Then Napoleon came.

In 1806, the French emperor crushed the Prussian army and seized the Polish territories. From the wreckage, he created the Duchy of Warsaw — a French client state with a Polish government. Overnight, the German settlers lost their Prussian protections, their legal standing, and their security. They were now foreigners in someone else's country, surrounded by a Polish population that remembered who had colonized their land.

By 1812, the situation was catastrophic. Napoleon marched 615,000 soldiers through Poland on his way to invade Russia, stripping the countryside of food, horses, and men. Ninety thousand Poles were conscripted into the Grande Armée. Most of them never came back. The German colonists, caught between an empire that had abandoned them and an army that devoured everything in its path, could only watch and wait.

It was in these years — the years of Napoleon, of famine, of uncertainty — that Johann Michael Isaak's younger sons came of age.

---

Blacka, Western Poland — June, 1812

The wheat was not yet ripe when the soldiers came.

Karl August Isaak was nine years old. He was in the east field with his older brother Johann Michael, pulling weeds from between the rows, when they heard it — a low sound, felt more than heard at first, like distant thunder on a clear day. Johann Michael straightened up and shaded his eyes, looking south toward the road. Karl August stood beside him, the pulled weeds still in his fist, and followed his brother's gaze.

The road was half a mile away, visible as a pale line cut through the green where it crossed the low rise beyond the Kowalski farm. Something was moving on it. At first it looked like a dark stain spreading slowly across the landscape, too wide to be a wagon train, too steady to be a herd. Then the stain resolved into shapes — men on horseback, men on foot, wagons, cannon on wheeled carriages — and the low sound sharpened into something recognizable: the rhythmic crunch of thousands of boots on packed earth, the jangle of harness, the distant snap of pennants in the wind.

"Get down," Johann Michael said. He was fourteen, old enough to know what an army looked like, old enough to know you did not want an army to notice you. He put a hand on Karl August's shoulder and pulled him into a crouch between the wheat rows.

They watched from the field as the column passed. It took a long time. The cavalry came first — lancers with red and white pennants, their horses kicking up clouds of dust that drifted across the fields like smoke. Then the infantry, regiment after regiment, their blue and white uniforms smudged grey with road dirt, muskets on their shoulders, faces blank with the particular exhaustion of men who have been marching for weeks and know they will march for weeks more. Behind them came the artillery — heavy guns on iron-wheeled carriages, each one pulled by teams of horses that were already too thin. And behind the artillery, stretching back as far as Karl August could see, came the supply wagons, the ambulance carts, the sutlers, the camp followers, the women and children and dogs that trailed behind every army like a second army of their own.

Karl August had seen soldiers before. Small patrols, couriers, the occasional squadron of cavalry passing through the village on their way somewhere else. But he had never seen anything like this. The column filled the road from edge to edge and spilled over into the fields on both sides, and still it kept coming — an endless river of men and horses and iron flowing east, east, always east, toward Russia.

"How many?" Karl August whispered.

Johann Michael shook his head. He didn't know. Nobody in Blacka had ever seen this many people in one place. Later, they would learn that what they had seen was a fraction of Napoleon's Grande Armée — 615,000 men from twenty nations, the largest invasion force Europe had ever assembled, crossing the Duchy of Warsaw on its way to destroy the Russian Empire. Ninety thousand of those men were Poles, conscripted from the very fields and villages the column was now trampling.

"Will they take our wheat?" Karl August asked.

"They'll take everything," Johann Michael said.

They stayed in the field until the column had passed and the dust had settled and the sound had faded to a hum on the eastern horizon. When they walked back to the farm, they found their father standing in the yard with their grandmother, Anna Elisabeth, watching the last of the wagons disappear. Johann Michael was sixty-four years old. Anna Elisabeth was seventy-two. She had been born in Lubelskie, married in Westphalia, widowed in Soest or Poland — and now she stood in a village in the Duchy of Warsaw and watched Napoleon's army march past her door on its way to a war that would kill a million men in six months.

She said nothing. She had seen armies before.

Their father turned to the boys. "Did they come into the field?"

"No," Johann Michael said.

"They will," his father said. "Tomorrow, or the next day. They'll send foragers. We need to move the stores."

That night, the family buried half their grain in the root cellar beneath the barn, wrapped in oilcloth and covered with straw. It was a crime — requisition officers had the authority to search farms and seize supplies, and hiding food from the army was punishable by imprisonment or worse. Johann Michael did it anyway. He had been a Prussian subject, then a subject of the Duchy of Warsaw, and he knew that governments came and went but winter came every year, and if you did not have grain in the ground come November, your children starved.

The foragers came two days later, as he had predicted. They took a third of the visible stores, two of the family's four horses, and the good wagon. They left a requisition receipt stamped with Napoleon's eagle — a piece of paper that promised future payment from a government that would not exist in eighteen months.

---

Six months passed. The Grande Armée did not come back. What came back were rumors, then refugees, then the wreckage.

By December, the survivors began staggering west through the Duchy — gaunt, frostbitten men in rags, many of them barefoot in the snow, their eyes emptied of everything except the animal desire to keep moving. Of the 615,000 who had crossed the Neman in June, fewer than 100,000 came back. The rest were dead in the Russian snow, or captured, or simply gone. Behind the survivors came the Russian army, advancing steadily westward, occupying the Duchy of Warsaw without resistance.

Karl August watched them too — the defeated men shuffling through the village in ones and twos, begging for bread, sleeping in barns, some of them dying where they fell. He was nine years old and he understood, in the way that children understand catastrophe, that the world had broken and would not be put back together the same way.

By the autumn of 1813, the Duchy of Warsaw had ceased to exist in any meaningful sense. Russian troops controlled the territory. Napoleon was retreating across Germany toward France. And in the German colonists' villages, in the churches and farmhouses and meeting halls where they gathered to speak their own language, a new word was circulating.

*Bessarabien.*

---

Johann Michael first heard the Tsar's offer in the autumn of 1813, though the formal manifesto was issued on November 29 — the same date, to the day, as his parents' wedding at St. Petri, fifty-three years earlier. Tsar Alexander I was calling for German settlers to colonize Bessarabia, a vast territory between the Danube and the Dniester rivers that Russia had taken from the Ottoman Empire the year before. The terms were astonishing: sixty-six hectares of free land for every family. Freedom of religion. Exemption from military service. A ten-year tax holiday. Money for building a house. It was, in every respect, the same offer that had brought Johann Michael to Poland a generation earlier — only further east, and from a different emperor.

The German colonists in Poland listened with open ears. They had been promised security by Prussia and abandoned by Prussia. They had been promised independence by Napoleon and crushed by Napoleon. The land they farmed was about to change hands for the fourth time in twenty years. Their churches had been neglected, their schools underfunded, their legal standing uncertain. More than 1,500 German families would accept the Tsar's offer between 1814 and 1815 alone. They would leave behind farms they had spent decades building, because they had learned — in the hardest possible way — that a farm means nothing if the government above you does not want you there.

Johann Michael, at fifteen, understood this in the way that children of unstable countries understand it — not as politics or history, but as the texture of daily life. You planted crops in the spring without knowing whose flag would fly over the village by harvest. You learned Polish because you had to, but you spoke German at home because your father insisted. You watched soldiers come and go and you learned to stay out of their way. You grew up fast, because the world did not slow down for boys.

Karl August, at ten, understood less but felt more. He knew the farm. He knew the church. He knew his grandmother's face — Anna Elisabeth, eighty-three years old now, the woman who had stood at the door of St. Petri in a black dress and a myrtle crown more than half a century ago, who had outlived her husband by twenty-one years and crossed half of Europe and was still here, still watching, still quiet in the way of women who have seen too many armies pass. He knew the sound the wind made in the flat Polish fields in October, when the harvest was in and the sky was the color of iron. He did not yet know that he would leave this place and never come back.

But that was coming. The Tsar's recruiters were in the village. The older men were talking in low voices after church. Anna Elisabeth sat by the stove and said nothing, because she had heard offers like this before — in Soest, in Brandenburg, in every place the Isaak family had ever been — and she knew what came next.

And their father, Johann Michael Isaak — the son of Andreas and Anna Elisabeth, the boy who had been born in Brandenburg during a Russian invasion and carried west to Soest and then east to Poland — was already making up his mind.

The Isaak family had been moving for three generations. They were about to move again.

## Chapter Three

# The Steppe

---

In January 1813, the Russian army occupied Warsaw. Napoleon's Duchy crumbled. The Congress of Vienna, convened the following year, divided what remained of Poland between Russia, Prussia, and Austria — the same three empires that had carved it up a generation before. For the German colonists who had settled in the Polish territories under Prussian protection, the Congress changed nothing and everything: the land beneath their feet was the same, but the government above them had shifted once again, and with it any claim they had to belonging.

The Tsar, however, had plans for them.

On November 29, 1813, Alexander I issued a manifesto calling for German settlers to colonize Bessarabia — a vast, treeless steppe between the Danube and the Dniester rivers that Russia had seized from the Ottoman Empire just one year earlier. The terms were extraordinary: sixty-six hectares of free land per family, freedom of religion, perpetual exemption from military service, a ten-year tax holiday, government loans for houses and seed, and a daily food allowance of five kopecks per person until the first harvest. The settlers had to be debt-free and willing to leave behind everything they could not carry.

More than 1,500 German families accepted. They traveled in columns — Kolonnen — by horse and wagon, southeast across the breadth of Europe, a journey of more than a thousand miles that took between two and six months. They were the first Germans to arrive in Bessarabia, in 1814, and they were known ever after as the Warsaw Colonists.

Among them were the Isaaks.

---

Blacka, Western Poland — Spring, 1815

They sold what they could not carry and buried what they could not sell.

Johann Michael Isaak spent the first weeks of April settling his accounts — paying off the last of his debts to the village miller, selling the plow and the heavier tools to a Polish neighbor for less than they were worth, trading the milk cow for a second horse because you could not pull a loaded wagon across a thousand miles of road with one animal. The farm itself — the house, the barn, the fenced kitchen garden that Anna Elisabeth had tended for a quarter century — could not be sold. It belonged to the land, and the land belonged to whoever governed it this year, and that was no longer a question Johann Michael cared to answer. He walked away from it.

Karl August watched all of this with the particular attentiveness of a twelve-year-old who understands that something enormous is happening but has not yet been told the full shape of it. He knew they were leaving. He knew the word *Bessarabien*, though he could not have found it on a map. He knew his father had been to the Russian recruiters' office in the nearest town and had come back with papers — a thick packet sealed with red wax — and that his grandmother, Anna Elisabeth, had held the papers in her hands for a long time without speaking, turning them over as if the weight of them might tell her something the words could not.

She was seventy-five years old. She had been born in Lubelskie, Poland. She had married in Soest, Westphalia. She had been widowed twenty-three years ago, when Andreas died in 1792\. She had followed her son to Poland and watched Napoleon's army march past her door and buried grain in the root cellar and waited, as she always waited, for the next thing to happen. Now the next thing was a wagon and a road that led southeast, toward a country she had never seen, and she was going.

Johann Michael — seventeen now, nearly a man — did the heavy work. He loaded the wagon in the grey April mornings while his father went from house to house in the German quarter, coordinating with the other families who were leaving. They would travel in a *Kolonne* — a column of wagons, twenty or thirty families together, for safety and for the simple human need to not be alone on a road that long. Johann Michael checked the axles, greased the wheels, fitted the canvas cover that would be their roof for the next four months. He packed the tools they were keeping — an axe, a saw, a set of chisels, a scythe — in oilcloth beneath the wagon bed. He packed the Bible and the hymnal in a wooden box lined with straw, and set them where they would not get wet.

Karl August's job was the animals. He fed and watered the two horses. He checked their hooves. He was responsible for the crate of chickens that would ride on the back of the wagon, and for the dog, a brown mutt named nothing in particular, who would walk alongside.

On the last evening, Karl August found his brother behind the barn, sitting on an upturned bucket, looking east across the fields. The wheat they had planted last autumn was coming up in pale green rows — wheat they would never harvest. Someone else would cut it, or no one would.

"What do you think it looks like?" Karl August said.

Johann Michael didn't answer right away. He picked up a clod of the dark Polish soil — rich, heavy stuff, the reason their father had come here in the first place — and turned it over in his hands.

"Father says there are no trees."

"None?"

"He says the grass is taller than a man and there is nothing else. No forest. No hills. Just grass and sky."

Karl August tried to imagine this and could not. He had never seen a landscape without trees. The woods beyond the village — birch and oak and linden, thick enough that you could lose sight of the road in summer — were the boundary of his known world. A country with no trees was as abstract to him as a country with no ground.

"What about the house?"

"There is no house. We build it when we get there."

"Out of what?"

Johann Michael looked at him. He was seventeen, and he had their father's face — the same long jaw, the same patient, slightly tired expression of a man who has learned not to expect too much from the world. "I don't know," he said. "Sod, maybe. Or whatever the Russians give us. Father says they give us money for building."

"How much money?"

"Enough, supposedly."

Karl August sat down on the ground next to the bucket. The spring air smelled like wet earth and new growth — the smell of Poland, the only country he had ever known, though it had been called three different things in his twelve years of life.

"Grandmother says the first ones who went last year are already there," he said. "She says they named the towns after battles. Borodino. Tarutino."

"Kulm," Johann Michael said. "That's where we're going. Named after the Battle of Kulm."

"Who won?"

"We did. The Russians, I mean. Against Napoleon."

Karl August pulled at the grass. "Is that a good sign? Living in a place named after a battle?"

Johann Michael almost smiled. "It's a better sign than living in a place named after nothing, which is what this place will be once we leave."

He tossed the clod of soil into the dark and stood up. "Get some sleep. We leave at dawn."

---

They left on a Tuesday in late April, when the roads had dried enough to hold a wagon's weight. The *Kolonne* assembled at the crossroads east of the village — twenty-two families, eighty-some people, a dozen wagons, twice as many horses and oxen, assorted livestock, and everything they owned in the world stacked and lashed and balanced on wooden frames that creaked with every rut in the road.

Anna Elisabeth rode in the wagon. She sat on a folded blanket on the bench seat beside Johann Michael, who drove. She did not look back at the village. She had done this before — left a place she knew for a place she did not — and she understood that looking back was a luxury for people who had a choice. Johann Michael and Karl August walked alongside, as most of the younger people did, because the wagons were too heavily loaded to carry anyone who could walk.

The route led southeast. From western Poland they crossed into Galicia — Austrian territory — skirting the northern edge of the Carpathian foothills. The roads were bad. In places they were not roads at all but mud tracks that swallowed wagon wheels to the hub, requiring the men to unload, lever the wagon free, reload, and continue. They averaged ten or twelve miles a day when the weather held, less when it rained, and it rained often.

The *Kolonne* developed its own rhythm. They rose before dawn, fed the animals, ate whatever bread or porridge could be managed, and were moving by first light. They stopped at midday to rest the horses and eat again, then pushed on until the light began to fail. Camp was made wherever the ground was relatively dry — in a field, beside a stream, under the lee of a hill if they were lucky. The women cooked over open fires while the men tended the animals and checked the wagons for damage. The children gathered wood and water and played in the margins of the road, the way children do even in the middle of a migration, because twelve-year-olds do not stop being twelve-year-olds just because the world is rearranging itself around them.

Karl August collected things. Stones, mostly — interesting ones, unusually shaped or colored — which he kept in his coat pockets until they wore holes in the lining and his mother made him throw them out. He collected feathers, too, and once a spent musket ball he found in a ditch near what must have been a battlefield, though there was no marker and no one could say which battle or which war. He showed it to Johann Michael, who turned it over in his palm and said, "There are enough of those where we're going. Don't keep it." Karl August kept it anyway.

Johann Michael walked mostly in silence. He was old enough to feel the weight of what they were doing — old enough to understand that his father was fifty-seven and his grandmother was seventy-five and that the journey itself might kill them, that people died on these migrations from dysentery and fever and simple exhaustion, that the wagons broke and the horses went lame and the food ran out before the road did. He watched the country change around them — the flat Polish farmland giving way to gentle hills, then steeper hills, then the long, rolling uplands of the Galician borderlands — and he tried to imagine what lay on the other side of all of it.

They crossed rivers whose names no one in the *Kolonne* knew. They passed through villages where the language changed from Polish to Ukrainian to something else — Moldavian, perhaps — and the churches went from Catholic to Orthodox, their onion domes strange and beautiful against the sky. They bought bread when they could and shot rabbits when they couldn't. One of the wagons broke an axle outside a town whose name Karl August never learned, and the whole column stopped for two days while a local wheelwright made the repair and charged them triple for it.

Anna Elisabeth endured. She did not complain, because complaining was not something she did, and because she understood — better than any of them, better than her son, better than her grandsons — that the road was the price of arrival, and that you did not argue with the price after you had already started walking.

---

They reached Bessarabia in late summer, after nearly four months on the road.

The steppe announced itself gradually, then all at once. The hills flattened. The trees thinned and then vanished. The sky, which had been a ceiling, became a dome — enormous, pale, pressing down on a landscape so flat and featureless that the horizon was a perfect unbroken line in every direction. The grass was waist-high and silver-green in the August heat, and it moved in the wind like water, rolling in long slow waves that made Karl August dizzy if he watched too long.

There was nothing else. No fences, no roads, no buildings, no trees. Just grass and sky and the sound of insects and the hot, dry wind that blew from the southeast and tasted like dust and distance.

Karl August stood in the back of the wagon and looked out over the steppe and felt, for the first time in his life, genuinely afraid. Not of anything specific — there was nothing to be afraid of, which was precisely the problem. There was *nothing*. The landscape offered no shelter, no shade, no landmark by which you could orient yourself. It was the largest emptiness he had ever seen, and they were going to live in it.

Johann Michael stood beside him. He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "We'll need to dig a well."

It was such a practical thing to say — so ordinary, so much like their father — that Karl August laughed, and the laugh broke something open in him, and after that the steppe did not seem quite so terrifying.

---

Russian officials met the *Kolonne* at a staging point near the Kogylnik River and directed them to their assigned parcels. The colony had been laid out according to plan — a central road with farm lots extending back on either side, each family receiving their sixty-six hectares of treeless steppe. The settlement was called Kulm, after the battle where the allied armies had defeated Napoleon's forces in August 1813\. It had been officially established the year before, in 1814, by the first wave of Warsaw Colonists, but when the Isaaks arrived, there was almost nothing to show for it — a few sod houses, some freshly broken ground, a well or two, and a wooden cross marking where the church would eventually stand.

---

The government provided what it had promised: money for building materials, seed for the first planting, a daily food allowance until the harvest came in. It was not much. The houses the colonists built that first year were *Erdhütten* — earth huts, dug partially into the ground, with walls of sod bricks and roofs of bundled steppe grass. They were dark, damp, and cold in winter. They leaked when it rained. They were home.

Johann Michel Isaak was fifty-seven years old. He had left Soest as a young man, built a farm in Poland, lost it to Napoleon, and walked a thousand miles to build another one in a country where the wind never stopped and there were no trees to break it. He would spend the rest of his life here, on this steppe, in this colony named after a battle he had not fought in, growing wheat in soil he had never seen before, speaking German to his neighbors and Russian to the officials and praying in a church that did not yet exist.

There was a saying among the Bessarabian Germans, passed down from the first generation to the last: *Die ersten fanden den Tod, die zweiten die Not, erst die dritten das Brot.*

The first generation found death. The second found need. Only the third found bread.

The Isaaks had just arrived. The dying had not yet begun. But the bread — the bread was three generations away, and every one of those generations would earn it.

## Chapter Four

# Black Earth

---

*By 1844, the colony of Kulm was nearly thirty years old. The sod huts of the first settlers had given way to whitewashed mud-brick houses roofed with thatch, each one set on a narrow lot that stretched back a quarter mile into the steppe. Acacia trees — planted by the colonists themselves, because the steppe had none — lined the main street and shaded the yards.*

*The first generation had found death. The second had found need. The third was beginning — just beginning — to find bread.*

*Karl Isaak was forty-eight. He had walked to Bessarabia as a nineteen-year-old and spent his entire adult life turning steppe grass into wheat fields. He had married Anna Elizabeth Louise Schimke in 1819 and given her more children than either of them could easily feed. The farm was sixty-six hectares of the blackest soil in Europe — so rich it needed no fertilizer, so flat you could watch your dog run away for three days.*

*Gottlieb Isaak, Karl's son, was ten years old.*

---

Kulm, Bessarabia — A day in late summer, 1844

The rooster woke him, as it always did, in the grey half-light before dawn. Gottlieb lay on the straw mattress he shared with his brother Andreas and listened to the sounds of the house coming alive — his mother at the stove, the iron clang of the door, water being poured into the kettle.

He dressed in the dark. Linen shirt, wool trousers that had been his brother Gottfried's before they were his, leather boots that were too big and stuffed with rags at the toes. The house was mud-brick — thick walls mixed from the black earth and straw, dried in the summer sun and whitewashed inside and out until they gleamed. Two rooms: the *Stube*, where the family lived and ate around the big clay stove, and the *Kammer* in the back, where his parents slept. The floor was packed earth, swept clean by his mother every morning before anyone else was awake.

His father was already in the yard. Karl Isaak rose before the rooster — a habit from the early years, he said, when the wolves still came at night.

"Feed the chickens," his father said. "Then come eat."

This had been a sore subject lately. Three weeks ago, something had gotten into the henhouse. Gottlieb had been the one to find them — feathers everywhere, blood on the straw, two hens dead and a third dying with her throat torn open. His father said it was a fox. Old Herr Flegel down the street said it was a wolf, the last wolf in the Budschak, come down from the hills to remind the Germans that the steppe had been here first. Karl had patched the henhouse with heavier boards, set a trap baited with offal, and told Gottlieb to check the coop every morning before dawn.

Gottlieb checked. The chickens were alive, irritable, and unimpressed. Four eggs today. He carried them inside in his cap, and his mother cracked two into the pan without comment. Breakfast was eggs, dark bread with lard, and chicory coffee. His father said grace — the same grace in the same German, the same words reaching back through the family like a rope to a church in Westphalia none of them had ever seen.

---

School was a low mud-brick building near the prayer house. One room, one teacher, one purpose: to make Lutheran Germans out of the children of Lutheran Germans.

The teacher was the *Küster* — the sexton — Herr Brauer, a thick-shouldered man with a voice that could fill the prayer house without effort. He also led the Sunday service most weeks, because the pastor lived in Tarutino, an hour's ride away, and could only come once or twice a month. The schoolbooks were the Bible, the hymnal, and Luther's Small Catechism. Reading was taught from Scripture. Writing was taught by copying Scripture. Arithmetic was taught because you needed it to measure land and sell wheat, and for no other reason.

What Gottlieb liked was the singing. The old Lutheran hymns — *Ein feste Burg*, *Nun danket alle Gott* — were the best part of school and the best part of Sunday and, if he was honest, the best part of being alive. When the whole village sang together in the prayer house, the sound was enormous, far bigger than the small building that contained it, and Gottlieb felt that he was part of something that could not be taken away — something that did not change when the borders changed or the government changed or the language on the official documents changed. The hymns were the same. The words were the same. The God was the same.

School let out at noon. Gottlieb walked home along the main street, kicking dust, past the whitewashed houses with their acacia trees and kitchen gardens, past the smithy and the prayer house and the cemetery where the oldest stones — the ones from the hunger years — were already sinking into the black earth as if the steppe were slowly swallowing them.

---

He was halfway home when he saw the camel.

It was standing in Herr Raugust's yard, tied to the fence post, chewing something with an expression of such magnificent contempt that Gottlieb stopped in the road and stared. He had seen camels before — the Nogai Tatars who grazed their flocks on the open steppe east of the colony sometimes brought them through the village, and a few of the Bulgarian traders used them to haul salt from the coastal lakes near the Black Sea. But he had never gotten used to them. They were ridiculous animals. They walked as if they had too many knees, looked at you sideways like they knew something you didn't, and made a noise — a deep, gargling groan — that sounded like a very old man complaining about his dinner.

This one was enormous, taller than a horse by half, its humped back piled with bundles of raw wool. A Tatar trader was sitting cross-legged in the shade of the fence, drinking something from a leather flask, while Herr Raugust examined the wool and shook his head and named a price that the Tatar rejected by spitting on the ground.

Gottlieb edged closer. The camel turned its head and regarded him with one enormous brown eye, its jaw working in a slow circle. It had the longest eyelashes he had ever seen on any creature, and its lips were curled back in what looked exactly like a sneer.

"Don't stand behind it," the Tatar said in broken German, without looking up. "It kicks."

Gottlieb moved to the side. The camel followed him with that one contemptuous eye. Then it groaned — that deep, wet, gurgling sound — and a string of green saliva swung from its lower lip like a pendulum.

Gottlieb laughed. He couldn't help it. The animal was absurd — too tall, too ugly, too proud of itself for something that looked like God had assembled it from leftover parts. His brother Gottfried had once said that God made the camel on the seventh day, after He was already tired, and Gottlieb believed it. No rested creator would have put that face on any living thing.

The Tatar and Herr Raugust were still arguing about the wool. Gottlieb watched for another minute, then walked home, grinning, the camel's offended expression stuck in his head like a song.

---

The afternoon was fieldwork. The wheat stood golden and heavy in the fields beyond the village, and Karl and his older sons cut it with scythes, working in long rows from dawn until the light failed, bending and straightening in a rhythm so ancient it preceded agriculture itself. Gottlieb followed behind with the rake, gathering the cut stalks into sheaves and binding them with twists of straw. Hot, repetitive, back-breaking work. Every sheaf was food for the winter, and winter in Bessarabia was not a thing you took lightly.

The steppe wind blew from the southeast, steady and warm, smelling of dry grass and distance. It never stopped. It was the one constant of life in Bessarabia — more reliable than the rain, more permanent than the government, more faithful than anything except the church. You did not fight the wind. You built your house with its back to it, planted your acacias to slow it, and eventually you stopped hearing it at all, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

They worked until the sun touched the western horizon — a horizon so flat and far that the sunset seemed to take an hour, the sky turning from blue to gold to copper to a deep, bruised red that spread across the entire dome of the heavens. It was the most beautiful thing about this country. The land was plain. The village was plain. The work was plain. But the sky was operatic, excessive, absurdly generous — a sky that belonged to a grander country than this one, a sky that had been here before the Germans and the Bulgarians and the Tatars and the Turks, and that would be here long after all of them were gone.

Gottlieb walked home in the blue dusk, shoulders aching, hands raw from the straw, his boots heavy with the black earth that stuck to everything and never fully came off. His mother had supper on the table — soup with potatoes and onions, bread, a glass of the thin wine that Karl made from his own vines. Grace was said. The same words. The same God.

After supper, his mother sewed by the door. His father smoked his pipe in the yard. The acacias rustled. From somewhere down the street came the sound of someone playing a fiddle, badly.

Gottlieb sat on the doorstep and looked at the sky. The first stars were out — the same stars that shone over Soest and Blacka and Brandenburg and every other place the Isaak family had ever been. He was ten years old. He had never been anywhere but Kulm. He had never seen a forest or a mountain or a city or the sea, though it was only sixty miles to the south. He knew the steppe, the village, the church, the field, and the sky, and he did not yet know that the same restlessness that had driven his great-grandfather from Soest and his grandfather from Poland would, in time, drive his own children across an ocean to a place called Dakota, where the prairie looked almost exactly like this and the wind blew just as hard and the sky was just as big, and where, at last, after six generations of moving, the Isaak family would finally stop.

But that was decades away. Tonight, the wheat was almost ready. The stars were out. The wolf, if it was a wolf, had not come back. And Gottlieb Isaak — ten years old, great-great-grandson of a woman who had married in a black dress in a cold church in Westphalia — was home.

## Chapter Five

# The Contract

---

*By the 1850s, the mother colonies of Bessarabia were full. The original sixty-six hectares that each family had received from the Tsar were being divided among sons and grandsons, and the math was unforgiving — what had sustained one family could not sustain four.*

---

**Kulm, Bessarabia — Winter 1853**

Karl August Isaak died on a Tuesday in November, at fifty-seven, and left behind a widow, a sixty-six-hectare farm, and ten living sons.

The trouble started before the funeral was over.

It did not look like trouble. It looked like brothers standing around a kitchen table after a burial, drinking coffee, not saying much. Gottfried, the eldest at thirty-three, stood at the head of the table because no one had told him not to, and no one told him not to because he had been running the farm for the last five years while their father's health failed, and everyone knew it. Christian and Karl Ludwig flanked him, thick-armed and sunburned, men who had been pulling plows since boyhood. Andreas leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed, already calculating.

The younger boys lined the walls. Gottlieb, nineteen, stood next to August, who was fourteen, who stood next to Johann, who was eleven, who stood next to Daniel, who was nine, who stood next to Martin, who was six and did not understand why everyone was so quiet.

Their mother sat in the corner and said nothing. She had buried a husband that morning. She did not have the energy to referee what was about to happen.

Nobody spoke the word *land*. They didn't need to. The farm was sixty-six hectares. There were ten of them. Even little Martin, standing on his toes to see over the table, could feel the weight of that arithmetic pressing down on the room like a change in weather.

What followed was not a single conversation but a slow, grinding negotiation that stretched across months — argued in kitchens and barns and fields, in voices that started reasonable and ended raw. The custom in the colonies was equal division among sons, but custom was a luxury that assumed a manageable number of heirs, not a battalion. Gottfried had five mouths to feed. Christian had four. They needed more land, and they said so, and they were not wrong. Andreas, who had married young and started building his own household, fought for the parcels nearest the village road. The younger brothers — still boys, most of them — got what was left, which in some cases was a strip of earth barely wide enough to turn a plow on.

Gottlieb did not fight. He watched. He watched Gottfried and Andreas square off across a rough sketch someone had drawn on the back of a feed bill, arguing over a piece of bottomland no wider than a wagon track. He watched Christian walk the boundary lines with a length of rope, measuring and remeasuring, as though precision could solve a problem that was not about precision but about the simple, brutal fact that the earth did not multiply with the family.

He was the seventh son. He understood, with the clarity of a man looking at a horizon he cannot reach, that his inheritance was not land. It was the knowledge that there was not enough.

---

**Kulm, Bessarabia — December 17, 1854**

He married Louisa Schulz a year later, on a cold Tuesday in the prayer house at Kulm. She was seventeen. He was twenty. The pastor from Tarutino performed the service — the same Lutheran rite, the same vows at the door, the same reading from Genesis — and afterward there was a meal at his mother's house, because the house was the family's now, which meant it was nobody's, which meant it was crowded and tense and full of brothers pretending the food was what mattered.

They tried. For five years, they tried. They worked their thin strip alongside the brothers, sharing tools and draft animals and the particular friction of men who love each other and resent each other in equal measure. Their daughter Regina came in 1856 — a healthy girl, strong-lunged, born in the same room where Gottlieb had been born twenty-two years earlier. The following year, Louisa bore a son, Simon, who lived eleven months and then did not. They buried him in the colony cemetery on a hill where the steppe wind never stopped, and Louisa did not speak for three days afterward, and Gottlieb did not ask her to. Jacob arrived in 1858, then David in 1860, and with each child the house grew louder and the fields grew quieter, because there was simply nothing left to grow.

Gottlieb would later describe those five years in Kulm in a single sentence: *troubled and burdened, primarily because of the shortage of land.* He did not elaborate. He did not need to. Every young couple in every mother colony in Bessarabia understood exactly what those words meant.

In 1859, he packed a wagon, loaded his wife and three surviving children, and left Kulm for a daughter colony called Sophiental, where a Russian landowner was renting parcels to Germans who could not afford to be choosy. It was not a triumphant departure. Nobody lined the road. His brother Andreas, who had wanted to leave but couldn't, watched from the fence and said nothing. August, still a teenager, helped load the wagon and then stood in the yard with his hands in his pockets, looking like a boy watching a train he wasn't old enough to board.

Johann — quiet, eleven-year-old Johann — walked alongside the wagon for half a mile before Gottlieb told him to go home. He turned back without a word, but he stood at the crossroads and watched until the wagon disappeared into the steppe, and Gottlieb, looking over his shoulder, saw him there — a small figure on the flat plain, not waving, just watching — and felt something shift in his chest that he would not be able to name for another sixteen years.

---

**Sophiental, Bessarabia — 1860s**

Sophiental was not Kulm.

Kulm sat in the Bessarabian black earth belt — the richest farmland in southern Russia, soil so dark and deep you could push your arm in to the elbow. Sophiental was rented land, and the soil was light and sandy, pale as flour compared to the heavy black loam Gottlieb had grown up with. The rent was cheap, which was why they had come. The harvests were poor, which was why the rent was cheap.

You learned this trade-off with your hands, not your head. Every spring the wheat came up thin and yellow instead of thick and green. Every autumn the yield was short. Every winter you counted what was in the granary and what was owed to the landlord and tried to make the first number cover the second.

Most years, it didn't. *Most of the time, cattle and horses had to be sold to pay for the rent,* Gottlieb would write later, and the sentence has the flatness of a man who has said the same thing so many times that the anger has gone out of it and only the fact remains. You sold a horse to pay the rent. Then you plowed with fewer horses. Then the harvest was worse because you had plowed with fewer horses. Then you sold another horse.

Louisa bore children. The house filled. The farm did not grow.

And then, because the steppe had not yet finished with Gottlieb Isaak, the village elected him mayor.

---

**Sophiental — 1873**

The crop failed completely in 1873\.

It was not only Sophiental. The entire region — the sandy country south and east of the mother colonies, where the daughter settlements scratched at soil that gave grudgingly in good years and gave nothing at all in bad ones — went dry that summer. The rain did not come. The wheat did not come. The corn withered in the fields. By August it was clear that there would be no harvest, and by September it was clear what that meant: no feed for the animals, no seed for next year's planting, no money for the rent, and nothing to live on.

Gottlieb was thirty-eight years old, the father of a growing family, and the mayor of a village that was about to starve.

The contract with the landowner — the Russian who owned the soil they farmed — had a clause for this. Gottlieb had read it carefully, because he was the kind of man who read contracts carefully. In the event of a crop failure, the clause said, the rent payment would be delayed and rolled into the following years. It was there in writing. It was clear. It was, he believed, the law.

He believed wrong.

The administrators — the Russian officials who managed the estate — came to Sophiental in the autumn and demanded the rent. Gottlieb showed them the contract. They told him to submit intelligence reports on the village's financial condition. He submitted them. They asked again for the money. He pointed again to the clause. The correspondence went back and forth through the fall and into the winter, the letters growing shorter and colder on both sides, until one day a letter arrived that ended the conversation.

The contract, it said, had been sold to the Crown's broker. The rent was due immediately. If the money was not paid, the colony would be cleared away and removed. They had thirty days.

---

**Akkerman — Thirty days later**

The justice court in Akkerman was a stone building in a town that smelled like the sea, which was only a few miles away. Gottlieb had never been to Akkerman. He had never been to court. He wore his best clothes — the same coat he wore to church, brushed and pressed by Louisa — and he carried the contract in his inside pocket, folded into quarters, the paper already soft from handling.

The judges sat behind a long table. There were three of them. Gottlieb stood.

He knew, before anyone spoke, that it had been arranged. The judges did not look at him the way men look at someone whose case they are weighing. They looked at him the way men look at someone whose case has already been decided, with the particular boredom of officials performing a formality.

"Submit the money or leave," they said, voting as one.

Gottlieb requested that the contract be read aloud. This was his right, and they could not refuse it, and so the clerk read the contract, line by line, in a droning voice, while the judges waited.

When the clerk reached the paragraph about crop failures — the clause that specified, in plain language, that rent payments would be delayed in the event of a failed harvest — Gottlieb stopped him.

"Is this as expressed in the contract?" he asked.

"It has been decided," the judge said. "There is no other way."

"For what purpose is the contract?"

The judge looked at him. It was not an unkind look, exactly — it was worse than unkind. It was the look of a man explaining something to a child who is too slow to understand it on his own.

"So you will know how to behave yourselves," the judge said. "And that settles the matter."

Gottlieb stood in the stone courtroom and felt the words land on him like a physical weight. He thought of things he could say. He thought of the German word *Gerechtigkeit* — justice — and the Russian word for prison, which he also knew. He thought of Louisa at home with the children, waiting for him to walk through the door with an answer, and he thought of what her face would look like when he told her what the answer was.

He said nothing. He borrowed the money from his neighbors and paid what the contract said he did not owe. He went home. He went back to work.

But he kept the contract. He folded it and put it in a drawer, and for the rest of his life it stayed there, next to a Bible and a hymnal — the paper getting softer, the ink getting fainter, the words *so you will know how to behave yourselves* getting louder.

---

**The Decree**

The Tsar's decree had come two years before the crop failure, though its full weight would not be felt for years.

On June 4, 1871, Alexander II revoked the privileges that had drawn the German colonists to Russia in the first place — the exemption from military service, the right to self-governance, the freedom to worship and educate their children in German. Everything the old tsars had promised was now undone. The colonists' sons would be drafted into the Russian army. Their schools would teach in Russian. Their villages would answer to Russian administrators.

They had ten years to comply.

The colonists responded the way the Isaaks had always responded when a government broke its promises: they started looking for the door.

Railroad companies were distributing pamphlets across Europe and Russia, advertising the American frontier — vast, cheap, empty grasslands in a place called Dakota Territory, where the United States government was offering 160 acres of free land to anyone willing to settle it. The Northern Pacific Railroad had reached Dakota in 1872\. Scouts from the colonies traveled to America and came back with reports that sounded almost too familiar: flat country, black soil, big sky, hard winters. It was the steppe. It was Bessarabia, but without the tsar.

The first families left in 1873\. Within two years, the trickle had become a river, and the colonies were hemorrhaging families the way a man hemorrhages blood — slowly at first, then fast enough to notice, then fast enough to kill.

---

**Sophiental — Autumn 1875**

Johann showed up on a Sunday.

Gottlieb hadn't seen his youngest brother in months — the fifteen miles between Sophiental and Kulm might as well have been a hundred when the fields needed working — and he was surprised to see him at the fence, climbing down from a borrowed cart with the careful, unhurried movements of a man who has driven a long way to say something important.

Johann was thirty-three now. He had grown into the quietest of the Isaak brothers, which was saying something in a family not known for wasted words. He had their father's jaw and their mother's eyes and a way of standing in a doorway that made you feel like he'd been there for an hour before you noticed him. He had never married — or rather, he had married late, to a woman as practical as himself, and they had no children yet, which meant he was the lightest of the brothers. The one with the least to lose and the most to carry.

He found Gottlieb behind the barn, mending a harness that had already been mended twice, and he sat down on an upturned bucket and watched his brother work without saying anything for a while, which was how Johann began most important conversations — with silence, until the silence itself became a kind of opening.

"I'm going to America," he said.

Gottlieb's hands stopped moving on the leather.

"I've been reading the letters," Johann said. "The Kellers went last spring. The Schmidts. The Wentzels. Half of Kulm is talking about it, and the other half is packing." He paused. "I want to go first. Get across, find the land, file the homestead. Then send for you."

Gottlieb set the harness down. "You're talking about the Dakota Territory."

"I'm talking about a hundred and sixty acres of free land." Johann let the number sit in the air between them. A hundred and sixty acres. Their father's entire farm in Kulm had been sixty-six hectares — about a hundred and sixty acres — and it had been divided among ten sons. What Johann was describing was the whole thing. The whole farm. For one family. For free.

"The pamphlets say the soil is black," Johann said. "The settlers say it's like Kulm — the real Kulm, the black earth, not this." He gestured at the sandy fields behind them, the pale, exhausted ground that had been slowly starving Gottlieb's family for sixteen years.

"Pamphlets say a lot of things."

"So do the Kellers. So do the Schmidts. They're not pamphlet writers, Gottlieb. They're farmers. They know dirt."

Gottlieb stood up and walked to the corner of the barn where he could see the fields — his fields, or rather, the landlord's fields that he rented and worked and bled into and sold horses to keep. The steppe stretched out flat and pale and empty in every direction, the same view he had looked at every day for sixteen years, the same sandy nothing that had taken a baby son and a milk cow and three draft horses and most of his pride and given back just enough wheat to keep them alive but never enough to get ahead.

"I've got nine children," he said.

"I know."

"Louisa."

"I know."

"The passage alone —"

"That's why I'm going first." Johann stood up. He was taller than Gottlieb, and thinner, and he had a way of holding himself that made him look like a man standing at a ship's railing, looking at something on the horizon that no one else could see yet. "One ticket. One man. I cross, I find the land, I build something. You sell the farm, settle the debts, get the passports. By the time you're ready, I'll be waiting at the station in Dakota with a wagon and directions to your quarter section."

"My quarter section," Gottlieb repeated. The words sounded foreign. They sounded American.

"Your quarter section. Your land. No landlord. No broker. No contract that someone can sell out from under you while you're standing in a courtroom being told how to behave."

Gottlieb looked at his brother sharply. Johann had not been in Akkerman. He had not stood before those judges. But the story had traveled — of course it had, everything traveled in the colonies, every humiliation and every injustice passed from mouth to mouth like scripture — and Johann had carried it with him for two years the way Gottlieb had carried the contract, folded up and put away but never forgotten.

From inside the house came the sound of Louisa singing to the baby — a German lullaby, the same one their mother had sung in Kulm, the same one her mother had sung before that, the melody threading backward through the generations like a rope tied to a place that no longer existed.

"Let me talk to Louisa," Gottlieb said.

Johann nodded. He picked up the harness, examined Gottlieb's repair, and finished the stitch his brother had abandoned — tighter, neater, the kind of small correction that only a younger brother would dare.

---

Johann left Bessarabia in the early spring of 1876\. He traveled by rail to Bremen, by steamship across the Atlantic, and by train to the Dakota Territory, arriving on the first of May in a country he had never seen, on a continent he could barely imagine, carrying everything he owned in a single trunk.

He wrote his first letter home within weeks. It was two pages long, practical, unhurried, and it changed everything.

The soil was black. The wind was constant. The land was flat and enormous and free. He had filed a homestead claim. He was building a sod house. The nearest neighbor was a family from Tarutino who had arrived the year before and already had twenty acres broken. There was a creek for water. There were no trees, but there were also no landlords, no brokers, no Russian officials, and no one — not a single person in the entire territory — who had ever told him how to behave himself.

The letter reached Sophiental in the autumn, and Gottlieb read it at the kitchen table, and then he read it again, and then he handed it to Louisa without a word.

---

**Sophiental — Autumn 1877**

August came on a Saturday.

He rode the fifteen miles from Kulm after the last of the harvest was in — what there was of it, which wasn't much — and he brought a jug of *Schnaps* and the particular set of his jaw that Gottlieb recognized immediately. It was their father's jaw. It was the jaw of a man who has made a decision and is working up to saying it out loud.

They sat behind the barn, out of the wind, with a fire between them. The steppe sky was enormous and cold and full of stars. From inside the house came the muffled sounds of too many children in too few rooms — a baby crying, an older boy shushing it, Louisa's voice cutting through like a blade, restoring order.

August passed the jug. "When?" he said.

"Spring."

"The boys?"

"Jacob is nineteen. David is seventeen. If I wait another year, they'll be on the conscription rolls and we'll never get the passports."

August nodded. He drank. He stared at the fire.

"I can't go with you," he said. "Not yet."

"I know."

"One more harvest. I need to settle things with Gottfried — the land, the house, the plow team. He's being Gottfried about it." August smiled thinly. Being Gottfried about something meant being stubborn and righteous and absolutely certain that whatever Gottfried wanted was what God wanted, which was a personality trait that their eldest brother had refined into a kind of art form. "Six months. I'll be behind you by six months."

"You'll be behind me by an ocean."

"I'll be behind you by six months," August said again, firmly, as though repetition could collapse the distance between Bessarabia and the American prairie. "You go in the spring. You get across. Johann's already there — he'll have the land, the house, whatever he's built. You get settled, and by the time the first snow falls, I'll be on a train from New York with four children and a wife who's going to kill me for doing this to her."

Gottlieb laughed. He couldn't help it. The image of August's wife — a formidable woman from Leipzig colony who had once thrown a soup pot at a tax collector — arriving on the Dakota frontier with four children and murder in her eyes was too vivid to resist.

"She'll adapt," August said. "She always does."

"She threw a pot at a government official."

"Exactly. She'll do well in America."

They laughed together, and the laughter felt like something breaking loose — not joy, exactly, but release. The release of men who have been carrying something heavy for a long time and have just realized they can set it down.

The fire burned low. The jug went back and forth. The stars wheeled overhead, the same stars that had watched over every place the Isaak family had ever been — the same stars that were, at this exact moment, shining down on Johann in a sod house in the Dakota Territory, lying awake on a straw mattress, thinking about his brothers on the steppe and wondering if they had decided yet.

They had.

August stayed the night. In the morning he stood in the yard, squinting against the autumn sun, looking east across the fields — the pale, sandy, miserable fields that had been the whole of Gottlieb's world for eighteen years.

"I won't miss it," August said.

"No," Gottlieb said. "But you'll remember it."

August climbed into the cart and drove toward Kulm. Gottlieb watched him go until the cart was a speck on the plain, and then he turned and went inside.

The kitchen table was covered in the ordinary debris of a family morning — cups, bread crumbs, a child's wooden toy, a scrap of mending Louisa hadn't finished. He cleared a space and laid out Johann's letters, one by one, in the order they had arrived. Next to them he placed the contract from the landlord — the one the judge in Akkerman had told him existed so he would know how to behave.

Louisa was at the stove. Her back was to him. She had heard everything the night before — every word, through the thin walls and the open window — and she had not said a thing, because Louisa was the kind of woman who listened first and spoke only when she knew exactly what she wanted to say.

She did not turn around.

"Yes," she said.

Gottlieb folded the letters and put them back in the drawer. He left the contract on the table. He would not need it where they were going.

## Chapter Six

# The Crossing

---

**The Road to Odessa — March 1878**

August came with the cart at dawn.

He had driven through the night from Kulm — fifteen miles in the dark, the horse picking its way along frozen ruts by moonlight — because August was the kind of man who would rather lose a night of sleep than risk being late for something that mattered. He pulled up in front of Gottlieb's house just as the sky was going grey in the east, and he sat on the bench and waited without knocking, because the lamp was already lit inside and he could hear the family moving.

They had been packing since before the sun went down. Louisa had not slept. She had spent the final hours walking through the empty rooms of the house — the house that was no longer hers, that had been sold to a family from Tarutino along with the tools and the livestock and everything else that would not fit in two trunks — touching the walls, the doorframe, the window ledge where she had set a candle every Christmas for eighteen years. She did not cry. She had cried earlier, in private, and she was finished with it now.

Gottlieb carried the trunks out. Jacob helped. David helped. Daniel, who was sixteen and furious at being treated like a child, carried the smaller bags with an exaggerated display of strength that fooled no one. Regina, twenty-two, moved through the house behind Louisa, checking the rooms one last time, closing doors that would never need to be closed again.

Solomon stood in the yard, watching everything. He was twelve, and he had not stopped talking since the previous afternoon. "Where does the ship leave from? How big is the ship? How long does it take to cross the ocean? What language do they speak in America? Will there be Indians?" David, who had appointed himself Solomon's handler, answered each question with the patience of an older brother who has long since accepted that silence is not an option.

Magdalena came out last. She was nine, holding her younger sister Emilie by the hand — Emilie was three and had been crying since she woke up, not because she understood what was happening but because the house was empty and empty houses frightened her. Wilhelmina, eleven, walked behind them, carrying a cloth bag with the hymnal and a wooden doll that had been hers since infancy and that she had refused, absolutely and without negotiation, to leave behind.

Louisa carried Antonie. The baby — not quite two — slept against her mother's shoulder, oblivious to the fact that she was leaving the only country she would never remember.

Eleven people. Two trunks. One Bible, one hymnal, and the clothes on their backs.

August climbed down from the cart and picked up the heavier trunk and loaded it without a word — the same gesture Johann had made two years earlier, and the same gesture he would make again in six months, when it was his own family's turn. He looked at Gottlieb. Gottlieb looked at the house. The house looked back at him with its dark, empty windows, already forgetting who had lived there.

"Let's go," Gottlieb said.

They loaded the cart — the children climbing in among the trunks and bags, finding places to sit the way children always do, by argument and negotiation and the occasional sharp elbow. Louisa climbed onto the bench next to August. Gottlieb walked. Jacob and David walked. Daniel walked, though he made it clear through the particular angle of his shoulders that he would have preferred to ride.

The cart turned south toward Akkerman, and the village of Sophiental fell behind them, house by house, until it was just a line of low rooftops against the steppe sky, and then it was nothing at all.

---

They reached Akkerman by midday.

The road came down from the steppe into the town along the shore of the Dniester estuary — a broad, flat arm of water, grey-green and still, stretching east toward the Black Sea — and as they descended, the fortress appeared.

Solomon saw it first. "What is *that*?"

It rose from the rocky shore like something out of a story that no one in the family had ever been told — massive walls of white limestone, fifteen meters high in places, stretching for what seemed like miles along the water's edge. Towers — round ones, square ones, some intact and some half-crumbled — punctuated the walls at intervals, and behind the walls the citadel loomed, its own towers rising above the rest like a castle within a castle. The whole structure was enormous and ancient and utterly unlike anything in the German colonies, where the tallest building was the church steeple and the oldest thing was a fifty-year-old acacia tree.

"It's the fortress," Gottlieb said. "Akkerman. It's been here since before the Turks."

He did not say more, because Akkerman held other memories for him — the stone courtroom, the judges, the contract — and he did not want to think about those things on this morning, on this road, heading in this direction. But Solomon could not stop staring, and Magdalena could not stop staring, and even Daniel, who was pretending not to be impressed by anything, turned his head as they passed and watched the walls slide by with the involuntary attention of a boy seeing something that is simply too large to ignore.

They did not stop. They did not enter the town. August kept the cart moving south along the coast road, and the fortress receded behind them — first the towers, then the walls, then just a pale smear of limestone against the grey sky — and Solomon asked how old it was, and Gottlieb said, "Six hundred years, maybe more," and Solomon was quiet for a while, which was unusual enough that David checked to make sure he was all right.

He was fine. He was thinking about a thing that was six hundred years old and still standing, and he was trying to understand what that meant for a family that had been in one place for fifty years and was already leaving.

---

South of Akkerman, the road followed the coast, and for a few miles they could see the Black Sea.

Gottlieb had been here before. Not often — the colonies were inland, and a farmer had little reason to visit the coast — but he had come once or twice, years ago, to sell grain in Akkerman or to settle some piece of business, and he had walked down to the shore and stood there and looked at the water the way a man from the steppe looks at anything that is not the steppe: with suspicion and wonder in equal measure.

The children had never seen it. Solomon and Daniel scrambled to the back of the cart to look. Even Emilie stopped crying and stared, her small mouth open, at the flat grey expanse stretching to the horizon. It was the biggest thing any of them had ever seen — bigger than the steppe, bigger than the sky, bigger than anything they had been taught to imagine.

"That's the Black Sea," David said to Solomon, who was gripping the side of the cart with both hands.

"It doesn't look black."

"It's the name."

"Why is it called that if it's not black?"

David did not know. Nobody knew. The sea was grey and green and silver, and it moved in slow, flat swells that caught the light and scattered it, and the wind coming off it smelled like salt and fish and distance, and the gulls wheeled above it, crying in voices that sounded nothing like the hawks of the steppe.

Louisa looked at the water and then looked away. She had never been comfortable with large things she could not understand, and the sea was both. Gottlieb watched her face and said nothing, but he thought about the ocean — the real ocean, the Atlantic, the one they would cross in a few weeks — and he wondered whether it would look like this or whether it would be something else entirely, something bigger and wilder and more indifferent, and whether Louisa would look away from that too, or whether by then she would have run out of places to look.

---

**Odessa**

They reached Odessa in the late afternoon — sixty-five miles from Sophiental, a full day's journey by cart — and August pulled up at the railway station and climbed down and stood in the street and looked at his brother.

This was where they parted. August would drive the cart back to Kulm, sell the horse, settle his own affairs, and follow in six months. Gottlieb would board a train heading west, toward Bremen, toward a ship, toward the other side of the world.

The brothers did not embrace. That was not how the Isaak men said goodbye. They stood facing each other in the noise and dust of the Odessa street — two middle-aged farmers from the steppe, sunburned and rough-handed, dressed in their church clothes because they owned nothing better — and they said the things men say when they are trying not to say the thing that matters.

"Write when you get there," August said.

"I will."

"Tell Johann I'll be behind you."

"I will."

August looked at the children in the cart — at Solomon, who was already asking questions about the train station; at Magdalena, who was watching him with her wide, quiet eyes; at little Antonie asleep on Regina's shoulder. He looked at Louisa, who met his gaze and nodded once, firmly, the way she did everything.

"Take care of them," August said.

"I always have."

August climbed back onto the cart. He picked up the reins. He looked at Gottlieb one more time, and then he turned the horse and drove north, back toward the steppe, back toward Kulm, back toward the life he had six more months of living before he would stand in this same street and board this same train and follow his brothers west.

Gottlieb watched him go. Then he turned to his family — to Louisa, to Regina, to Jacob and David and Daniel and Solomon and Magdalena and Wilhelmina and Emilie and Antonie — and he said, in a voice that was not loud but that carried: "Let's pray."

They bowed their heads right there on the platform, eleven people standing among their trunks while the crowd of a Russian port city flowed around them like water around stones. Gottlieb prayed in German — the plain, direct German of the Lutheran colonies, without flourish, without poetry, just a man asking God to protect his family on a journey that was too big for any man to manage alone. He asked for safe passage. He asked for strength. He asked for the courage to keep walking when the road got dark, and he asked — this last part quietly, almost to himself — for forgiveness for leaving the place where his parents were buried, and for the faith to believe that the place he was going would be worth what it cost to get there.

Louisa said *Amen*. The children said *Amen*. Solomon said *Amen* slightly too loud, the way he did everything.

They boarded the train.

---

**The Train to Bremen**

The railway connected Odessa to the European network, and the journey that had taken Gottlieb's grandfather four months by wagon would now take days. The children pressed their faces to the windows. Most of them had never been on a train. Solomon kept asking how fast they were going, and Daniel kept inventing numbers until Louisa told them both, in the voice, to sit down and be quiet.

They crossed the border out of the Russian Empire. No ceremony. No farewell. Just a change in the uniforms of the officials and a stamp on the passport and a line on a map that meant they were no longer subjects of the Tsar — that Russia was finished with them and they with it. Gottlieb felt the moment pass through him like a shudder, though he said nothing about it and no one noticed.

Austria-Hungary. The bread tasted different. The language on the platform signs changed. The officials wore different uniforms but looked at them with the same indifference.

Then Germany.

Germany was not what they expected. It was green. It had trees — not the stunted, wind-bent acacias the colonists had planted on the steppe, but forests of them, thick and old and everywhere. The towns were close together, built of brick and stone, and there were factories with chimneys that smoked day and night, and the trains ran on time, and the language on the station signs was the same language they spoke at home but also somehow different — faster, sharper, full of words they didn't know. The Bessarabian Germans were steppe people, a century behind the industrialized nation that Germany had become. They passed through it like visitors from another age.

Jacob stood at the window for hours, watching the countryside, and did not speak. David noticed, the way David noticed everything, that his older brother's eyes were wet, and he said nothing about it. It was the Fatherland. They had heard about it their entire lives — in hymns, in stories, in the way their grandparents said the word *Deutschland* with a particular softness, like a prayer for a place they would never see again. And here it was, green and prosperous and utterly foreign, and they were passing through it on a train, headed somewhere else.

Magdalena pressed her nose to the glass and fogged it with her breath and drew pictures in the fog with her finger — houses, trees, a horse. Louisa pulled her back and wiped the glass with her sleeve, but Magdalena did it again a minute later, and Louisa let her.

---

**Bremerhaven**

Bremen. The end of Europe.

The Norddeutscher Lloyd operated out of Bremerhaven, where passengers were processed, housed, and medically examined before boarding. The through-ticket they had purchased covered everything — the train to the port, the Atlantic crossing, and the railway from New York to Dakota. It was the most expensive thing Gottlieb had ever purchased. It represented the entire proceeds of a liquidated life.

They were housed in emigrant barracks near the quay — long, clean, institutional rooms where families slept in rows, waiting for their ship. The barracks smelled like carbolic soap and boiled potatoes and the particular anxiety of people who have sold everything they own and are now waiting for the next thing to happen. Solomon made friends with a boy from a Swabian family headed for Nebraska. Emilie cried most of the first night and would not be consoled until Regina took her outside and showed her the ships in the harbor — enormous iron-hulled vessels with smokestacks and masts, the largest things any of them had ever seen.

On the second evening, Gottlieb gathered the family in their corner of the barracks and read from the Bible. He chose Psalm 107 — *Those who go down to the sea in ships, who do business on great waters; they see the works of the Lord, and His wonders in the deep* — and his voice, reading the familiar German words in this unfamiliar place, drew the attention of the families in the neighboring bunks, who stopped what they were doing and listened, because the words were the same words they all knew, and the fear was the same fear they all carried, and for a few minutes the barracks was not a holding pen for emigrants but a church without walls, the way every room becomes a church when someone reads scripture into the silence.

The ship was the SS *Mosel*. She was 349 feet long and 3,200 tons, built in Greenock, Scotland. She carried ninety first-class passengers, 126 second-class, and six hundred in steerage.

The Isaaks were in steerage.

---

**The Atlantic**

The *Mosel* departed Bremerhaven and steamed down the coast to Southampton, where she took on English passengers and coal.

Solomon stood at the rail as the ship entered the English Channel and watched the water with an expression that shifted, over the course of an hour, from excitement to awe to something that looked very much like fear. He had seen the Black Sea from the road — flat and grey and far away — but this was different. This was beneath him. The ship was *on* it, and it was moving, and there was no bottom he could see and no shore he could reach and the water stretched in every direction without end. He gripped the rail and did not let go until David came and stood beside him and put a hand on his shoulder and said, very calmly, "It's just water."

"It's not just water," Solomon said. "It's all the water."

David looked at the Channel — the grey-green chop, the distant smudge of the English coast, the sails and smokestacks of other ships — and he could not argue with this.

Steerage was below the waterline, in compartments fitted with rows of narrow wooden bunks stacked three high. The air was close — a permanent fog of body heat, damp wool, engine grease, and the sweetish stench of seasickness. The engines throbbed through the iron hull day and night, a vibration you felt in your teeth. Six hundred people in a space designed for efficiency, not comfort, rolling west across the Atlantic at thirteen knots.

Louisa was sick the first week. Not violently — she was too proud for that, or too stubborn — but a steady, grinding nausea that kept her in the bunk with her eyes closed, one hand on the wooden rail and the other on little Antonie, who slept beside her with the boneless indifference of a toddler. Regina took over. She had been her mother's deputy for years, and she moved through the compartment with quiet competence — feeding Emilie, keeping Solomon from climbing the bunk ladders, making sure Daniel and Wilhelmina ate something even when the ship rolled and the smell made eating feel like an act of defiance.

Jacob helped without being asked and never mentioned it. David studied the rigging, asked a deckhand in broken gestures how the engine worked, and came back with information that no one had requested but that Solomon, at least, found fascinating.

Gottlieb spent hours on deck, which was technically reserved for the higher classes, but the crew did not enforce the boundaries strictly, and Gottlieb was the kind of man whose quiet persistence made people step aside rather than argue.

The Atlantic was not the Black Sea. He had known this in theory, but knowing it and standing in the middle of it were different things entirely. The Black Sea had been large but comprehensible — you could stand on the shore at Akkerman and see the curve of the coast, the line of the estuary, the fishing boats coming in at evening. It was a sea with edges. The Atlantic had no edges. It moved without reference to anything human — calm one hour and violent the next, the water heaving in long, slow swells that lifted the bow and dropped it and lifted it again, endlessly, day after day, as though the ocean were breathing and the ship were a thing caught in its throat.

It was beautiful. It was terrifying. It was both at once, and Gottlieb, who had spent his life on the flattest land in Europe, stood at the rail and watched it and understood, for the first time, that the world was not flat but curved, and that somewhere beyond the horizon where the water met the sky there was a country he had never seen, and that his whole family was balanced on the skin of this enormous, indifferent, breathing thing, rolling west at thirteen knots, toward something that might be salvation or might be nothing at all.

On the seventh day, Magdalena came up on deck and stood beside him. She did not say anything. She was nine, and she had already learned the Isaak habit of standing next to someone in silence as a form of conversation. They watched the water together — the grey-green swells, the white crests, the occasional shadow beneath the surface that might have been a whale or a trick of the light. After a long time, she reached up and took his hand, and they stood like that until Louisa sent Daniel to fetch them for supper.

On the tenth night, with the ship rolling in heavy seas and the steerage compartment groaning and retching around them, Gottlieb knelt beside the family's bunks and prayed again. The children were awake — all of them, even Emilie, even Antonie — because sleep was impossible when the floor tilted thirty degrees in each direction and the timbers screamed. He prayed the Lord's Prayer in German, and one by one the children joined in, their voices thin and frightened in the dark — *Vater unser im Himmel, geheiligt werde dein Name* — and from the bunks around them, other families joined too, other German voices, other Lutheran voices, the prayer spreading through the compartment like a candle being passed from hand to hand until the whole of steerage was praying together in the dark, six hundred people in the belly of an iron ship in the middle of an ocean, asking the same God in the same language to bring them through the night.

The ship brought them through the night.

---

**New York Harbor — April 13, 1878**

They came up on deck in the grey light of early morning and saw America.

No Statue of Liberty — not yet. Lady Liberty would not be dedicated for another eight years. What they saw instead was the harbor: a forest of masts and smokestacks, ferries and tugs and barges in every direction, and beyond them the skyline of lower Manhattan — not the skyscrapers of the next century, but a dense wall of brick and stone that rose from the waterline like a cliff.

Solomon stood on his toes at the rail, trying to count the ships, and gave up at forty. David stood beside him, quiet for once, his hand on his younger brother's shoulder. Magdalena watched from behind them, recording everything with her wide, serious eyes.

Castle Garden. The old circular fort at the southern tip of Manhattan, converted into an immigrant processing station in 1855\. The Isaaks entered through the main doors into a vast circular hall echoing with a thousand conversations in a dozen languages. Clerks sat behind counters recording names. Interpreters shouted. Currency exchangers called out rates. The air smelled like disinfectant and sweat and the sea.

They registered. Russia, they said, because that was what Bessarabia was, officially, though they had never felt Russian and never would. Farmer. Dakota Territory. A clerk wrote it down. A doctor checked their eyes, their throats, their hands. Healthy. Admitted.

Then they were out — into New York, into the noise and light and impossible density of the largest city in the Western Hemisphere. Louisa pulled Antonie tight against her and held Emilie's hand and did not let go of either until they were inside the railway station. Jacob carried one trunk on his shoulder. David carried the other. Solomon and Daniel ranged ahead like scouts, drawn by everything, restrained by nothing except the sound of their father's voice.

They boarded a westbound train. New York vanished behind them before they had time to understand it, which was perhaps for the best. New York was not for them.

---

**The Train West**

Four days across a continent so vast it made the Bessarabian steppe look like a garden.

Pennsylvania — green, rolling country, thick with forests and rivers. Gottlieb pressed his face to the window and stared. In Bessarabia, a single tree was an event. Here, trees grew in uncountable millions, covering the hills like fur, and no one seemed to notice.

Pittsburgh at night. The furnaces glowing orange against the dark sky, the rivers reflecting the fires of the steel mills so that the water itself seemed to burn. Solomon pressed his face to the glass and whispered, "Is it always on fire?" and nobody answered because nobody knew.

Chicago. The platform swarming with more people than lived in the entire Akkerman district. A man selling sausages from a cart called out in German — Bavarian German, not the Bessarabian kind — and Gottlieb bought four and divided them among the children, and for a moment, standing in the middle of the largest railroad hub in the world, they were just a family eating sausages.

West of Chicago, the country changed.

The forests thinned. The towns shrank. The hills flattened. The green gave way to gold, then to the pale straw of prairie grass in early spring — not yet fully green, the winter brown giving way to the first pale shoots. The sky opened up, wider and wider, until it was the whole world again.

Gottlieb sat up straight. He knew this. He *knew* this landscape — the same treeless plain, the same wind-bent grass, the same enormous sky. Louisa saw it too. She put her hand on his arm and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. They had crossed an ocean and half a continent to arrive at a place that looked like the place they had left.

West of the Mississippi, the prairie opened into something beyond language. It was April, and the spring thaw had turned the low places to mud and standing water, and the higher ground was covered in last year's grass, bleached white by winter, with the new green just pushing through. The light was different here — clearer, sharper, throwing long shadows across a landscape that had no end.

Then Solomon saw the antelope.

A herd of them — thirty, maybe forty — standing in the grass a quarter mile from the tracks. Tan-and-white bodies catching the afternoon light. Not deer, not goats, not cattle — something else entirely, something native to this place the way the acacias had been native to the steppe. They stood watching the train with enormous dark eyes that held no fear, only curiosity.

"What are they?" Solomon said, his face flat against the glass.

"*Antilopen*," David said, though these were not the antelope of any book he had read. They were pronghorn — the fastest animals in North America — and they watched the train pass with the calm indifference of creatures who had been watching things cross this prairie for ten thousand years.

The herd broke and ran. Not in panic — in a fluid, coordinated burst of speed that made Solomon gasp — and then they were gone, flowing over a ridge and into the grass as though the prairie had swallowed them.

"I want one," Solomon said.

"You can't have one," Magdalena said, without looking up from the window.

"Why not?"

"Because they're faster than you."

The train pushed north through Minnesota and into the Dakota Territory. At each stop, families disembarked — Germans, Norwegians, Swedes — stepping off into the grass and the wind and the mud, met by a man with a wagon or sometimes by no one at all, just the prairie and the sky and 160 acres of unbroken sod waiting somewhere beyond the horizon.

The April air smelled like wet earth and snowmelt and the sharp green smell of grass deciding, after a long winter, to come back to life.

---

**Yankton, Dakota Territory**

The train reached the end of the line on a Wednesday afternoon.

Gottlieb stepped down onto a wooden platform. The wind hit him immediately — the same wind, the same direction, the same steady, grass-scented pressure that had blown through every day of his childhood in Kulm. He stood on the platform with Louisa and the children and their trunks, blinking in the afternoon light, and looked out at the Dakota prairie.

Then he saw his brother.

Johann — John, as he called himself now — was standing at the far end of the platform next to a wagon and a pair of horses, squinting against the sun. He was older than Gottlieb remembered — weathered by two Dakota winters, browned by Dakota sun — but he had the same Isaak jaw, the same patient eyes, the same unhurried way of moving that their father Karl had carried from Poland to the steppe. He had done what he said he would do. He had crossed first. He had found the land. And he had come to the station with a wagon, the way he promised, because Johann was the kind of man who kept his promises.

He walked over and picked up the heaviest trunk without a word, as if his brother had been gone for an afternoon instead of two years. Then he said something in the quiet German of the colonies — something about the road being muddy from the thaw — and the sound of that voice, speaking that language, in this place, broke something open in Gottlieb's chest that he had been holding shut since Sophiental.

Louisa looked at the prairie — the flat, treeless, wind-scoured, limitless prairie — and she looked at Johann, and she said, "Show me the house."

"It's sod."

"I've lived in sod before."

"I know you have."

He took the reins. The children piled into the wagon — Jacob and David on the tailgate with their legs hanging over, Regina holding Antonie, Solomon already asking Johann about the quarter section, Magdalena watching the prairie, Daniel pretending not to be impressed, Wilhelmina sitting quietly next to Emilie, who had fallen asleep against a flour sack.

The wagon rolled west, into the grass, into the wide open nothing that looked almost exactly like the Budschak — the same treeless plain, the same enormous sky, the same wind. The Isaak family had spent 118 years looking for a landscape that felt right, and they had finally found it — not in Germany, not in Poland, not in Bessarabia, but here, at the end of a wagon road in the middle of a continent that none of their ancestors could have imagined.

David was quiet for a long time, watching the prairie unfold behind them. Then he said, softly: "It looks like home."

He did not mean Sophiental. He did not mean Kulm. He meant something older than either — the steppe itself, the flat world, the big sky, the wind that his grandfather and his great-grandfather and his great-great-grandfather had known, all the way back to the flat country of Westphalia where it all began.

Gottlieb put his hand on his son's shoulder. The mud sucked at the wheels. The horses leaned into the harness. The April wind carried the smell of wet earth and new grass and the clean, mineral edge of snowmelt from hills he could not yet see.

From somewhere in the wagon, Solomon was asking what kind of birds those were — the big ones, circling high above the grass.

"Hawks," David said.

"What do they eat?"

"Rabbits, mostly."

"Are there rabbits here?"

"There's everything here," David said, and from the way he said it — quietly, with wonder, like a man seeing something for the first time that he has been imagining his whole life — Gottlieb knew that his son had already begun to love this place, and that the Isaaks, after six generations of leaving, had finally arrived somewhere they would stay.

## Chapter Seven

# Wide Open Spaces

---

*The Homestead Act of 1862 promised 160 acres of free land to anyone who would settle it, build on it, and farm it for five years. There was one condition the German-Russians had not anticipated: you could not live in a village. The law required each family to reside on its own claim, separated from its neighbors by a quarter mile or more of open prairie. For a people who had lived in tight-knit colonies for a hundred years — who had shared a single street, a single church, a single schoolteacher — the isolation of the American homestead was a kind of shock. But the land was free, and free land was something no Isaak had ever had.*

---

**Near Parkston, Dakota Territory — April 1878**

Johann's wife had made Bierocks.

They were sitting around his brother's table — Gottlieb, Louisa, the children, Johann and his family — eating the stuffed bread rolls that every German-Russian woman made slightly differently and every German-Russian man considered a matter of serious importance. Johann's wife filled hers with beef and cabbage and onion, the dough golden and tight, and Gottlieb ate three of them before he said a word, because he had been eating train food and ship food and emigrant-hall food for six weeks and his body recognized this meal the way a man recognizes his own language after months of listening to someone else's.

"The soil," Gottlieb said finally, wiping his mouth. "Tell me about the soil."

Johann grinned. He was younger than Gottlieb but looked older now — the Dakota sun had baked him brown and lean, and he moved with the loose confidence of a man who owned his own land and owed nothing on it. "Black," he said. "Like Kulm. The real Kulm."

"Not like Sophiental."

"Nothing is like Sophiental. Sophiental was God's punishment for something we did in Poland."

Gottlieb laughed. It was the first time he had laughed since Bremen, and the sound surprised him. Louisa looked over from the other end of the table, where she was helping Johann's wife clear the dishes, and something in her face softened — not quite a smile, but the memory of one.

"How deep?" Gottlieb asked.

"Deep enough. You can push a rod in to your elbow and not hit clay. It's never been farmed. It's never been anything. Just grass, since the beginning of the world."

"And the rain?"

"It rains. Not like Bessarabia — you don't pray for it the same way. It comes." Johann leaned back in his chair. "The winters are hard. Harder than the steppe. But the land is good, and it's yours, and nobody comes to your door asking for rent."

That word — *rent* — landed on the table between them like a stone. Johann knew the story of Akkerman. Every Isaak knew the story of Akkerman.

"No rent," Gottlieb said quietly.

"No rent. No landlord. No contract that teaches you how to behave." Johann reached across the table and tore a Bierock in half. "Welcome to America, brother."

---

**The First Year**

They had no money. This was not a figure of speech. The through-tickets from Bremen had consumed nearly everything the sale of the Sophiental farm had produced, and what was left after the crossing barely covered seed and a few tools. But they had hands — eleven pairs of them, if you counted Emilie's, which were three years old and not yet useful — and they had the Homestead Act, which asked nothing except that you stay and work.

Everyone worked. Gottlieb and his older sons — Jacob, David, Daniel — broke virgin prairie, cutting through sod that had been accumulating roots for centuries, turning the earth for the first time in its history. The work was brutal. The sod was thick and tangled and resisted the plow the way the Bessarabian sand never had, because this soil was alive — dense with roots and worms and the compressed organic wealth of ten thousand years of grass growing and dying and growing again. It fought back. But when it finally turned, the underside was black and rich and deep, and Gottlieb knelt down and picked up a handful and held it to his nose and smelled it, and the smell was the smell of Kulm, the real Kulm, the Kulm of his childhood, and he closed his eyes and squeezed the dirt in his fist and did not say anything because his sons were watching and he was not the kind of man who cried in front of his sons.

Louisa and Regina planted the *Baschtam* — the kitchen garden, cucumbers and pumpkins and watermelons — and that first summer's garden was extraordinary. *The likes of which we never again had,* Gottlieb would write, and the sentence trembles with remembered abundance. The soil had never been asked for anything, and when they finally asked, it gave back with a generosity that bordered on the absurd — melons the size of a man's head, cucumbers longer than a child's arm, pumpkins so heavy that Solomon, who was twelve and determined to be useful, could barely carry one.

American wages were a revelation. Every member of the family who could work hired out to neighboring farms, and the money that came back was astonishing. *The wages received amounted to more than three times the amount I had to pay in Russia for expenses,* Gottlieb wrote, and the sentence has the quiet shock of a man who has just discovered that the world is not, in fact, arranged to cheat him. Everything the children earned came into his hands, and he counted it at the kitchen table the way he had once counted rubles — carefully, suspiciously, as though the money might disappear if he looked away. But it did not disappear. It accumulated. It became a pair of oxen, a cow, swine. It became twenty-five acres of broken prairie, and then fifty, and then a sod house that was rough and dark but solidly built, and for the first time in Gottlieb's life, the math was going in the right direction.

The old debt from Russia — 330 rubles, the money he had borrowed to pay the rent the Akkerman court had demanded — he paid it off. The favorable exchange rate between dollars and rubles made it possible, and paying it gave him a satisfaction that had nothing to do with money and everything to do with closing a book that should never have been opened.

In 1880, two years after they arrived, Louisa gave birth to a son. They named him Emanuel. He was born in Parkston, Dakota Territory — an American from his first breath, the first Isaak in the family's long history who would never know what it meant to be a subject of a king, a tsar, or anyone else. Louisa was forty-two. She had been bearing children for twenty-four years, across two countries and five relocations, and she held this new one with the practiced tenderness of a woman who knew exactly what she was doing and was not yet finished doing it.

---

**August — November 1878**

Six months after Gottlieb's arrival, almost to the day, August stepped off the train at Yankton with his wife and four children.

Gottlieb was waiting on the platform with a wagon. He had driven down from Parkston the day before, and he stood in the November cold with his hands in his pockets and watched his brother descend from the passenger car — thinner than he remembered, road-worn, carrying a trunk on one shoulder and a sleeping child on the other. August's wife came behind him with the older children, her face set in the particular expression of a woman who has crossed an ocean with small children and is ready to murder someone but has not yet decided whom.

The brothers looked at each other across the platform.

"You're late," Gottlieb said.

"The ship was slow."

"Everything about you is slow."

August set the trunk down and grinned — the first real grin Gottlieb had seen on his brother's face since the campfire in Sophiental, a year and a lifetime ago. "I'm here," he said. "That's what matters."

He was right. He was here. The chain was complete — Johann first, Gottlieb second, August third, each brother arriving to find the one before him already established, already able to point west and say *this is where the land is, this is where you'll sleep, this is where we begin.*

Over the next several years, the chain kept growing. Letters crossed the Atlantic in both directions — from Dakota to Bessarabia and back — and with each letter another family made the decision, sold the farm, obtained the passport, boarded the train. Gottlieb's brothers and cousins arrived in waves: Gottfried, the eldest, came with his grown children. Martin, the youngest, came as a young man looking for land of his own. Cousins from Kulm and the neighboring colonies followed, drawn by the same reports that had drawn Johann and Gottlieb — black soil, free land, no tsar. By the mid-1880s, the prairie around Parkston was filling with German-Russian families who had known each other in Bessarabia, who attended the same Lutheran services on Sundays, who made Bierocks with the same recipes and sang the same hymns and complained about the same weather in the same Swabian-inflected German, and who had, collectively, picked up an entire colony and set it down again on the other side of the world.

They even named a town after the old colony. Kulm, North Dakota. Because what else would you name it.

---

**David and Hanna — 1883**

David met Hanna Hetzler the way most young people met in the German-American communities of Dakota Territory — at church, or at a neighbor's table, or at one of the harvest gatherings where the families came together and the young men stood on one side of the room pretending not to look at the young women and the young women stood on the other side pretending not to notice.

She was not what anyone expected.

Hanna was American. Not German-Russian-pretending-to-be-American — *American*, born in Missouri in 1861, raised in a German-speaking household but with English as her native language, comfortable in this country in a way that the Bessarabian families, with their colony habits and their steppe accents, were still learning to be. Her parents had crossed the Atlantic in 1852, a full generation before the Bessarabian exodus, and they had raised their daughter in the particular confidence of people who consider themselves settled rather than arrived.

David noticed her. Of course he noticed her — she spoke English without an accent, she understood how things worked at the county land office, she could read the signs on the storefronts in Parkston that the rest of the family puzzled over. She was practical and direct and she did not suffer foolishness, which was a trait she shared with every woman David had ever admired, starting with his mother.

They married on August 30, 1883\. David was twenty-three. Hanna was twenty-two. The ceremony was Lutheran — the same rite, the same vows, the same reading from Genesis that had married Gottlieb and Louisa in Kulm, and Andreas and Anna Elisabeth in Soest, and every Isaak couple going back to the beginning. But it was performed in Dakota, on American soil, by an American pastor, and if the words were the same, the world they were spoken into was very different.

Gottlieb approved. He did not say so — Gottlieb was not a man who distributed approval out loud — but he watched David and Hanna together and he saw something that pleased him: his quiet son had married a woman who could navigate the country that Gottlieb himself would never fully understand. David could farm. Hanna could talk to the Americans. Between them, they might manage.

Their first daughter, Josephine, was born in 1885\. Their second child — a boy — came on June 13, 1887\. They named him Albert Friedrich Isaak, and he was born in Dakota Territory, an American from his first breath, and he would carry the Isaak name into the twentieth century and beyond.

But that was decades away. For now, he was an infant in a sod house on the prairie, and his grandfather Gottlieb held him the way old farmers hold babies — carefully, with rough hands, as if the child were something that had come up unexpectedly from good soil.

---

**The News from Bessarabia**

Even in the good years, the old world reached across the ocean.

In 1882, a letter arrived — not a letter of invitation or instruction but the other kind, the kind that came in an envelope that felt heavier than it should. Gottlieb's mother, Anna Elizabeth Louise Schimke, had died in Kulm. She was eighty-three. She had been born before Napoleon's invasion of Russia, had walked to Bessarabia as a girl, had raised twelve children on the steppe, and had outlived her husband by nearly thirty years. She died in the colony, in the house on the land that her husband Karl had divided among too many sons, and she was buried on the hill where the wind never stopped, next to the husband who had walked a thousand miles to give her a home.

The letter had taken weeks to cross the Atlantic. By the time Gottlieb held it in his hands, his mother had been in the ground for a month. He read it at the kitchen table, folded it, and put it in the drawer where he kept the other letters — Johann's, August's, all the letters that connected the old world to the new. Then he went outside and stood in the yard and looked at the Dakota sky, which was the same sky that hung over Kulm, and he thought about the woman who had carried him on her hip while his father argued with his brothers over the land, and who had watched him load a wagon and drive away to Sophiental without saying the thing she must have wanted to say, which was: *don't go.*

The following year, his brother Daniel died. The old colony was sending him obituaries now instead of letters. But the living were coming too — Martin arrived, and cousins, and families from Tarutino and Leipzig, and the prairie around Parkston grew more German every season, until the sound of Swabian German in the general store was more common than English, and the children who had been born in Bessarabia were growing up alongside children who had been born in Dakota, and the two groups could barely tell each other apart.

---

**The Move North — 1886**

Then the sons wanted land of their own, and there was none to be had.

Every quarter section around Parkston had been filed on. The colonization had been that fast — eight years, and the land was gone. The sons would have to go north, into the open country of central Dakota, where the railroad was still pushing into unsurveyed territory.

Gottlieb could have stayed. He was fifty-two. He had built the best farm of his life. The debts were paid, the house was solid, and for the first time since Kulm he was not worried about the rent or the harvest or the Russian army. He could have handed the farm to one of the older boys and sat on his porch and watched the wheat grow and lived out his years in something approaching peace.

But he was Gottlieb Isaak, and Gottlieb Isaak did not let his children go somewhere without him.

*"Children, if you are going to leave, I too do not wish to remain here."*

He found a buyer and sold the farm. The whole family packed again — Gottlieb and Louisa, David and Hanna with Josephine and the infant Albert, Solomon, Magdalena, Daniel, the younger children. They traveled south to Yankton, east to St. Paul, Minnesota, then west on the Northern Pacific Railroad to Hebron, North Dakota.

Hebron. The hills.

Gottlieb and Louisa stepped off the train and saw, for the first time in either of their lives, a landscape that was not flat. The country around Hebron rolled and buckled into rough, broken hills — buttes and coulees and eroded badlands that looked nothing like anything they knew. It looked nothing like the steppe. It looked nothing like Parkston. It looked, if anything, like the surface of the moon, if the moon were brown and covered in scrub grass and had a wind that could strip the skin off your face.

*Then my wife and I first perceived the hills; we were of one mind: "In this place we do not want to die."*

But they had provisions, feed, seed, and a herd of swine, and they were not the kind of people who turned back. They planted potatoes — an enormous amount — and broke fifty acres and built a sod house. David worked alongside his father, breaking the same ground, eating the same dust. Hanna, holding Albert on one hip, surveyed the landscape with the expression of a Missouri-born woman who had married into a family of professional optimists and was beginning to question her judgment.

Then they waited for the rain.

The rain did not come.

*It was real dry, no rain. The time arrived for haying, no grass, and what was planted and seeded, did not come up. The potatoes remained in the ground and not a penny was derived as an income. We were fearful that everything, man and beast, would have to die from hunger during the next winter.*

---

**Mercer County, North Dakota**

Solomon saved them.

He was twenty-one — the boy who had asked too many questions on the train from New York, the boy who had wanted to catch a pronghorn, the boy who had spent his childhood watching and asking and never being satisfied with the first answer. He had grown into a young man of considerable restlessness and even more considerable nerve, and when the Hebron farm failed, he did what no one else in the family had the energy to do: he rode sixty miles west with Johann Klein, looking for better land.

They found it along the Knife and Missouri rivers — flat, open, grass-covered country that looked, at last, like the landscape the Isaaks had been chasing since Soest. A few Irish and English families lived there, mostly hunters. A handful of Germans from Hannover had settled nearby. The higher ground was empty.

*This was exactly what we wanted.*

Gottlieb and Klein bought railroad land. The sons homesteaded government claims around them. David filed his own claim — his first land, his own name on the deed, the first piece of earth in five generations of Isaaks that belonged to one man and could not be divided among ten brothers or sold to a broker or taken by a judge.

The first winter in Mercer County was brutal. The sod house overflowed with visitors — other settlers arriving daily, drawn by the same open land, needing a place to sleep until they could build their own. *During every meal two servings had to be made,* Gottlieb wrote. At night, the visitors slept on hay spread across the floor, body to body, while the wind howled and the snow piled against the walls.

But the land was good. *We were, however, able to console ourselves with the feeling that we had good land, which made us all feel more content.* Good land. It was always good land. The promise that came from the earth itself — the only promise, in Gottlieb's long experience, that had ever been reliably kept.

---

**The Church**

There was no Lutheran congregation when the Isaaks arrived in Mercer County. There was no church. There was no pastor, no prayer house, no building of any kind where the German families could gather on Sunday mornings and sing the hymns they had carried across an ocean and hear the scriptures read in the language they had spoken since Soest.

This was, for David, intolerable.

He was the quietest of the Isaak sons — everyone said so, and his obituary would confirm it: *naturally of a quiet and retiring disposition.* But beneath the quietness was a faith as solid and unyielding as the sod beneath the prairie grass, and a conviction that a community without a church was not a community at all but merely a collection of farms.

He organized the founding of the Evangelical Lutheran congregation himself. Not loudly, not publicly — that was not David's way. He went from homestead to homestead, speaking to families he knew from the crossing, from Parkston, from the old colonies. He asked them what they needed, and what they needed was what he needed: a place to worship. He arranged the first services in a schoolhouse — the Krem Schoolhouse, a rough wooden building that served as classroom, meeting hall, polling station, and, on Sunday mornings, church. The families came in wagons from miles around, the women carrying food for the potluck after the service, the men standing outside in the yard talking about the weather and the wheat until the bell rang — not a real bell, not yet, just someone banging on a piece of iron with a hammer — and they filed inside and sat on wooden benches and bowed their heads and prayed in German to the God who had brought them across an ocean and a continent and who, they trusted, had not brought them this far to abandon them in the grass.

David was there every Sunday. He was there when the first pastor arrived — a circuit rider who served three or four congregations across a hundred miles of prairie, arriving every other week on horseback, dusty and tired and carrying a Bible in his saddlebag. He was there when they took the collection for a permanent building. He was there when they laid the cornerstone. *He was one of the founders of the Eureka Lutheran church and served her faithfully until his death,* the obituary would say, and the sentence, in its plain simplicity, understates what David had done. He had built a church. Not with his hands — though his hands were there for that too — but with the steady, modest persistence of a man who believed that faith was not something you carried in your heart alone but something you built in the world, with lumber and nails and the donated labor of your neighbors, so that it would still be standing when you were gone.

---

**County Commissioner**

Then came politics, and with politics came trouble of a kind Gottlieb had not expected.

He was elected county commissioner — the only German on a board of five, the other four being English-speaking Americans who had been in the territory longer and who regarded the German-Russian settlers with the particular suspicion that established people reserve for newcomers who work too hard and complain too little.

*Oh, what a time I had.*

The county was badly in debt, despite having almost no population. The bills came in thick and heavy — contracts and requisitions and expenses that Gottlieb could see were inflated, padded, designed to extract money from a treasury that had no money to extract. The four English commissioners voted yes on everything. Gottlieb voted no.

They did not appreciate this.

He was a German farmer with an accent and a Bible and a deep, Bessarabian suspicion of any official who told you to submit the money or leave. He had stood before the Akkerman court and watched judges vote as one. He knew what arranged outcomes looked like. And he said no — *most of the time I said "no,"* he wrote — until finally the flow of inflated bills began to slow, not because the other commissioners agreed with him but because it is exhausting to steal from a treasury when one man at the table keeps asking to see the contract.

*Eventually more Germans became county commissioners and the county was released from the burden of heavy debts, but not during my time. I was only there for six years.*

Six years of being the only German in the room, saying no in a language the others pretended not to understand, fighting the same fight he had fought in Akkerman — the fight of a man who believes that words on paper should mean what they say.

David watched his father navigate American politics the way he had watched him navigate Russian courts — with a mixture of admiration and the quiet conviction that there had to be a better way. David's way would not be politics. David's way would be business.

---

**Magdalena's Wedding — October 27, 1889**

That autumn, Gottlieb gave away his daughter.

Magdalena — the girl who had pressed her face to the train window and drawn pictures in the fog, the girl who had taken her father's hand on the deck of the *Mosel*, the girl who had watched everything with those wide, recording eyes — was twenty-one now, and she was marrying Adam Sailer at the Krem Schoolhouse, the same wooden building where David had founded the church.

The marriage certificate was numbered one. It was believed to be the first marriage recorded in Mercer County under the new state of North Dakota, which had been admitted to the Union just days earlier. A German-Russian couple, married in a schoolhouse-turned-church, on the raw frontier of a state that was less than a week old. Marriage certificate number one. The Isaaks had been in this country for eleven years and they were already part of its founding records.

---

**The Portrait — 1900**

In 1900, when Gottlieb was sixty-six and Louisa was sixty-two, they sat for a photograph.

It was taken in a studio — a proper one, with a painted backdrop and a heavy wooden camera on a tripod and a photographer who arranged them carefully and told them not to move, which was unnecessary because Gottlieb and Louisa had never moved quickly in their lives and were not about to start for a man hiding behind a box.

They sat together in the center of the frame — Gottlieb in his best coat, his face as weathered and unreadable as the steppe itself, his hands folded in his lap with the studied formality of a man who is deeply uncomfortable being looked at but is doing it because his wife asked. Louisa sat beside him, solid and serious, her mouth set in a line that could have been disapproval or could have been the expression of a woman who has spent forty-six years married to a man who does not like sitting still and is trying not to smile about it.

Their daughter Antonie stood behind them. She was twenty-four, the baby who had crossed the Atlantic on Louisa's hip, who had no memory of Bessarabia and never would. She was the one who would marry Pastor Bruntsch, who would preserve her father's autobiography, who would be the last of Gottlieb's children to die — in 1958, in Arlington, Virginia, a long way from the steppe.

And there was Emanuel. The American. The first Isaak born on this side of the ocean, twenty years old now, standing at the edge of the frame with the slight, restless energy of a young man who is already looking past the camera toward a life that will take him to Wisconsin and Iowa and Illinois — away from the Dakotas, away from the German-Russian colony orbits, away from wheat and sod and the endless prairie. He would be the one who left. Every family has one.

The photographer took the picture. The shutter clicked. Four people frozen in silver and light — a patriarch who had walked out of one empire and into another, a matriarch who had crossed an ocean with nine children and never once looked back, a daughter who would save the story, and a son who would scatter it.

They did not know any of this yet. They were just a family sitting for a photograph in a studio in South Dakota, wearing their best clothes, trying not to blink. The century was turning. Louisa had five years to live. The autobiography had not yet been written. The Bible that would contain it had not yet been given. The Golden Wedding was still four years away.

But the photograph survived. It always survives. Long after the names are forgotten and the farms are sold and the sod houses crumble back into the earth they were made from, the photograph remains — four faces, one moment, a family that had traveled farther than any of them could measure, sitting still at last, looking into a camera, looking into the future, looking at you.

## Chapter Eight

# The Hardware Store

---

*By 1892, Gottlieb Isaak was fifty-eight years old. He had homesteaded near Parkston, broken sod at Hebron, settled in Mercer County, served six years as county commissioner, and survived every crop failure, hard winter, and crooked official that Dakota Territory could throw at him. He was tired. His bones ached in the cold. His hands, which had gripped plow handles and scythe handles and wagon reins since he was old enough to walk, were stiff in the mornings and did not always close the way he told them to.*

*His son David had a proposition.*

---

**Eureka, South Dakota — 1892**

The hardware store was David's idea.

He had been thinking about it for years — since the Parkston days, maybe longer, since the first time he walked into an American general store and understood that the man behind the counter worked indoors, year-round, and did not depend on the rain. David was thirty-two, married to Hanna, father of three surviving children: Josephine, Albert, and Edward. He had farmed his homestead in Mercer County and done well enough, but farming had always been his father's calling, not his. David wanted walls. He wanted a roof that didn't leak when the wind blew. He wanted a building with his name on it.

Eureka was the right town for it. A growing settlement in McPherson County with a railroad connection, German-speaking settlers pouring in from every direction, businesses opening, churches organizing. It was not a frontier anymore — it was becoming a town, with a main street and a general store and a post office and the particular energy of a place that has decided it intends to last. The proposition came through a man named Mewing, who was already in the hardware business. The deal was a partnership: Gottlieb, David, Solomon, and Mewing would run the store together, pooling their capital and their labor.

Gottlieb had misgivings. He said so. *I had misgivings, wondering if the wife and I had the proper determination for this.*

He put the question to Solomon and Klein. Their answer surprised him.

*"Do you think we want to remain here? At the first opportunity that comes along, we are going to leave."*

Even his own children were ready to move on from Mercer County. The land was good, but the winters were savage and the isolation was crushing — sixty miles from the nearest proper town, surrounded by empty prairie and English-speaking hunters who had no interest in the kind of community the German-Russians needed.

Gottlieb took the deal. The whole clan moved to Eureka in the fall of 1892 — Gottlieb and Louisa, David and Hanna with the children, Adam Sailer and Magdalena's family that same autumn. Solomon, Klein, and Netzer followed in 1893\. The Isaaks were doing what the Isaaks had always done: reassembling in a new place, the way they had reassembled in every new place since Soest.

---

**The Store**

The hardware store stood on the main street of Eureka, between the grain elevator and the land office, and for the first time in the Isaak family's history, the center of their life was not a field but a building with a door and a bell above it.

David ran the day-to-day. He had a knack for it — the quiet disposition that made customers trust him, the head for numbers that farming had never required, the ability to stand behind a counter for ten hours and treat every man who walked through the door as though his purchase of a box of nails were the most important transaction of the day. He was not a natural salesman — he was too modest for that, too reserved — but he was honest, and in a frontier town where everyone knew everyone else and your word was the only credit you had, honesty was worth more than charm.

Hanna kept the books. She was better at it than David — faster with figures, sharper with the ledger, more willing to tell a customer that his account was overdue. She spoke English without an accent, which mattered when the non-German customers came in, and she had the Missouri-bred directness of a woman who had grown up in a country where people said what they meant and expected you to do the same. David handled the floor. Hanna handled the money. Between them, the store ran.

Gottlieb handled what he could, though he was a farmer in a shopkeeper's apron and never entirely at ease. He stood behind the counter with the slightly bewildered expression of a man who has been plowing fields for forty years and has suddenly been asked to sell nails. He knew every tool on the shelves — knew what each one did, knew when it was wearing out, knew which brand held an edge and which one didn't — but the act of selling felt foreign to him, an indoor version of a life he had always lived outdoors, and he never quite shook the feeling that he was playing a role in someone else's story.

Albert was five when the family moved to Eureka. He grew up behind that counter the way other boys grew up behind a plow — absorbing the rhythms of commerce the way his father had absorbed the rhythms of the steppe. He stacked shelves. He swept floors. He learned to count change before he learned to read English, because the customers who came through the door spoke German and paid in American coins, and someone had to bridge the gap. He watched his father deal with people — watched the quiet way David handled a complaint, the patient way he explained a tool's purpose, the careful way he extended credit to a man he knew might never pay — and he filed it all away, the way the Isaak men had always filed things away: silently, completely, for later use.

The store smelled like metal and oil and sawdust. The shelves were stacked with nails, hinges, rope, chain, plowshares, axe heads, saw blades, stove parts — the hundred small iron necessities that held a prairie life together. The bell above the door rang all day.

---

**The Trouble with Credit**

The settlers who walked through that door were, almost without exception, desperately poor.

They were German-Russians and Scandinavians and Americans, all of them homesteading on the same empty prairie, all of them one bad harvest from ruin. They needed tools. They needed supplies. They did not have cash.

*Each time they purchased something, it would be said, "Of course, you will have to write this up on a charge."*

Gottlieb knew this voice. He had been this voice — the voice of a man who needs something he cannot pay for, who promises to settle up after the harvest, who means it sincerely and cannot always keep the promise. He had bought seed on credit in Sophiental. He had borrowed 330 rubles from his neighbors in 1873\. He understood the arithmetic of need.

But understanding it did not make it profitable.

*After three or four years, we usually received the information that this or that customer had moved away from the area. Because of this, many of the notes we had received came up with no value.*

They moved away. That was the cruelest part — not that the settlers couldn't pay, but that they left. They vanished into the vast American interior the way people vanished on the frontier, heading further west or further north, chasing the next homestead, the next quarter section, the next promise of rain, and the debts they left behind evaporated like morning dew on the prairie grass. The ledger filled with names of men who no longer lived within a hundred miles. The notes became wallpaper.

David did not complain. He was not the complaining kind. But Hanna watched the ledger thicken with unpayable debts, and she said what David would not: this cannot go on.

The losses mounted. The partnership strained. Sailer left for Mannhaven, North Dakota, where he started a good business of his own — hardware, apparently, because Sailer had learned enough behind this counter to know what he was doing. Solomon went into business with a man named Raab: Eureka Implements and Feed. The partnership dissolved, and when the accounts were settled, Gottlieb found himself holding the shortest straw.

*Neither of the two, nor I could demand the return of our investment. The two did however receive a few notes receivable and a small portion of their partnership share, but I had the loss of even that, and left the business empty handed.*

Empty handed. After sixty years of work — after Kulm and Sophiental and Akkerman and Parkston and Hebron and Mercer County and the Atlantic Ocean — Gottlieb Isaak walked out of a hardware store on the main street of Eureka, South Dakota, with nothing.

David kept the store. He would run it alone for the rest of his life — quietly, modestly, without partners or investors or anyone else's money at risk. The hardware store was David's, and he managed it with the careful, unshowy competence that defined everything he did. It never made him rich. It kept him indoors. It did not depend on the rain. That was enough.

---

**Wilhelmina — January 9, 1899**

Not everything that broke could be mended.

Wilhelmina was twelve. She was the quiet one — the girl who had sat in the corner of the wagon during the crossing, who had carried her wooden doll from Bessarabia to Dakota without ever being asked to give it up. She had been born in Mercer County in 1886, the last of Gottlieb and Louisa's children to be born in the Dakotas, and she had grown into a serious, watchful girl who looked like her mother and said even less.

She died on January 9, 1899, in Eureka. She was twelve years old. The cause is not recorded. The autobiography does not mention her death at all — an absence more eloquent than any words could be. Gottlieb, who wrote about crop failures and court cases and the price of swine, could not bring himself to write about the death of his youngest daughter. He left the page blank, and the blankness says everything.

Louisa had buried a son before — Simon, in Kulm, forty years earlier. She knew what this was. She knew the weight and the shape of it. But knowing did not make it lighter, and the house in Eureka was quieter after January, and Louisa was quieter too, and Gottlieb noticed and said nothing, because there was nothing to say.

---

**A Peaceful Life**

Gottlieb left the hardware store with nothing except the house. He was in his sixties, too old for the counter, too old for the field, too stubborn to admit either of those things but too honest to deny them.

He bought a smaller lot and a smaller house, on which he paid interest. And then something happened that had never happened to Gottlieb Isaak before: he became a landlord. The larger house — the one he'd bought when the family first moved to Eureka — he rented out. The income was steady, modest, and more reliable than anything he had ever earned from farming or shopkeeping.

*We had, thank God, a more tranquil life than we had while in business. Too, from the larger house we had a steady rent income, which took better care of our needs than did the business.*

A more tranquil life. The sentence is almost startling in the context of Gottlieb's autobiography, which up to this point has been a catalog of crop failures, court battles, forced moves, and dissolved partnerships. Tranquility was not something the Isaak family specialized in. But here, finally, in a small house in Eureka, with rent checks arriving on schedule and no inventory to manage and no judges to face and no rain to pray for, Gottlieb and Louisa found something approaching peace.

The children took care of the parents. The Bruntschs sent money. The Sailers visited. David and Hanna lived close enough that Louisa could walk to their house and hold her grandchildren and sit in the kitchen and drink coffee and say nothing for an hour, which was her preferred form of conversation. This was what family was for. This was what all the moving and the breaking and the building had been about — not the land, not the store, not the quarter section or the contract or the homestead, but this: the people around the table, the children who came back, the grandchildren who climbed into your lap without asking permission.

---

**The Golden Wedding — December 17, 1904**

Fifty years.

They had exchanged vows in the prayer house at Kulm, Bessarabia, on December 17, 1854, when she was seventeen and he was twenty and the steppe wind blew through the cracks in the walls. They had farmed rented sand at Sophiental and fought the Akkerman court and crossed an ocean and broken sod in Dakota and survived every winter and every drought and every broken promise the world had offered. And they were still here. Still married. Still together.

The Golden Wedding Celebration was held at the Evangelical Lutheran Zion Church — David's church, the congregation he had helped to found. It was the first fiftieth anniversary celebration ever held in Eureka, and the entire town came. The pastor, Reverend H. Reinhardt, presided. The pews were full. The hymns were the old hymns — *Ein feste Burg*, *Nun danket alle Gott* — the same words in the same German, reaching all the way back to the prayer house in Kulm.

The couple was presented with a new Bible.

The Isaak family had been carrying Bibles across Europe and across oceans for 144 years. The Bibles wore out. The pages thinned and tore. The bindings cracked. But the words did not change, and the family did not stop reading them, and now, at the end of a long life in a small town in South Dakota, the church placed a new Bible in Gottlieb's hands, and the old man held it the way he held everything that mattered — carefully, with rough hands, as though it might break.

It would not break. It would outlast him. And inside its cover, ten years later, he would write the story of his life.

---

**May 1, 1905**

Louisa had been in pain for years.

Gottlieb did not describe the illness — only that it had caused her suffering for a long time, and that it had finally become almost intolerable, and that she was confined to her bed. The woman who had walked beside wagons across the Bessarabian steppe, who had crossed the Atlantic with nine children, who had broken sod and planted *Baschtam* and raised eleven children across two countries and half a dozen relocations, was dying in a bed in Eureka, and there was nothing anyone could do except sit with her and pray.

David sat with her. He came every day from the store — closed early some afternoons, left Hanna minding the counter — and he sat by his mother's bed and held her hand and read to her from the German Bible, the one the church had given them five months earlier at the Golden Wedding. He read the Psalms because they were her favorites, and because the Psalms are what you read when someone is dying and you do not know what else to do.

*In 1905, our loving God released her from her suffering on the first of May.*

She was sixty-seven. She had been married to Gottlieb for fifty years and four months. She had borne him eleven children and buried two of them. She had followed him from Kulm to Sophiental to Parkston to Hebron to Mercer County to Eureka without — so far as the record shows — ever once refusing to go.

Gottlieb buried her in Eureka. He sold the small house. He paid the debts.

And then, at the age of seventy, alone for the first time since he was twenty years old, he began what he called, with the dry humor of a man who had moved too many times already, *the beloved journeys.*

---

**The Beloved Journeys**

*Every one of the children wanted me to live with them.*

He moved from child to child — Solomon, David, Magdalena and Sailer, Antonie and Bruntsch, Emanuel — staying for a while in each home, a guest in the houses his children had built. Each household welcomed him. Each household tried. But Gottlieb Isaak had spent his entire life building things — farms, houses, partnerships, communities — and living in someone else's house was not something he knew how to do. He was restless. He was grateful and restless, which is the most difficult combination, because you cannot complain about kindness without seeming ungrateful, and you cannot be ungrateful without betraying the people you love most.

Emanuel, the youngest, the American, had moved to Oshkosh, Wisconsin — far from the Dakotas, far from the German-Russian orbit. Gottlieb stayed with him for a time, and the Wisconsin damp hit his joints like a hammer. *I was struck with rheumatism so severely that living became a bitter task.* He left. He regretted it. *I should never have left Emanuel, but I lacked the patience to remain.*

It is the most personal, most vulnerable line in the entire autobiography — an old man admitting that his own stubbornness cost him the comfort of his youngest son's home. Gottlieb, who had fought judges and survived oceans and broken sod on three continents, could not sit still in a warm house in Wisconsin. He lacked the patience. He had always lacked the patience. It was the engine that had driven him from Kulm to Sophiental to America, and now it drove him out of his son's kitchen and back to South Dakota, back to the dry air and the wind and the flat country where his bones ached less and his pride ached more.

He returned to Eureka on October 28, 1913\.

---

**The Midnight Prayer — November 13, 1913**

Two weeks after his return, Gottlieb could not sleep.

The rheumatism was worse. The pain had followed him from Wisconsin, settling into his hips and his knees and the small bones of his hands — the hands that had gripped plows and contracts and Bibles and the rail of the *Mosel* and the rough wood of a sod house wall and everything else a life could put in a man's hands — and now those hands would not close without burning, and the burning would not stop, and the night was long and empty and full of the particular silence of a house where no one else is sleeping because no one else lives there.

At midnight, Gottlieb left his bed for a rocking chair.

He sat in the dark. He did not light a lamp. He folded his hands — slowly, painfully, the knuckles swollen and stiff — and he spoke aloud, in German, to the God he had been speaking to since boyhood:

*"I have been living in the belief that your word is the truth, and this I firmly believe. You have said, 'all that you shall ask for, in my name, I will grant you' — this promise is true. This you can prove to me by removing my pain."*

It was not a prayer of supplication. It was a negotiation. It was the voice of a man who had read too many contracts and trusted too few promises but who had, despite everything, never stopped believing that somewhere, somehow, there was a promise that would be kept. He was not begging. He was holding God to His word, the way he had tried to hold the Akkerman court to its contract, the way he had held the county commissioners to their budgets, the way he had held every authority in his life to the terms of the deal.

He went to bed. He fell asleep. He woke in the morning and the pain was gone.

It did not come back. According to Pastor Bruntsch, who recorded the event in a footnote to the autobiography, Gottlieb testified that *from that night on, the trusting God and Savior removed the rheumatic pains which he never thereafter experienced.*

Whether you believe this or not — whether you read it as miracle or coincidence or the psychosomatic relief of a man who finally gave himself permission to stop fighting — is up to you. Gottlieb believed it. He believed it completely, unshakably, with the same stubborn certainty he brought to everything else in his life. He had asked. He had been answered. That was the end of it.

---

**The Autobiography — March 17, 1914**

Four months after the midnight prayer, Gottlieb sat down with a pen and the Bible the church had given him at his Golden Wedding and began to write.

He was eighty years old. His handwriting was shaky — the German script cramped and uneven, the letters pressed hard into the page as though he were trying to anchor the words against the wind. He wrote in the margins of the Bible, on the blank pages at the front and the back, filling every available inch of space with the story of his life.

He did not write for publication. He did not write for posterity. He wrote for his children and their children — for David behind the counter in Eureka, for Solomon and his farms, for Magdalena who had married Adam Sailer and been widowed and was raising her children alone, for Antonie and Pastor Bruntsch, for Emanuel in Wisconsin, for the grandchildren who would never know what Sophiental looked like or what it meant to stand in a courtroom in Akkerman and be told how to behave.

He wrote about Kulm and the land shortage. He wrote about Sophiental and the sandy soil and the rent. He wrote about the crop failure and the contract and the judges. He wrote about the decree and the emigration and the crossing. He wrote about Parkston and the black soil and the first garden and the ox team and the debt paid off in American dollars. He wrote about Hebron and the hills and the rain that did not come. He wrote about Mercer County and the hardware store and the settlers who vanished with their debts. He wrote about Louisa.

He did not write about Wilhelmina.

The autobiography ends abruptly — mid-sentence, almost, as though the pen simply stopped. The last dated entry is March 17, 1914\. He was eighty. He had seven more years to live. The world was about to go to war — a war that would reach across the ocean and pull his grandson Albert into a uniform and send him to France, the same France that had marched through Soest in 1760, the same France that Napoleon had led across the steppe that Gottlieb's grandfather had walked to reach Bessarabia. The circles were closing, but Gottlieb did not know that. He only knew that the story was told, and that the Bible held it, and that someone — someday — would open the book and read what he had written and understand what it had cost.

He closed the Bible. He set down the pen. The wind blew outside the window, the same wind that had blown in Kulm and on the *Mosel* and across the Parkston prairie and through every place the Isaaks had ever been.

The story was written. The rest was living.

## Chapter Nine

# The Corporal

---

*On April 6, 1917, the United States declared war on Germany. Within six weeks, Congress passed the Selective Service Act, requiring every man between twenty-one and thirty-one to register for the draft. In Eureka, South Dakota — a town where seventy percent of the population spoke German, where the church services were in German, where the store signs and the newspapers and the kitchen conversations and the prayers before supper were all in German — the declaration landed like an artillery shell in a wheat field.*

*Overnight, the language became the accusation. Sauerkraut became "liberty cabbage." Libraries pulled German books from their shelves. The state Council of Defense banned German in public gatherings, including church. In Collinsville, Illinois, a man was lynched for having a German accent.*

*Albert Friedrich Isaak was twenty-nine years old. He ran the family store. He played clarinet in the Eureka band. He spoke English without an accent and German without apology. And on June 5, 1917, he walked to the McPherson County courthouse to put his name on a piece of paper that might send him to France to kill men who spoke the same language his grandfather had carried across an ocean.*

---

**The Store**

The sign above the door read **Isaaks 5&10¢ Store**. It had been David's hardware business once — plowshares and axe heads, the iron necessities of the frontier — but Eureka was not a frontier anymore, and the store had changed with it. Thread, buttons, penny candy, school supplies, kitchen utensils: the small commerce of a settled town.

Albert and his brother Ed ran it now. Albert was the older brother by two years — five feet eleven, brown-haired, blue-eyed, reserved in the way of a man who does his thinking before he opens his mouth. Ed was shorter, quicker to laugh, gifted with the easy warmth that makes customers feel remembered. Albert handled the stock and the ordering. Ed handled the people. Between them, the store ran.

Their father David sat behind the register most mornings, on a wooden stool, watching his sons work. He nodded when they did something right and said nothing when they did something wrong. He was fifty-seven and looked older. His heart had been failing since a severe illness years earlier — a slow, interior collapse that showed itself in shortness of breath and grey mornings and the way he sometimes gripped the counter with both hands, as though the floor might tilt. The customers noticed. Nobody mentioned it.

Their mother Hanna kept the books — precise, unforgiving, balanced to the penny. She had buried two children: Richard, who lived fifty-eight days in the winter of 1892, and Anna, who died at three in the summer of 1900\. Albert had been thirteen when Anna died. He had carried her on his shoulders through the store, letting her grab penny candy from the jars on the top shelf, and then one July morning she was gone and the jars were still there and everything was the same except the weight on his shoulders, which he would never feel again.

Their older sister Josie managed the household — the cooking, the cleaning, the daily machinery of a family that was held together by a woman who did not ask for thanks and did not receive it.

On Sunday evenings, after the store closed and the dishes were cleared, Albert played clarinet. He played in the Eureka band with his cousins — Hank, Walter, Edwin, Otto — Solomon's boys, the next generation of Isaaks, a loose assembly of German-Russian musicians who performed at church suppers and harvest dances and the occasional wedding. Otto was seventeen, the youngest, and he played with the unself-conscious joy of a boy who has not yet learned that the world has teeth. Albert was the steady one, the man who held the tempo and kept the others from rushing. Music was the only thing he did that was not practical, and perhaps that was why he loved it — because it existed purely for the sake of something beautiful, and beauty was a thing the Isaaks had always carried with them but rarely spoke about.

---

**June 5, 1917**

The night before Registration Day, Albert and Ed sat in their mother's kitchen.

David was upstairs. He was having a bad month — the breathlessness worse, the pallor deepening. Josie brought him broth and read to him from the family Bible.

Hanna was at the stove, not cooking but gripping the cast-iron rim, the way she always did when something needed to be said.

"What do you think they'll ask?" Ed said. Nobody knew exactly. The questions had not been published.

"They'll ask about dependents," Albert said. "And whether we have a reason not to go."

They looked at their mother. Hanna set down the spoon.

"Your father is sick. He cannot work. I have no income that does not come through you."

In the morning, they walked to the courthouse together. Josie watched them go from the porch.

The board was set up inside — a long table, a registrar, a line of men stretching out the door. Every eligible man in McPherson County, standing in work clothes, holding his hat, waiting to sit across from a stranger who would ask him twelve questions and write the answers on a card.

Ed sat down first. The registrar asked the questions aloud, one by one, and wrote the answers in his own hand. Name. Address. Date of birth. Occupation. Then: dependents?

"Mother and sickly father," Ed said.

Grounds for exemption? Ed explained that he was the buyer and general manager of a department store, that it was nearly impossible for him to get away. Also, his eyes — he was having trouble with his eyes.

The registrar wrote it down. Ed signed the card and stood.

Albert sat down. The same questions.

Dependents? "Mother and sickly father."

Exemption? He explained that he was necessary to manage the farm and store business. He would like to explain it to the board in person, if they'd allow it.

The registrar looked at the two brothers — same surname, same address, same sickly father — and wrote his assessment in the margin of both cards:

*Greater part of excuses questionable.*

Six weeks later, on July 20, the Secretary of War drew numbers from a glass bowl in Washington. Albert's number came up. Ed's did not.

"I'll keep the store," Ed said.

"You'll keep Mother," Albert said. "And Father."

---

**Camp Funston**

No Isaak, in any generation, had ever served in a military. The Isaaks were peaceful people — Lutherans who believed killing was wrong, and who had arranged their entire history around that conviction. Every generation, when a government demanded they bear arms, had chosen to leave instead. Brandenburg. Russia. Across the Atlantic.

But Albert was an American. This was the last country.

He was sent to Camp Funston, at Fort Riley, Kansas, where the 340th Machine Gun Battalion was being organized as part of the 89th Division. The 340th drew its men from South Dakota — which meant that many of Albert's fellow soldiers were German-Russians like himself, boys from Eureka and Hosmer and Leola who spoke German at home and carried surnames the drill instructors mangled. This was a comfort, but not a shield. The anti-German poison that had sickened civilian life was worse in the barracks — concentrated, inescapable, delivered by men from other outfits who had nothing to do after drill except find someone to hate. The South Dakota boys were called Kaiser. Called Hun. A man from Kansas urinated on a private's bunk because the private's name was Schreiber. A drill sergeant asked Albert, in front of the company, whether he planned to shoot the wrong way when they got to France.

Albert said nothing. He ran the drills. He learned the Hotchkiss M1914 — a French-made heavy machine gun, fifty-two pounds without the tripod, strip-fed, air-cooled, eight rounds per second. He learned to strip it and reassemble it in the dark, by feel alone. He was promoted to corporal — squad leader for a machine gun crew — because the Army needed men who could stay calm when the world was exploding, and Albert, at twenty-nine, had a decade of life on most of the boys in his battalion, and a steadiness that the younger men recognized and leaned on even when they could not have explained why.

He brought his clarinet. He played it in the barracks at night, after lights-out, quietly, for the men who could not sleep. German hymns and American folk songs and things he made up as he went. The music traveled through the thin wooden walls and reached the men in the next building, who said nothing about it and were grateful.

---

**France**

On June 4, 1918, Corporal Albert Isaak boarded the SS *Khyber* in New York Harbor. He stood on the deck and saw the Statue of Liberty on her island. His grandfather Gottlieb had not seen her — she hadn't been built when the family arrived in 1878\. Albert saw her now, growing smaller as the ship headed for the Atlantic.

His family had come *from* Europe, fleeing war. Now he was going back to fight one. Against men who spoke the language he had grown up speaking. Against boys who looked like his cousin Otto.

He brought the clarinet.

The 89th Division landed in France and was sent to the Lucey Sector in Lorraine for training under French supervision. The French officers who received them were polite and exhausted — hollow-eyed men in faded uniforms who had been fighting this war for four years, who had survived Verdun and the Somme and the mutinies of 1917\. They regarded the Americans with a courtesy that was also a kind of pity. The Americans were strong and well-fed and eager. They had never smelled a trench in summer. They had never heard the sound a man makes when a shell fragment enters his body. They had not yet learned the thing that four years on the Western Front had taught every soldier who survived it: that bravery is not the thing you need. The thing you need is endurance, and endurance is not something you can train. It is something you discover in yourself at three in the morning in a shell crater full of rainwater when the man beside you has stopped breathing and the barrage will not stop.

Then came the fighting.

---

**Saint-Mihiel — September 12, 1918**

A German salient that had bulged into Allied lines for four years. Albert's crew moved forward before dawn — the Hotchkiss on one man's back, the tripod on another's, ammunition strips in canvas bags. Albert positioned the gun behind a low ridge, mapped the field of fire, and waited.

The barrage started at one in the morning. Three thousand guns opened fire along a forty-mile front. The ground dissolved. The sky turned white — not the white of daylight but the white of sustained explosion, a strobing, percussive whiteness that seared the afterimage into your eyes so that when you closed them the landscape was still there, inverted, pulsing. The sound was beyond sound. It was a physical pressure — a wall of concussion that compressed the air in your lungs and turned your heartbeat into something you could feel in your teeth. The man next to Albert pressed his face into the mud and screamed, and the scream was inaudible, and Albert put his hand on the man's shoulder and held it there, because that was all the barrage allowed.

Then the infantry went forward, and Albert gave the order, and the Hotchkiss hammered to life.

The salient fell in two days. Thirteen thousand German prisoners.

Sometime after Saint-Mihiel, Albert was on guard duty at the American camp. It was night. He had orders to shoot on sight.

He heard movement in the dark — not the random noise of the forest but the deliberate, careful movement of a man trying not to be heard. Albert raised his rifle. He waited. The shape materialized out of the shadows: a boy, maybe seventeen, in a German uniform, moving through the American perimeter with the tense, crouching gait of someone who was not supposed to be there.

A spy. Or a saboteur — someone sent to scout the camp's layout, or worse, to set charges. Albert's orders were clear. Shoot on sight.

But Albert looked at this boy. Seventeen. The same age as his cousin Otto, his bandmate, the kid who played with unself-conscious joy. This was someone's Otto. Someone's son. Someone who, before governments drew their lines and issued their orders, had been not an enemy but a brother — a German, like Albert's grandfather, like Albert's father, like every Isaak since Soest.

He could not do it.

He spoke to the boy in German — sharply, the way you speak to a child you've caught doing something dangerous. *Raus. Verschwinde. Sofort.* Get out. Disappear. Now. And never come back.

The boy froze. He had not expected German from an American soldier. He stared at Albert with the wide, uncomprehending eyes of a seventeen-year-old who has just been handed his life by a man who had every right and every order to take it. Then he turned and ran — back into the darkness, back through the perimeter, back into the war — and Albert stood alone in the night with his rifle unfired and his orders broken and the absolute certainty that he had done the only thing a man of his blood and his faith could do.

He never reported it. He never spoke of it publicly. He carried it the way the Isaaks carried everything — inward, silent, certain.

---

**The Argonne**

Two weeks after Saint-Mihiel, the 89th Division was redeployed to the Meuse-Argonne.

It was the worst of the war. Dense forest, fortified ridges, interlocking machine gun fire, artillery registered on every road. The offensive lasted forty-seven days. Over 1.2 million Americans fought in it. Twenty-six thousand were killed. A hundred and twenty thousand were wounded or gassed.

And for Albert — a man whose family had spoken German for two hundred years, whose grandfather had grown up among Germans in Bessarabia, whose cousins still wrote letters in German script — the Argonne was a particular kind of hell. The men on the other side of the wire spoke his language. The commands shouted across no-man's-land were commands he could understand. The curses, the prayers, the cries of the wounded — he heard them in German, and he understood every word, and each word was a knife.

Albert's crew set up in shell craters and ruined farmhouses, broke down the gun and hauled it forward through knee-deep mud, set it up, laid down fire, moved again. The rain never stopped. The dead lay where they fell.

Sometime in late September or early October, the gas came.

It came at night, which was the worst way, because you couldn't see it. Mustard gas was heavier than air, and it settled into the low places — the shell craters, the trenches, the hollows between ridges where men sheltered from artillery. It smelled like garlic, or like horseradish, or like something burning that should not be burning. By the time you smelled it, it was already on you.

Albert was in a hollow. He may have been sleeping, or trying to sleep, or lying on his back staring at nothing the way soldiers stare when they have been awake too long. The gas found him the way water finds a drain — silently, invisibly, pooling around his body in the dark. He didn't know it was there until his eyes began to burn.

Then his skin began to burn.

Then his throat closed.

The gas did not kill quickly. That was the cruelty of it. Mustard gas was not designed to kill — it was designed to incapacitate, to overwhelm, to fill hospitals and consume resources and break the will of men who could endure bullets and shrapnel but could not endure the feeling of their own skin blistering and their own lungs filling with fluid and their own eyes swelling shut while they clawed at their face in the dark. The blisters came within hours — rising on his arms, his neck, the soft skin of his armpits and groin, everywhere the gas had touched, which was everywhere, because the gas was everywhere. His eyes streamed and swelled. His throat burned as though he had swallowed a coal. And his lungs — his lungs felt as though someone had poured acid into them and then asked him to breathe.

He was pulled from the line. Stretcher bearers carried him to a field hospital behind the front, where he lay on a cot in a long tent filled with gassed men — dozens of them, rows of cots, the air thick with the sound of labored breathing and the sharp, chemical smell of the agents still seeping from their skin and clothes. His eyes were bandaged. His blisters were dressed. The nurses moved between the cots with saline and cotton and the particular gentleness of women who have seen enough suffering to know that sometimes the most you can do is be present while it happens.

---

**The Hospital**

In the hospital, Albert learned two things.

The first was that his father was dead.

David had died on December 21, 1917 — nine months earlier, while Albert was still training at Camp Funston. The heart that had been failing him for years had given out on a Friday morning, two days before Christmas. *Death found him in midst of duty.* The funeral was held on Sunday at the Lutheran church David had helped to found. Hanna sat in the front row with Josie and Ed. Albert's chair was empty.

Hanna had written to Albert in France. The letter had followed him from camp to camp, from sector to sector, through the chaos of a war that moved men like chess pieces and delivered mail like an afterthought. It reached him in the hospital. She told him his father was gone. She told him to read Psalm 91\. She told him to cling to the Lord.

*He that dwelleth in the secret place of the most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty. Thou shalt not be afraid for the terror by night; nor for the arrow that flieth by day; nor for the pestilence that walketh in darkness.*

Albert lay on his cot with his eyes bandaged and his lungs scarred and his father nine months dead and read his mother's letter with his fingers, because he could not see the words, and the words did not need to be seen because Psalm 91 was a psalm he already knew, the way he knew the Lord's Prayer and the hymns and the sound of the clarinet and the smell of the store and his mother's voice — all the things that belonged to the world before the war, the world that was still there, waiting for him, if his lungs would let him breathe long enough to reach it.

The second thing he learned was the Spanish flu.

The field hospital was full of it. Men who had survived bullets and shrapnel and gas were dying of influenza — choking on their own fluids, turning blue, filling the cots that the gassed men had just vacated. The flu had been burning through the American Expeditionary Forces since September, filling hospitals faster than the fighting could empty them. It would kill forty-five thousand American servicemen — more than the combat did. Worldwide, fifty million people would die before it burned itself out, twice the war's total. It was the deadliest disease in human history, and it was in the next cot.

Albert survived. His lungs healed enough to breathe, though the tissue was scarred in a way that would never fully repair — the emphysema that would shadow his breathing for the rest of his life.

The armistice came on November 11\. The guns stopped. The silence that followed was louder than the barrage had been.

---

**Home**

Albert did not go home with the 340th — the battalion would not return until May 1919\. He was discharged from the hospital and assigned to a casual company at Saint-Aignan for transport home. On January 7, 1919, he boarded the USS *Pueblo* at Brest. Thirteen days later, the ship entered New York Harbor.

The Statue of Liberty. He had seen her shrinking behind the *Khyber* going out. Now she was growing, coming home.

On February 14, 1919, Corporal Albert Friedrich Isaak was discharged from the United States Army.

He was thirty-one. He was home. He still played the clarinet.

---

**Eva**

His cousin Hank Isaak called one day.

Hank was Albert's age, Solomon's eldest son, the boy who had played beside Albert in the Eureka band for years — dependable, gregarious, the kind of man who knew everyone and who could not leave a friend unmatched. He wanted to organize a sleigh ride party. Albert said he'd come but didn't have a partner.

"Call the new girl at the bank," Hank said. "Eva Roesch. She might like to get acquainted with the bunch."

Albert called. She said she'd be glad to go.

Eva Roesch was nineteen years old — five foot two, dark-haired, clear-eyed, one of nine children, raised by her grandparents. She was working as a clerk at a bank in McPherson County, and she carried herself with the direct, unsentimental composure of a woman who had been taking care of herself for a long time and did not intend to stop. Her family was German-Russian — the same Black Sea origins, the same wheat fields, the same Lutheran faith.

The sleigh ride was in winter — bundled in blankets and buffalo robes, the horses' breath steaming in the air, a group of friends crowded into sleighs with the particular giddiness that comes from being young and cold and close to someone whose shoulder you can feel through three layers of wool.

She became his partner for the sleigh ride. She became his partner for life.

Eva's family encouraged the match. Albert was a veteran. He had a business. He was thirty-two, but the Roesches understood that a man who has been tested the way Albert had been tested and has come through it still steady is not a risk but a certainty.

On November 11, 1919 — the first anniversary of the armistice, the day the world had stopped killing itself — Albert Friedrich Isaak married Eva Roesch. He chose the date the way he chose everything: quietly, deliberately, and for reasons he did not explain to anyone but that the people who knew him understood without being told.

---

On January 20, 1921, Albert's grandfather Gottlieb died in Tripp, South Dakota, at eighty-six. The old man who had crossed an ocean, fought judges, broken sod, and prayed away his rheumatism at midnight was gone.

Four months later, on May 8, Albert and Eva's first daughter was born. They named her Alice Estelle Isaak.

---

**Autumn 1926**

Alice was five and a half, and she had opinions about everything.

She had her father's blue eyes and her mother's stubbornness, and she moved through the world with the purposeful velocity of a child who knows that adults will always be a step behind.

That October, she was playing near the schoolhouse with two neighbor children when she stopped and stared.

A man was walking along the road at the edge of the schoolyard. He was tall and lean, with dark skin and black hair worn in two long braids that fell past his shoulders. He wore a cotton work shirt and denim trousers, but over the shirt he had a vest of tanned hide worked with geometric beadwork in red and blue and white — patient, intricate handwork that spoke a language older than English or German. His face was deeply lined by wind and weather, and he walked with the unhurried stride of a man who knows exactly where he is.

Alice had heard about Indians. Every child in McPherson County had — the stories at school, the names: Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, Red Cloud. The Standing Rock Reservation was less than fifty miles west across the Missouri. But she had never seen one. The German families in Eureka had almost no contact with the people whose land they were farming — a silence so old it had become invisible.

The man glanced at the schoolyard. His eyes met Alice's.

She waved.

She was five. She waved at everyone. Nobody had taught her yet that some people were supposed to be invisible.

The man paused. Something shifted in his face — not a smile, but a softening, the faintest acknowledgment that a child's wave reaches across every wall ever built between strangers. He raised one hand. A single, slow, open-palmed gesture. Then he turned and walked on.

That evening at supper, Alice announced that she had waved at an Indian and he had waved back.

Albert set down his fork. "You don't wave at strangers, Alice."

"He wasn't a stranger. He waved back."

"He was a stranger. You don't know him."

"But he was *nice*."

Albert picked up his fork and said nothing. Eva, who noticed things that Albert did not, saw Alice's face — the confusion, the faint hurt of being corrected for kindness — and later that night, after the girls were in bed, she said quietly: "She was just being polite."

Albert said nothing. He went to bed. The wind blew outside.

---

**Edith Allegra — July 2, 1927**

The following summer, in Aberdeen, a second daughter arrived. She had blonde hair — golden, fine, the color of wheat in August — and they named her Edith Allegra Isaak.

The names came from a poem.

Albert had been called Kaiser at Camp Funston. He had been mocked for his heritage. He had fought a war against men who spoke his grandfather's language. He had come home to a country that had banned his mother tongue from the church pulpit. And when it came time to name his daughters, he reached not for the old family names — not for Louisa or Magdalena or Regina — but for the most American poem he knew.

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow. *The Children's Hour*:

 

From my study I see in the lamplight,  Descending the broad hall stair, Grave Alice, and laughing Allegra,  And Edith with golden hair.

 

His first-born was Alice. His second was Edith Allegra — both of Longfellow's other daughters pressed into a single name, as though he could not bear to leave either one out. And when the baby arrived with golden hair, the poem completed itself.

He did not teach Alice or Edith to speak German. He broke the chain — deliberately, permanently — so that his daughters would never hear what he had heard. The girls called their German nanny Tante Freda, and Tante Freda was the last thread of the old language in their daily lives. When Tante Freda married and moved away, little Edith was heartbroken. She cried for days. It was her first loss, and she did not yet understand that people leave — that the ones you love can be there one morning and gone the next, and that this is the way of the world and always has been.

---

**The Roaring Twenties**

But the world, for once, was cooperating.

The 1920s arrived in Eureka like a season that had been delayed. Wheat prices climbed. The railroad ran full. New buildings went up on Main Street — a pharmacy, a movie theater, a second barbershop. Automobiles appeared on roads that had known only wagons. Electricity reached houses that had been lit by kerosene. The country was becoming modern so fast it could barely keep up with itself, and the German-Russian families of McPherson County — who had known poverty for five generations and scarcity for longer than that — looked at the abundance and blinked, and then they smiled, and then they spent.

Albert and Eva's store thrived. The 5&10¢ store was exactly the right business for the moment — small luxuries, affordable goods, the things people buy when they have a little money and the confidence that more is coming. Hair ribbons and costume jewelry. School supplies and kitchen gadgets. The candy jars were always full. Eva handled the customers with the instinct of a woman who knew what people wanted before they asked. Albert handled the ordering and the books, and on Sundays they went to church, and in the evenings the cousins gathered and the music started — Hank and Walter and Edwin, and Albert with his clarinet, the Eureka band playing on.

Ed married and had a son — Melvin, who would grow up alongside Alice and Edith in the small orbit of German-Russian Eureka. Josie remained unmarried, remained steady. Hanna, widowed since 1917, lived quietly in McPherson County, sustained by her children and her faith.

Albert's lungs were not right. They would never be right — the emphysema from the gas was a permanent tenant, a tightness, a ceiling on every deep breath. He did not complain. But Eva noticed the way he paused at the top of the stairs, the way the cough never went away — not in spring, not in summer, not ever — and she said nothing, because Eva understood that some things are managed and not cured, and that the managing is its own kind of love.

On a summer evening in 1928, the family sat on the porch of their house in Eureka. Alice was seven. Edith was one, balanced on Eva's hip, pulling at her mother's hair and laughing. The store was doing well. The wheat was high. The band was playing Saturday night. The candy jars were full — the same jars that had been full when Albert was a boy, the same jars Anna had reached for from his shoulders, the same jars that had stood on the top shelf through everything: the deaths, the war, the gas, the silence.

The Isaaks had been moving for two hundred years — from Brandenburg to Poland, from Poland to Bessarabia, from Bessarabia to Dakota — peaceful people, always seeking peace.

Albert had found it. He was home. The house was full. The wheat was high, and the evening was warm, and the century was young, and nobody on that porch knew what was coming next.

## Chapter Ten

# Tante Frieda

---

*On October 29, 1929, the stock market crashed and the American century stumbled. Banks failed. Factories closed. Bread lines formed in cities that had been building skyscrapers a year earlier. In the farm country of the northern plains, the catastrophe was double: wheat prices, which had been climbing through the twenties, collapsed overnight. A bushel that had sold for a dollar now sold for twenty-five cents — less than it cost to grow. Farmers who had borrowed against their land to buy tractors and seed found themselves owing more than the land was worth.*

*Then the rain stopped.*

*The drought began in 1930 and did not fully break for seven years. Without rain, the crops withered. Without crops, the topsoil — plowed bare of the native grasses that had held it for ten thousand years — turned to powder. The wind took it. Massive dust storms rolled across the Great Plains like brown walls, blackening the sky at noon, burying fence posts, choking cattle, sifting through closed windows and sealed doors until everything inside was coated in fine, gritty dirt. They called it the Dust Bowl. They called it the Dirty Thirties. In South Dakota, no other state lost a greater share of its population.*

*The Isaaks had survived the steppe, survived the Atlantic, survived the sod houses and the blizzards and the failed partnerships and the war. Now they had to survive the wind.*

---

**Eureka, South Dakota — 1932**

Albert and Eva and the girls lived above the store.

The apartment was small — two bedrooms, a kitchen, a sitting room — built directly over the Isaaks 5&10¢ on one of Eureka's modest commercial blocks. The floor shook when the front door opened downstairs. The smell of the store — dust and candy and cardboard and the faint metallic trace of the old hardware days — rose through the floorboards and settled into the furniture and the curtains and the girls' hair. If you grew up above a store, the store was in everything. It was the smell of home.

Eva worked the counter downstairs. Albert handled the ordering and the books. Between them, they kept the doors open, though keeping the doors open in Eureka in 1932 was an act of stubbornness that bordered on faith. Half the storefronts on Main Street had gone dark. The grain elevator was nearly idle. The customers who came in had less money every month, and the ones who had credit were the ones who would never pay, because there was nothing to pay with.

Alice was eleven, growing tall but not yet serious — that would come later. She had joined the Girl Scouts, a handful of Eureka girls in brown uniforms learning knots and first aid and the particular brand of prairie resourcefulness that no Scout handbook covered but that South Dakota demanded. She loved it. The meetings, the projects, the badges, the feeling of belonging to something organized and purposeful in a world that was coming apart at the seams — all of it suited her. In May of 1933, just after her twelfth birthday, Alice stood with her troop for a photograph, and she was smiling. Not the dutiful half-smile of a girl told to hold still, but a real smile — the kind that comes from a twelve-year-old who has found her people and does not yet know that the world will ask her to leave them.

Edith was five. She had begun piano lessons with her aunt Josie — who had the patience, the musical ear, and the understanding that a five-year-old's attention span is approximately the length of one hymn, and that the hymn had better be interesting. Josie sat beside her on the bench and guided her fingers and did not lose her temper when the wrong notes came, which was often, because Edith was five and the piano was enormous and her hands were the size of teacups. But the lessons took. The music took. Something in the child responded to the instrument the way her father responded to the clarinet — not as a skill to be mastered but as a language to be spoken, a way of saying things that words could not reach.

And Tante Frieda was upstairs, mending a dress.

---

**Tante Frieda**

She was a middle-aged German immigrant who had recently arrived in Eureka — a newcomer in a town that had stopped receiving newcomers, a woman with broken English and sturdy hands and the particular gentleness of someone who has crossed an ocean alone and knows what it means to need company.

Eva hired her to care for Edith during the day while Eva worked the store.

The arrangement was simple. Tante Frieda cooked. Tante Frieda cleaned. Tante Frieda washed Edith's hair, mended her dresses, served her goat's milk and homemade bread, and hovered over her during the terrifying bouts of whooping cough and a blood infection that nearly killed her. And in the hours between the crises, the two of them sat together in the small apartment above the store and taught each other language.

Edith taught Tante Frieda English. Tante Frieda taught Edith to sew.

They were an unlikely pair — the five-year-old who chattered without pause and the immigrant woman who struggled with every vowel — but the struggle was the joy. They laughed over the pronunciations. They laughed over the sewing. They laughed over nothing, the way people laugh when they are happy in each other's company and do not need a reason. The apartment filled with the sound of a child's voice and a woman's laughter and the clatter of the sewing machine, and if you stood at the bottom of the stairs and listened, you could hear the two languages braiding together into something that was neither German nor English but was, unmistakably, love.

Then, when Edith was seven, Tante Frieda left.

She did not warn the child. One day she was there — mending a dress, heating the milk, humming a German hymn — and the next day she was gone, moved far away to marry a German widower, and the apartment above the store was silent in a way it had never been silent before.

Edith's grief was enormous. It was the kind of grief that fills a child's entire body, that cannot be reasoned with or consoled. She cried for days. Nobody could comfort her. Tante Frieda's leaving seemed worse than death, because Tante Frieda was still alive — still somewhere in the world, still breathing and sewing and humming — but they were not together, and never would be again.

It was 1934\. The dust was blowing outside, and the woman who had smelled of bread and goat's milk was gone, and Edith Allegra Isaak learned, at seven years old, the lesson that every Isaak eventually learned: that the people you love can leave, and that the leaving does not kill you, but it changes the shape of the room.

---

**Sundays**

But there were Sundays.

Every single Sunday, without exception, the family drove out to Grandma Roesch's farm — Eva's mother, who had been widowed in 1930 but who ran the homestead with the same iron will that had built it. Edith had been coming here since she was a baby. The farm was the fixed point around which the rest of the week rotated, and no drought or depression or death was going to change that.

Getting there was an adventure in itself. Some of the cousins rode with Uncle Ed — Edith's cousin Melvin wedged between them in the back seat — in a car with an incurable muffler problem that turned every trip into a shouting match with physics. The noise was so deafening that conversation was impossible, but everyone tried anyway, arriving hoarse and half-deaf and grateful to both Uncle Ed and Henry Ford as they recalled the horse-and-buggy alternative of former years.

Upon arrival they were greeted by Grandma and the enormous dinner she had prepared.

The big oilcloth-covered table was set for the invasion. The meal was everything Grandma had to offer — mountains of mashed potatoes, loaves of homemade bread, and her specialty: fried chicken. These were birds she had lovingly raised, dutifully decapitated, patiently plucked, bravely eviscerated, and expertly cooked, all as a love-gift for her family.

"Edith, sit. Eat."

"I'm not hungry, Grandma."

"You are hungry. Sit."

Edith sat. She was always hungry. She had said she wasn't because she wanted to go play, but Grandma Roesch was not a woman who entertained the concept of a child who was not hungry, and the argument — if you could call it an argument — lasted approximately two seconds.

Grandma was a strong woman. She had emigrated from Russia, settled on raw prairie, survived winters that killed livestock and summers that blistered paint. She had given birth to fourteen children without anesthetic — nothing but a wooden spoon from the kitchen clenched between her teeth for biting down on when the contractions got unbearable. Her teeth-marks were still on that spoon. Edith knew. She had seen the spoon. It was in the mashed potatoes.

After dinner, the family dispersed into its natural groups.

The aunties stayed at the table with Grandma, discussing the matters that grandmas and aunties are particularly qualified to handle. The uncles went out to the pasture to geld horses, talking in German, their conversation incomprehensible to the children and — even translated — not much of a way to spend a Sunday afternoon.

The girl cousins had better ideas.

They played with dolls — Grandma had a few orphaned ones upstairs, very much in need of love and clothing. She had scraps of cloth, too, from which they would have made dresses, but sewing was a sin on Sunday. And so they wrapped the scraps around each doll and tied these "dresses" in place with string, because tying was not a sin on Sunday. They considered cutting ladies from the Sears and Roebuck catalog to make paper dolls, but using scissors was a sin on Sunday, too.

"What about glue?" one cousin asked.

"Glue isn't sewing."

"But is glue a sin?"

Nobody was sure. They decided not to risk it.

There was never any thought of challenging the regulations. They accepted them the way they accepted gravity — as parental directives, and thereby God-ordained. Edith, for one, was terrified by the thought of deliberately disobeying God. She had been raised Lutheran, confirmed Lutheran, and intended to remain Lutheran, and if God did not want her to use scissors on Sunday, then scissors would wait until Monday, and God would be satisfied, and Edith would be safe.

When the dolls were exhausted, they moved to the real sport of the house: killing flies.

This was a highly competitive event — a genuine test of stealth and skill. The most promising playing field was the entry hall off the kitchen, where the fragrance of Grandma's cooking had drawn prey from every corner of the prairie. Grandma encouraged the activity with the enthusiasm of a woman who had been fighting flies since long before any of these children were born.

"I got four\!" a cousin shouted.

"I got six\!"

"Edith got nine," another cousin reported, with the grudging respect of an athlete acknowledging a superior performance. "With one swat."

Edith held up the flyswatter. Nine flies. Or was it eleven? The historical record is ambiguous, but the glory was not.

The fly-killing got noisy. The aunties, still deep in their *Gemütlichkeit* around the dining table, sent the girls outside.

Outside was better. Outside, they played house — and the building material was the one resource the South Dakota prairie had in abundance: stones. Each girl gathered a supply and laid them on the ground to form the perimeter of a house, with an opening that was the door. Woe to anyone who tried to enter by stepping over the wall instead of using the door. Within each outline, they laid more stones for rooms. And when the houses were built, they straightened them up, tended their imaginary families, and went out through their designated doors to visit the cousins who lived nearby.

For a break from domesticity, they walked out to the pasture to pick wool left by the sheep on the barbed-wire fences. They brought their bundles of fluff back to their stone houses, planning to sew little mattresses for their dolls. On Monday. When it wasn't a sin.

When they came in from play, thirsty and grass-stained, they lined up beside the single source of drinking water: a big pail with a single ladle, shared by everyone. The shared germs from that one ladle probably gave every cousin in McPherson County immunity to every disease in South Dakota, including the ones that killed the cattle.

Grandma and Grandpa's house had one remarkable feature: a big, beautiful indoor bathroom — a genuine upgrade for its day, surely the only one in the area. In reality, the plumbing was notoriously uncooperative. The bathroom did little to shorten the line of relatives waiting by a certain small outbuilding visible from the window. The outbuilding was no showcase. But nothing ever went wrong with its plumbing.

There were always new dogs to meet — an astonishing variety of breeds and origins, few of them named, all of them battling fleas. Farm dogs did not last long. The greatest threat to their survival was the gravel highway past Grandma's house — what the family considered the Interstate, the Turnpike of the Prairie. In the dogs' eyes, it was Death Row.

And then there was the player piano.

It was Grandma's most valuable possession, at least as far as the cousins were concerned. They took turns pumping the pedals with ferocious determination — one cousin per pedal, since the smallest girls lacked the size or strength to manage both. They learned every tune and melodic run: all the World War I songs, all the hits of the twenties, everything that remarkable instrument could produce. The player piano threatened to destroy forever any desire to learn a regular piano, because they knew they could never get that good.

At the end of the day, the family loaded back into the cars and drove home down the gravel road that kicked up dust so thick it shrouded each car until it was out of sight. Edith sat in the back seat and watched the farm grow smaller through the dust and felt the particular satisfaction of a child who has spent a day among people who belong to her and to whom she belongs, and who will be there again next Sunday, and the Sunday after that, and every Sunday until the world ends or the roads close, whichever comes first.

---

**The Drought**

The roads did close.

The drought during those years was beyond anything the German-Russian families had experienced — and these were people whose grandparents had survived the Bessarabian steppe. The fields turned to desert. The government's newly planted windbreak trees became dry sticks, bending to the ground in the storms. The prairies were strewn with the carcasses of cattle. Farmhouses and barns stood empty. People left behind homes and land in which they had invested their lives.

Water became the center of everything, because there was almost none.

The family's only supply was a meager pool at the bottom of their cistern — a few inches of water that was cloudy and smelled wrong and tasted of minerals and rust and the particular desperation of a resource that is running out. It was all they had. Eva filled one sink basin in the morning, and that basin of water was asked to do the work of a river. She washed the dishes in it. She washed her hands in it. She washed the girls' clothes in it. The water turned grey, then brown, then the color of weak coffee, and still she used it, because there was no more and there would be no more until it rained, and it was not going to rain.

Albert, who had drunk from shell craters in the Argonne and breathed mustard gas in a hollow, did not complain about the water. But Eva — Eva, who had grown up on a farm that had a well, who had worked in a bank with clean running water, who had spent the twenties in a store where the faucet worked and the toilet flushed and the girls had baths every night — Eva felt the drought as a personal humiliation. She had married a veteran and a businessman. She had built a life. And now she was washing her daughters' underwear in water that had already washed the breakfast dishes, and hanging them on a line where the dust would coat them before they dried, and bringing them inside still gritty, still faintly damp, still not clean — because nothing was clean, because clean required water and water required rain and rain required God's cooperation and God, for reasons He did not explain, had decided to cooperate with someone else.

The dust storms were like blizzards — walls of brown dirt rolling across the prairie, burying everything. One night, driving home from Grandma's house, a storm blew the car off the road and into a ditch. The wind buried them. Dust sifted inside the car, and it became hard to breathe. An uncle crawled out through a window and made it to a farmhouse, and eventually returned with a farmer and a team of horses that pulled them free.

Then, on a winter day in 1934, Albert and Alice were driving home when they reached the railroad crossing.

Albert saw the train. He hit the brakes. The car did not stop. The tires locked and the wheels slid on the ice as if the road had turned to glass, the car drifting forward with the slow, sickening inevitability of something that is no longer under anyone's control. Albert gripped the wheel. Alice braced her hands against the dashboard. The train filled the windshield — enormous, black, moving — and the car slid into it sideways.

The impact was a sound Alice would never forget — the shriek of steel on steel, the explosion of glass, the way the car folded around them as though it were made of tin. The world spun. Her head struck something hard and the light went white.

Then silence.

Albert opened his eyes. His hands were shaking. He turned to Alice — thirteen years old, slumped in the passenger seat, covered in glass, a gash on her forehead bleeding freely into her eyes. She blinked at him. She was conscious. She was breathing.

"Alice."

"I'm okay," she said, though her ears were ringing and the blood was warm on her face and the word *okay* did not mean anything it usually meant.

The car was destroyed — crumpled against the train's undercarriage, every window shattered, steam rising from the engine into the winter air. The train moved on. Albert and Alice climbed out through what had been the driver's side door. They stood on the frozen road beside the wreckage, their hearts hammering with the wild, electric clarity that comes after the body decides it is not dead, and they looked at what was left of the car, and they looked at each other, and there was nothing to do.

They walked home.

It was miles. The road was icy. Albert's hands would not stop shaking. The blood on Alice's forehead dried in the cold and cracked when she moved her eyebrows.

When they came through the door, Eva saw their faces and went white.

Albert looked at her. He looked at the dust on the windowsill. He looked at his daughter, who was bleeding from the head and standing in the kitchen of an apartment above a store in a town that had no water and no rain and no prospects and had just tried to kill them with a train.

"We're leaving," he said.

---

**Cedar Falls, Iowa — 1935**

"It's a city that has running water," Albert told the family, and the way he said it — with the quiet wonder of a man who had spent five years washing dishes in the same water he washed his socks — made running water sound like a miracle, which, to the Isaaks, it was.

Edith was eight. She had never lived anywhere but the apartment above the store. She had never lived in a house with a yard and a door that opened onto grass instead of a staircase.

Albert wanted a new kind of store. Not the 5&10¢, not the prairie general store with its penny candy and muddy boots. He had an artistic streak, a restlessness, a need to build things nobody else had thought of. He hired a professional architect and designed a women's clothing store that would have been at home on Fifth Avenue. To name it, he held a contest — inviting customers and neighbors to submit ideas, generating buzz for a store that did not yet exist. Someone suggested **Apparel Art**. Albert heard it and knew it was right. They opened it at 217 Main Street in Cedar Falls.

Chrome and glass fixtures. Rose-tinted mirrors that made every woman look better than she felt. Lighted shadow boxes. Imported woods. Rich fabrics. A design so unconventional it was featured in a national magazine. It was Albert Friedrich Isaak's masterpiece — the expression of a man who had grown up behind a counter in a prairie town and who had seen enough ugliness in the Argonne to spend the rest of his life building beautiful things.

Eva worked beside him, as she always had. The customers were different now — they did not wear muddy boots, and nobody spoke German. For the first time, the Isaaks lived in a real house, a mile from the store. The floor did not shake when the front door opened. The water came from a tap.

Albert's inventive mind did not stop at architecture. He had always been a man who noticed problems and could not rest until he had solved them — the same restlessness that had made him bring his violin to France, the same impulse that had driven him to design a store that looked like it belonged on Fifth Avenue instead of Main Street. In 1939, he filed a patent for a clothes hanger he called the **Time Saver** — a hanger shaped so that garments could be slid on and off without stretching the neckline. The patent was approved on February 18, 1941, and he stamped each hanger *Albert F. Isaak, Cedar Falls, Iowa* and marketed them to retailers in Minneapolis and across the Midwest. The idea was so good that he filed a second patent for an improved version in 1944, which was approved in early 1945\. But Albert, as his daughter would say later, "wasn't a pusher" — he was an inventor, not a salesman, a man who could see a problem and build a solution but who lacked the particular shamelessness required to knock on doors and demand that strangers buy it. The Time Saver remained what it was: a small, perfect thing, designed by a man who could not leave a problem unsolved. He was always inventing things. The world was full of problems that had elegant solutions, and Albert could see them all, even if he could not always sell them.

---

**The War — Again**

And then, because the world had not yet finished with the Isaak family, another war came.

On December 7, 1941, Japan bombed Pearl Harbor, and the United States entered the Second World War. For Albert — who had carried a machine gun through the Argonne, who had breathed gas in a hollow, who had let a seventeen-year-old German spy go free because he could not kill a boy who spoke his grandfather's language — the declaration was a door opening onto a room he had spent twenty years trying to leave.

He was fifty-four. He was too old to fight. But the war took the young men the way wars always take them — eighteen-year-olds were required to register, and the seventeen-year-olds, not wanting to be left behind, volunteered. The seniors vanished from Cedar Falls High School first, then the juniors. The hallways thinned. The girls who remained — Edith among them — watched the gaps appear in the rows of desks and said nothing, because what was there to say? The boys were gone. The women took their places — in the factories, on the machines, behind the counters, doing the work that men had always claimed required a man, and doing it fine.

Five months before Pearl Harbor, on April 6, 1941, Edith had been confirmed at St. John Lutheran Church in Cedar Falls. She was thirteen — standing before the congregation in a white dress, reciting the catechism that her great-great-grandfather Gottlieb had memorized in Kulm, answering the same questions in English that he had answered in German, affirming the same faith that had carried the Isaaks from Soest to Brandenburg to Bessarabia to Dakota to Iowa. The language had changed. The faith had not.

By fifteen, she was the church organist.

The organ at St. John was a full pipe organ — multiple keyboards, foot pedals, a rank of pipes that reached toward the ceiling like the interior of a cathedral compressed into a Lutheran church in Iowa. Playing it required hands and feet and the ability to read three staves of music simultaneously while managing the stops and the swell pedals and the particular mechanical temperament of an instrument that had been built to fill a room with sound and did not care whether its operator was fifteen or fifty. Edith found it terrifying and magnificent. She practiced every afternoon, alone in the empty sanctuary, the pipes responding to her touch with a sound so large it vibrated in her sternum, and she thought: *This is what it feels like to hold thunder in your hands.*

She was also a clerk at Apparel Art. She designed the window displays and assembled the outfits on the mannequins. On the side, she wrote music. She was, in short, the kind of teenager who makes other teenagers feel tired just by existing — but she did not think of it that way. She thought of it as normal, because in the Isaak family, everyone worked. Everyone had always worked. Gottlieb had broken sod at fifty-two. Louisa had crossed an ocean with nine children. Hanna had kept the books until the numbers blurred. Work was not something the Isaaks did because they had to. It was something the Isaaks were.

Albert and Eva worked day and night. Part of their motivation was the store. Part of it was the war economy — defense contracts, rationing, the strange prosperity that comes when a country turns its factories to munitions. But surely the deepest part was this: they wanted their daughters to go to college. Albert had never gone past third grade — not for lack of intelligence but because when he was a boy in Eureka, there were no more grades to go to. The rest of his education was learned by living and attempting. He wanted something different for Edith. He wanted her to have the thing no Isaak had ever had.

Edith knew what she wanted to study. Writing — the love of her life, the thing she had been doing since Tante Frieda taught her to hold a pencil, the thing that felt as natural to her as music felt to her father, as farming had felt to Gottlieb, as walking felt to everyone who had ever put one foot in front of the other and refused to stop.

---

**May 1945**

In the spring of 1945, the war ended in Europe.

On May 8 — V-E Day — the same continent that had swallowed Albert's youth and scarred his lungs and sent him home with a clarinet and a silence that lasted the rest of his life finally stopped killing people. Church bells rang in Cedar Falls. People wept in the streets. Albert stood in the doorway of Apparel Art at 217 Main Street and listened to the bells and said nothing, because the end of a war is not the same thing to a man who fought one as it is to a man who read about it in the newspaper.

That same month, Edith graduated from Cedar Falls High School. She was seventeen.

The bells were ringing. The war — her father's war, the war that had shaped him and scarred him and silenced him — was finally over, at least in Europe. And the girl who had grown up above a 5&10¢ store on the South Dakota prairie, who had killed eleven flies with a single swat and played thunder on a pipe organ and lost Tante Frieda and survived a train — that girl was going to college.

She was going to study writing.

## Chapter Eleven

# South

---

*In the summer of 1945, the war in Europe was over but the war in the Pacific was not. American boys were still dying on islands whose names their parents could not pronounce. The atomic bomb had not yet been dropped. The world was holding its breath, waiting for an ending that had not arrived, and in Cedar Falls, Iowa, a seventeen-year-old girl who had just graduated from high school was packing a suitcase for Mexico.*

---

**June 1945**

The train left Cedar Falls in the morning.

Edith Allegra Isaak sat by the window with a book in her lap and a suitcase on the overhead rack and the particular expression of a person who is doing something that nobody in her family has ever done before and is determined not to look nervous about it. She was seventeen. She had never left the Midwest. She had never been further south than the Iowa border, never seen a mountain, never heard Spanish spoken by someone who meant it. And she was traveling alone — by train, through Kansas City, through Texas, through the border at Laredo, and into Mexico — to attend a summer session at Mexico City College, an English-language liberal arts school that had been founded five years earlier for American students living abroad.

It was the kind of thing that did not happen to girls from Cedar Falls. It was the kind of thing that did not happen to the granddaughters of German-Russian immigrants who had grown up above a 5&10¢ store on the South Dakota prairie. But Edith had spent her childhood watching her father build things nobody expected — a chrome-and-glass fashion boutique in a farm town, a patented clothes hanger, a life assembled from dust and stubbornness and the conviction that the next thing should always be better than the last — and something in that example had settled into her bones. She was going to be a writer. Writers needed to see the world. Mexico was the world, or at least a part of it that she had never seen, and the train was moving, and the corn fields of Iowa were sliding past the window, and there was no turning back.

Albert had driven her to the station. He had carried her suitcase to the platform and set it down and stood there with his hands in his pockets, looking at his daughter the way he had looked at the Statue of Liberty from the deck of the *Pueblo* — with the quiet, complicated expression of a man who understands that some departures are permanent even when the ticket says round-trip. He did not say: *Be careful.* He did not say: *Write every day.* He said: "You'll be fine." And then he turned and walked back to the car, because Albert Isaak was not a man who lingered at train stations, and because he had learned, a long time ago, that the hardest thing about loving someone is letting them leave.

Eva had packed food for the journey — sandwiches wrapped in wax paper, an apple, a thermos of coffee — because Eva did not trust the dining car and because feeding people was her language the way music was Albert's and words were Edith's. The sandwiches would last through Kansas. The coffee would last through Oklahoma. After that, Edith was on her own.

---

The train crossed Kansas in the afternoon and Texas overnight. Edith slept in her seat — or tried to, her head against the window, the rhythm of the wheels tapping out a pattern that was almost music if you listened to it long enough. She woke at dawn somewhere south of San Antonio, and the landscape outside had changed. The endless green corn fields were gone. In their place was a dry, scrubby country of mesquite and cactus and red dirt that stretched to a horizon so flat and so far that it made South Dakota look crowded. The air coming through the cracked window was warm and smelled like dust and sage and something she could not identify — something vegetal and sweet that she would later learn was creosote bush, the smell of the desert after it thinks about raining but decides not to.

At Laredo, the train stopped. Everyone got off. There were customs officials and immigration officers and a bridge over the Rio Grande that connected the United States to Mexico, and on the other side of that bridge was a country Edith had only read about in books — a country of pyramids and cathedrals and murals painted on the sides of buildings by artists whose names she did not yet know but would.

She did not need a passport. In 1945, Americans crossed into Mexico with a birth certificate and a tourist card, and Edith had both, and she presented them to the Mexican customs official with the composed certainty of a girl who had been raised by Eva Roesch Isaak and who had learned, from watching her mother handle difficult customers at Apparel Art, that the trick to dealing with officials is to behave as though you belong exactly where you are.

The official looked at her. He looked at the birth certificate. He looked at her again — a blonde, blue-eyed, seventeen-year-old American girl, alone, heading to Mexico City by train. He stamped the card. She crossed the bridge.

On the other side, she boarded the Mexican National Railway — the *Ferrocarriles Nacionales de México* — and the train pulled out of Nuevo Laredo and headed south through the dry plains of Tamaulipas toward Monterrey. The trip from the border to Mexico City would take thirty hours. Thirty hours through a country she had never seen, in a train that was older and slower and louder than the American ones, with passengers who spoke a language she did not understand and who ate food she did not recognize and who looked at the blonde girl by the window with friendly curiosity and said things to her that she smiled at because smiling is a language that works everywhere.

The train climbed. The desert gave way to mountains. The mountains gave way to valleys, and the valleys gave way to more mountains, and the air grew cooler as the elevation rose, and somewhere past Querétaro the landscape turned green — not Iowa green, not the flat, monotonous green of a corn field, but the green of a volcanic highland, layered and complex, with agave plants and pine forests and terraced fields carved into hillsides by people who had been farming this land for a thousand years before the Isaaks left Soest.

Mexico City, when it arrived, was the largest city Edith had ever seen.

It was not Cedar Falls. It was not Eureka. It was not anything she had a frame of reference for — a sprawling, ancient, modern, chaotic metropolis of two million people built on the drained bed of a lake at seven thousand feet above sea level, ringed by volcanoes, layered with the ruins of civilizations that had risen and fallen long before Columbus sailed. The air was thin and cool. The light was sharp. The noise was constant — car horns and church bells and vendors shouting and music pouring from open doorways and the general sonic chaos of a city that has been alive for six hundred years and does not intend to be quiet about it.

Mexico City College was downtown — a small, scrappy institution housed in a building in the city center, founded in 1940 to serve the English-speaking community. The student body was a mix of Americans living in Mexico, expatriates, and a handful of adventurous kids like Edith who had seen an advertisement in a college catalog and thought: *Why not?*

On her first day, a Mexican student tried to say her name.

"Eh-DEET?"

"Edith."

"Eh-DEET." The student shook his head apologetically. The *th* did not exist in Spanish, and the short American *i* landed somewhere foreign on a Mexican tongue. Every introduction was the same — the polite attempt, the mangled vowels, the helpless smile. By the end of the first week, Edith had solved the problem the way Isaaks had always solved problems: she adapted. She introduced herself as Allegra — her middle name, the name from Longfellow's poem, the name her father had given her because he could not bear to leave any of the poet's daughters out. *Allegra* rolled off a Spanish tongue like music. It needed no translation, no explanation, no awkward repetition. It was, in every language, beautiful.

She never went back. From that summer forward — for the rest of her life — she was Allegra.

The professors were an eclectic group — American academics, European refugees who had fled the war, Mexican scholars who taught in English — and the curriculum was liberal arts in the broadest sense: literature, history, philosophy, art, the kind of education that teaches you not what to think but how to see.

Allegra saw. She saw the murals of Diego Rivera — enormous, political, gorgeous, covering the walls of public buildings with the history of a country told in color and fury. She saw the Aztec ruins at Teotihuacán — the Pyramid of the Sun rising from the valley floor like something that should not exist but did, built by a civilization that had calculated the movements of the stars before Europe had running water. She saw the markets — the stalls piled with chiles and mangoes and flowers and pottery and things she could not name — and the cathedrals built from the stones of temples, and the women in rebozos carrying babies on their backs, and all of it was so far from the 5&10¢ store and the stone houses on the prairie that she felt, for the first time in her life, the vertigo of a person standing at the edge of a world much larger than the one she had been given.

She wrote. She wrote letters to Eva and Albert and Alice. She wrote in her journal. She wrote descriptions of the markets and the murals and the light at seven thousand feet and the way the volcanoes looked at sunset — Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl, the Smoking Mountain and the Sleeping Woman, two peaks so beautiful that they seemed like something out of a story, and Allegra, who wanted to be a writer, understood that the world was full of things that seemed like stories and were, in fact, just the world being itself.

The summer ended. She went home. She was not the same girl who had left. She was not even the same name.

---

**Iowa State Teachers College — 1946**

Allegra came back to Iowa and enrolled at Iowa State Teachers College in Cedar Falls — the school that was right there, walking distance from home, the practical choice. She studied education, because education led to a job, and a job was a thing you needed while you figured out how to do the thing you actually wanted to do, which was write.

She spent a year there. She took the courses, earned the credits, learned the methods that teachers learn. All of it useful. None of it the thing that made her heart beat faster.

She had seen Mexico. She had stood at seven thousand feet and watched the volcanoes burn at sunset. She knew the world was larger than Teachers College, and that the words she wanted to write required a larger education than Cedar Falls could give her.

---

**Stanford — 1947**

That same year, Albert and Eva closed Apparel Art.

They had heard about a town on the California coast — Santa Barbara, a place of mild winters and Spanish tile and bougainvillea climbing white stucco walls, as far from the Dust Bowl as geography could arrange. Albert found a house at 388 Wyola Road, in a neighborhood called Mission Park, and they packed up the store on 217 Main Street and left Cedar Falls for good. Eva's mother was tasked with emptying the Iowa house. She was not a sentimental woman. She did not save much — including, to Allegra's later heartbreak, things Allegra had written as a girl. The pages disappeared the way Tante Frieda had disappeared, the way the stone houses on the prairie had disappeared, the way the old world always disappears when the new one arrives: completely, without ceremony, and without the possibility of return.

In the fall of 1947, Allegra Isaak enrolled at Stanford University.

It was a real switch from being a little Iowa girl. Stanford sat in a valley in Northern California, nearly eighteen hundred miles from Cedar Falls, surrounded by golden hills and palm trees and a kind of sunlight she had never seen — clean, sharp, the light of a place that had never known a dust storm. The campus was enormous — sandstone buildings with red tile roofs, arched colonnades, a quad that could have held every building in Eureka with room to spare.

She was nineteen. She majored in creative writing and minored in philosophy.

No Isaak had ever attended a university. Not Gottlieb, who had taught himself to read in a schoolhouse in Kulm. Not David, who had taught himself business behind a counter. Not Albert, who had never gone past third grade and had taught himself everything else — the clarinet, the violin, the machine gun, the art of building a store that belonged on Fifth Avenue — by living and attempting. Allegra had the luxury none of them had: a classroom that existed solely for the purpose of learning. Albert and Eva had worked for it — the late nights at Apparel Art, the Time Saver hangers, every garment Eva sold, every window display Allegra had dressed as a teenager. All of it, in some small way, tuition.

Stanford was culture shock. The students were from everywhere — California, New York, Chicago. They dressed differently. They talked differently. They had opinions about books she had not read and music she had not heard, and for the first time in her life she was not the smartest person in the room, which was, she discovered, exactly where she wanted to be.

---

**Spring 1948**

And then there was the matter of the taxi.

It was spring vacation, and Allegra was heading home. The Southern Pacific peninsula train from Palo Alto terminated at the Third and Townsend Depot in San Francisco — a Mission Revival station at the southern edge of the city, handsome but inconveniently located. The Ferry Building, where she needed to catch the ferry across the Bay to the Oakland Mole and board the eastbound transcontinental, was a mile and a half north along the waterfront. Not a terrible walk. A terrible walk with a suitcase.

She hailed a taxi. Two young men were doing the same thing, heading the same direction, and in the practical democracy of mid-century travel — where sharing a cab was not intimacy but arithmetic — the three of them climbed in together.

They were twins. Jack and Jim McBirney — identical, six foot three, with the easy bearing of young men who had recently been in uniform and had not yet lost the habit. They had been stationed at a military base in Hawaii, and they were heading home to Colorado. Jack was starting at Stanford that fall.

Allegra was five foot two. She looked up at them the way a person looks up at a cathedral ceiling — with the mild vertigo of someone confronting an architectural fact she had not been prepared for.

They talked. The taxi crawled through San Francisco traffic — the cable car tracks, the hills, the fog rolling through the gaps between buildings like a slow white tide — and by the time they reached the Ferry Building, Allegra knew that Jack McBirney laughed easily, that he liked to talk, and that he had the particular quality of attention that makes a person feel as though they are the only other person in the room, even when the room is a taxi with his twin brother in it.

The ferry was the old way — the traditional, romanticized start of a cross-country rail journey. The boat pulled away from the Ferry Building and headed across the gray-green water toward Oakland, and the city shrank behind them, and Allegra stood at the rail and watched it go, and Jack stood beside her, and neither of them said anything important, which is how the most important conversations often begin.

At the Oakland Mole — the massive Southern Pacific terminal where the transcontinental trains waited — they boarded the same eastbound train on the Overland Route. For a few hours they were heading the same direction, through the valley and into the Sierra Nevada, before Jack and Jim's route diverged south toward Colorado and Allegra's continued east.

Before they parted, Jack did something that Allegra did not notice at the time but would understand later. He glanced at her return ticket — casually, the way you glance at a clock on the wall, as though it does not really matter. He noted the date. He noted the train.

Weeks later, Allegra boarded the westbound at the Oakland Mole, found her seat, and opened her book. She was reading when someone sat down across from her.

She looked up. Jack McBirney was smiling.

"What a coincidence," he said.

It was not a coincidence. It was the most deliberate accident in the history of the Southern Pacific Railroad. Allegra smiled back.

*Right away, we knew we were for each other.* She would say this later, in the plain, certain way of a woman who believes that some things are arranged by a hand larger than her own. She attributed it to God's calling — the same God who had carried the Isaaks across oceans and prairies, who had held Gottlieb's midnight prayer and sent Hanna's psalm to a field hospital in France. The God who arranges taxis.

Jack was everything Allegra was not — outgoing where she was reserved, spontaneous where she was deliberate, six foot three where she was five foot two. He made her laugh. He was studying at Stanford now, and the campus that had been golden before was golden in a different way, the way all landscapes change when you see them alongside someone who is seeing them for the first time.

In December 1949, Jack proposed. The engagement was announced in the Santa Barbara News-Press — the daughter of Mr. and Mrs. Albert F. Isaak of Santa Barbara, to Mr. Jack McBirney of Colorado. The Isaaks had made the newspaper. Not for a draft card or a death notice or a Golden Wedding, but for a beginning.

---

**August 31, 1950**

Allegra graduated from Stanford with honors. She was twenty-two — the first Isaak in two hundred years to hold a diploma. Gottlieb, who had written his autobiography in the margins of a Bible because there was no other paper, would have wept.

On the last day of August, at the First Evangelical Church in Cedar Falls, Iowa, Allegra Isaak married Jack McBirney.

She wore a white organdy gown with matching mitts. Her veil was attached to a lace-trimmed bonnet, and she carried a prayerbook with a shower arrangement of stephanotis. Her attendants were her sister Alice and her friend Virginia Rich, both in blue taffeta gowns with matching organdy picture hats and mitts. The best man was Jim McBirney — the twin, the other tall one, the man who had been in the taxi that day in San Francisco when everything started.

They honeymooned at Yellowstone. The geysers. The hot springs. The elk standing in meadows at dawn. The particular American grandeur of a place that has been beautiful since before anyone was looking.

Allegra had always wanted fourteen children, like her Grandma Roesch — the woman with the teeth-marks on the wooden spoon. She did not get fourteen. She got six, which was, by any reasonable standard, plenty.

---

**The Children**

Michael — Mike — was born on April 8, 1952, in Santa Barbara. The first grandchild. Albert held him the way Gottlieb had held Albert — carefully, with rough hands, as though the child were something that had come up unexpectedly from good soil.

Mark arrived on February 8, 1954\. Connie on October 1, 1956 — the first girl, and Allegra's particular joy. David — Davey — on April 7, 1959\. Scott — Scotty — on November 7, 1962\. Five children in ten years, in a house in Santa Barbara, a mile from Albert and Eva's place on Wyola Road.

And then the Isaaks did what the Isaaks had always done. They moved.

---

**Newfoundland — 1963**

In 1963, Jack and Allegra packed all five children into a van and drove across the continent to Newfoundland for missionary work.

It was the farthest east any Isaak had gone since Johann Michel left Brandenburg in 1760\. The family that had spent two centuries moving west was now moving east — not fleeing, not seeking land or water or escape from a draft, but answering a call. Allegra's faith, the same faith that had carried the family from Soest to California, had pointed her to the rocky coast of Canada's easternmost province, and she had followed it the way Gottlieb had followed Johann's letter to Dakota: completely, without reservation, with five children in the back seat.

They went for a year. When the year was over, they drove all the way home to Santa Barbara. Then they decided they wanted to go back.

Eva was crushed.

She and Albert had been living a mile from Allegra — close enough to walk, close enough to see the grandchildren every week, close enough to be the Sunday grandparents the way Grandma Roesch had been the Sunday grandparents on the farm in South Dakota. The news that Allegra was moving to Newfoundland — permanently, with all the children — hit Eva the way Tante Frieda's departure had hit seven-year-old Edith. The person she loved most was leaving, and the leaving was worse than death because the person was still alive but would not be there, and the distance was not a mile but three thousand.

Allegra packed everything into the van. They drove east. They settled in St. John's, Newfoundland — a harbor city at the edge of the Atlantic, the easternmost city in North America, a place of fog and fishing boats and salt air and a particular rugged beauty that reminded Allegra, in ways she could not quite articulate, of the prairie. Both landscapes were vast. Both were indifferent to human plans. Both required the kind of people who could look at a hard place and say: *This will do.*

On June 5, 1966, the sixth child was born in Newfoundland. A girl. They named her Kim Allegra McBirney — the mother's name passed down, the way names had always been passed down in the Isaak family, from generation to generation, because names are the thing that survives when everything else is gone.

Kim was always a little love bug — affectionate and fearless and a tomboy who preferred her brothers' company to dolls. She played outside. She climbed things. She got dirty. She was, in temperament, the opposite of the delicate organdy-and-stephanotis bride her mother had been at twenty-two, and Allegra loved this about her, because Allegra understood — better than most — that the best things in life are the ones that surprise you.

---

**Daly City — 1970**

In 1970, the family moved back to California.

Not Santa Barbara this time but Daly City — a suburb just south of San Francisco, perched on the fog-swept hills above the Pacific, a place of identical pastel houses and supermarkets and the particular suburban normalcy of post-war America. It was not glamorous. It was not the stone houses on the prairie or the chrome-and-glass elegance of Apparel Art or the rocky coast of Newfoundland. But it was California, and it was close to the family, and the children needed schools, and schools needed a permanent address, and the Isaaks — who had been moving for two centuries — were, at last, ready to stop.

Allegra was forty-three. She had six children. She had a degree from Stanford and a husband who made her laugh and a faith that had carried her from the apartment above the 5&10¢ store to the edge of the Atlantic and back. She had her mother's stubbornness and her father's inventiveness and her great-great-grandfather's conviction that the most important thing a person can do is write down what happened, so that the people who come after will know.

She was home. The story was not over. But the moving was done.

## Chapter Twelve

# Pig Feet

---

*By the early 1970s, the Isaak family's westward migration had come to rest in Daly City, California — a suburb of identical pastel houses on the foggy hills above the Pacific, where the wind came off the ocean and the supermarkets were open until nine and the nearest prairie was two thousand miles behind them. Allegra was forty-three. She had six children, a Stanford degree, a husband who made her laugh, and a faith that had been building pressure for years, the way a river builds behind a dam — quietly, invisibly, until the force of it demands an outlet.*

*In 1971, she found one.*

---

**The Ministries**

Allegra started a radio ministry.

It was not a large operation — not the kind of broadcast that reaches millions, not the kind with a studio and a staff and a toll-free number scrolling across the bottom of a television screen. It was Allegra, with a microphone and a script she had written herself, speaking into the air the way her great-great-grandfather Gottlieb had spoken into the darkness of a rocking chair at midnight in 1913 — directly, without apology, to a God she believed was listening and to an audience she hoped was too.

She had been writing all her life. She had studied writing at Stanford. She had composed music, designed window displays, taught English to a German nanny, played a pipe organ that vibrated in her sternum. But the radio ministry was different. It was the first time she had used her voice — her actual, physical voice — to do the thing the Isaaks had been doing since Soest: carry the faith forward, across whatever distance was required, to whoever was willing to hear it.

That same year, she and Jack founded Grace Bible Church of Daly City. It started small — a handful of families meeting in a living room, the way David Isaak had started the Lutheran congregation in Mercer County with a handful of homesteaders in a schoolhouse. The Isaaks had been founding churches for three generations. It was, apparently, in the blood.

In 1972, Allegra started the San Francisco Christian School. Because when you have built a church and a radio ministry and raised six children and graduated from Stanford with honors, the obvious next step is to build a school. The Isaaks did not do things halfway. They never had. Gottlieb had broken fifty acres of virgin prairie in his first year. Albert had designed a clothing store that belonged on Fifth Avenue. Allegra built institutions the way her ancestors had built sod houses — with whatever materials were at hand, on whatever ground was available, and with the absolute conviction that the thing being built was necessary.

---

**The Van — Summer 1973**

And then Allegra decided to take the whole family to Europe.

All eight of them. Jack, Allegra, Mike, Mark, Connie, Davey, Scotty, and Kim — ages twenty-one down to seven — packed into a rented Volkswagen van with a sliding door that would become, over the course of six weeks, the most important architectural feature of their lives.

The trip was missionary in purpose. Jack and Allegra were exploring opportunities for overseas ministry — looking for contacts, visiting churches, scouting locations where the work they had been doing in Daly City might be needed and welcomed. The children understood this in theory. In practice, what they understood was that they were driving across Europe in a van with seven other people who did not bathe.

They flew to London. They rented the van. They bought a budget guidebook — the kind that promised Europe on five and ten dollars a day — and they did everything in it. Everything. If the guidebook said a castle was worth seeing, they saw it. If it said a cathedral was free, they entered it. If it said a public park had a view, they found the view and stood in it and took a photograph and got back in the van.

---

**England and Scotland — Two Weeks**

They started in England. In a public square — the kind of cobblestoned plaza where street performers gather and tourists stop and pigeons strut with the confidence of animals who have never been told they are unwelcome — the entire McBirney family set up and played music.

All of them. Jack and Allegra and every child who could hold an instrument or carry a tune, performing for the crowd the way Albert had performed with his cousins in the Eureka band, the way Gottlieb had sung hymns in the schoolhouse in Kulm. The music was in the family the way the farming had once been in the family — not a talent but a trait, passed down through the generations like blue eyes or stubbornness or the inability to sit still when there was work to be done.

The English were polite about it. The English are polite about everything.

Scotland was rain and hills and the particular greenness of a country that has never experienced a drought and cannot imagine one. They drove the van through valleys so narrow that the road was the valley, and the sheep on the hillsides watched them pass with the flat, philosophical stare of animals who have seen tourist vans before and are not impressed.

---

**The Continent — Four Weeks**

They crossed the Channel and entered a world that was larger, older, and more confusing than anything the guidebook had prepared them for. France. Austria. Switzerland. Holland. Italy. Spain. The van became a house on wheels — sleeping quarters, dining room, changing room, argument chamber, and mobile embassy of the McBirney family to the nations of Western Europe.

They did not bathe. This is not an exaggeration. Bathing, in a VW van with eight people and a budget that measured its daily expenditure in single digits, was a luxury that ranked somewhere below fuel and food and the five-franc admission to the Eiffel Tower, which was one of the few things they actually paid for. Occasionally they found public showers — five-minute affairs, coin-operated, lukewarm at best — and they lined up and took turns with the efficiency of a family that has learned to do everything in formation. Every few days they splurged on a hotel room, and the scramble for the bathroom was competitive in a way that would have impressed the fly-killing cousins of McPherson County.

The older kids — Mike and Mark — took turns driving, with Jack navigating from the passenger seat using a large paper map that folded and unfolded with the reluctant cooperation of a document that had been folded and unfolded too many times. The map became a character in its own right — consulted, argued with, blamed for wrong turns, and occasionally held upside down, which explained certain detours through villages that the guidebook had not mentioned because they were not worth mentioning.

Scotty had sandals. Bad sandals — the kind that expose every toe and offer no protection from the dust and dirt and cobblestones and general filth of six weeks of European travel on foot. His feet got dirtier and dirtier. The dirt was not the ordinary dirt of a child who has been playing outside. It was the accumulated grime of multiple nations — French dirt, Austrian dirt, Swiss dirt, Italian dirt, layered and compressed until his feet were a geological record of the trip itself.

His siblings called him Pig Feet.

"I don't want to sit next to Scotty and his pig feet\!" became the rallying cry of the van, the daily objection, the hill upon which sibling diplomacy died and was reborn every morning. Scotty sat on the floor. The sliding door was kept open while they drove — not for the scenery, though the scenery was spectacular, but for the air, because eight unwashed people in a Volkswagen van in July produce an atmosphere that the Geneva Convention should have addressed.

---

**The Castles**

When they saw a castle, they pulled over.

This was not a policy. It was a compulsion. The European countryside was littered with castles — crumbling stone towers on hilltops, walled estates behind iron gates, medieval ruins rising from fields like the bones of a civilization that had been enormous and was now decorative. The McBirneys could not pass one without stopping. They parked the van, climbed out, and approached the castle with the cheerful determination of a family that has confused tourism with a constitutional right.

Many of the castles were on private property. The McBirneys did not always notice this, and when they did notice, they did not always care, because the castle was *right there* and the fence was *not very high* and the sign — if there was a sign — was in a language they did not read. They trespassed. They explored. They took photographs. And then a groundskeeper or a farmer or an actual aristocrat would appear, speaking rapidly in French or German or Italian, and the McBirneys would smile apologetically and retreat to the van with the particular grace of a family that has been kicked off private property before and has developed a system for it.

---

**Innsbruck**

In Innsbruck, Davey wandered off.

He was fourteen. He had the Isaak tendency toward independence and the teenager's conviction that he was perfectly capable of navigating a foreign city without assistance. He left the group — to look at something, to explore a street, to follow a sound or a smell or the simple magnetic pull of a direction that seemed more interesting than the one the family was going — and then he was gone.

There was no way to contact him. No cell phones. No pagers. No technology of any kind that could bridge the gap between a fourteen-year-old boy and his family in a city of a hundred thousand people where nobody spoke his language. Jack and Allegra split up. The older kids fanned out. They searched the streets, the squares, the churches, the parks, the train station, the places a fourteen-year-old might go and the places he might not, and the hours passed — one, two, three, four, five — and the panic that every parent carries in the back of their mind, the panic that is always there and is usually wrong, was no longer in the back of anyone's mind.

Six hours later, they found him sitting on the steps of a building in the center of town, calm and unbothered, with the serene expression of a boy who has spent an interesting afternoon in Innsbruck and does not understand what all the fuss is about.

The fuss was considerable.

---

**Holland**

In Holland, on the day before they were scheduled to leave, they discovered a camping supply store.

The van was full. It had been full since France and had become fuller in every country — souvenirs, brochures, clothing that had been washed and clothing that had not, the paper map, the guidebook, the instruments, and the accumulated debris of six weeks of family travel at close quarters. There was no room for anyone to sleep comfortably, and there had not been room for several countries.

The camping store had display tents — set up outside, fully assembled, the kind of demonstration models that are meant to be admired and not inhabited. The McBirneys parked the van, took the pillows from the car, walked into the display tents, and went to sleep.

It was, in hindsight, one of the great triumphs of McBirney logistics — the moment when the family's refusal to spend money intersected with the camping industry's refusal to lock up its display models, and both sides got what they needed.

---

**Italy**

Mark found a sign.

It was a no-parking sign — the Italian variety, a cardboard placard in a plastic sleeve, the kind of bureaucratic artifact that holds no aesthetic value and possesses no intrinsic interest to any reasonable person. Mark was not a reasonable person. He was nineteen, and the sign was foreign, and foreign signs are, to a nineteen-year-old abroad, irresistible. He took it. He put it in his suitcase. He did not mention this to anyone.

The sign got wet. Suitcases on the floor of a VW van that has been driving through Europe for five weeks get wet. The ink from the cardboard bled through the plastic and imprinted itself on Mark's clothes — a mirror-image Italian no-parking notice, transferred permanently onto his shirt, as though the Italian *polizia* had stamped him with a citation he could not remove.

He wore the shirt for the rest of the trip. He did not have a choice. The van was full, the laundry situation was what it was, and Mark McBirney walked through the remaining countries of Western Europe with the word *DIVIETO* printed backwards across his chest, a walking monument to the consequences of petty theft and inadequate luggage.

---

**Spain**

In Spain, they found a department store that sold fencing swords.

Real ones. Or real enough — the kind of blunted practice foils that a sporting goods section might carry, displayed alongside soccer balls and tennis rackets and the general athletic merchandise of a country that takes its sports seriously. The McBirney children, who had been cooped up in a van for five weeks and who had the accumulated physical energy of six young people who have not bathed properly since London, regarded the fencing swords with the reverence of knights discovering Excalibur.

Whether any swords were purchased is a matter of family debate. What is not debated is that the Spanish sporting goods section provided the McBirneys with approximately forty-five minutes of entertainment, which was, by the standards of the trip, a significant achievement.

---

Somewhere in the countryside — the country is disputed, the details are hazy, the way details always get hazy when a family of eight is telling a story and each member remembers a different version — a family of strangers invited the McBirneys into their home.

They had sheep. The strangers, not the McBirneys. The sheep were in a field behind the house, and the McBirney children — city kids from Daly City, California, who had never been closer to livestock than Grandma Roesch's unnamed dogs and their brief, tragic relationship with the gravel highway — were delighted. They chased the sheep. The sheep ran. The children ran faster. The sheep ran faster still. And then the sheep were gone — scattered across the countryside, fled into hedgerows and over fences and into the kind of deep, committed hiding that prey animals achieve when they have been chased by six American children who do not understand that sheep are not toys.

The strangers were less delighted than the children.

The McBirneys returned to the van. They drove on. The sheep, presumably, eventually came home, because sheep do, and because the McBirneys were no longer there to chase them.

Connie, throughout all of this, was waiting for a letter from Dave Tucker. Every town, every post office, every hotel lobby — she checked. The letter was coming. She was sure of it. Love letters, like sheep, find their way home eventually. Whether this particular letter arrived before the end of the trip is another matter of family debate.

---

They came home.

Eight people, six weeks, one VW van, zero consistent hygiene, an unknown number of trespassing violations, a stolen Italian sign, a flock of dispersed sheep, and enough mosquito bites to qualify as a medical event. They had played music in an English square, lost a child in Innsbruck, slept in display tents in Holland, and driven across a continent with the sliding door open because Scotty's feet smelled like a war crime.

It was the best trip any of them ever took. They would argue about the details for the rest of their lives — which country had the best castles, which country had the worst showers, who was driving when they got lost in Austria, and whether the sheep incident was in France or Switzerland or somewhere else entirely. But they would never argue about the trip itself. It was magnificent. It was ridiculous. It was the McBirneys at their most McBirney — loud, unwashed, deeply faithful, and absolutely certain that the world was theirs to explore, even the parts that had fences around them.

Allegra, sitting in the passenger seat on the drive back to the airport, looked at her family — her beautiful, filthy, sunburned, mosquito-bitten family — and thought about her grandmother Louisa, who had crossed an ocean with nine children in 1878 and had not, so far as anyone knew, complained about it once.

Six was plenty. But she understood the impulse.
