## Chapter Eleven

# The Golden Wedding

Eureka, South Dakota — 1903 to 1905

---

The roses were doing something Gottlieb had not expected.

He was sitting in the chair on the right side of the porch — the chair was always on the right side, the chair he had set there in the spring of 1899 when they had moved into the small house at the end of the quiet street, the chair he had not, until this summer, ever quite admitted to himself was *his* chair — and he was watching the four rosebushes in the front yard the way a man watches something he has half-accidentally created and is now responsible for. It was the second week of July of 1903. The afternoon had cooled into the kind of South Dakota evening when the heat lifts off the earth in waves you can almost see, and the sky over the rooftops across the street was the color of the inside of a peach.

The roses were red.

He had bought them from a German nurseryman in Aberdeen by mail order, three summers ago, on the strength of a small advertisement in a Bismarck newspaper that had promised *hardy red blooms suited to the prairie climate*. He had not believed the advertisement when he sent the money. He had still not believed it when the roots arrived in a wooden crate packed in damp sawdust. He had planted them along the front of the house because Louisa had stood at the kitchen window one morning and said, *Gottlieb, the front yard is bare*, in the particular voice she used when she was not making a request but was nevertheless making a request, and he had taken the hint and dug four holes in the dust by the porch and put the roots in and watered them and waited for them to die.

They had not died. They had bloomed in the second summer, modestly. This summer they were doing something extravagant. The four bushes had thrown out so many blooms that the canes were bowing under the weight, and the smell of them in the evening air was the kind of smell that made a man stop and lift his head, the way an animal lifts its head at a sound it does not have a category for.

Gottlieb sat in his chair and looked at his roses, and he thought about how much lighter Louisa had gotten.

He had been thinking about it for weeks. Not steadily — he was not a steady worrier, he was a man who put a thing in a drawer and shut the drawer until the drawer started rattling — but the drawer had been rattling since June. He had felt it first when she had taken his arm to come down the porch step on a Sunday morning, and her hand on his sleeve had been the wrong weight. Not the wrong shape; the shape was the same, the small dry shape of her hand he had been feeling on his sleeve since 1854. Just the wrong weight. As if there were less of her inside the hand than there had been the year before. He had not said anything. He had walked her down the step and across the yard to the buggy and to the church, and he had not said anything in the church and he had not said anything coming home, and he had not said anything since.

He was not going to say anything now, either. He had decided that.

The screen door behind him opened with the small wooden slap it always made, and Louisa came out onto the porch.

She was carrying a cup of coffee. She was wearing the everyday dress, the brown one with the small white pattern at the cuffs, and her hair was pinned up in the tight knot she had worn every day of her life since their wedding. She crossed to the left chair — the chair was on the left side of the porch because she had decided in 1899 that she preferred to look at the rose bed from that angle — and she sat down. Slowly. More slowly than she had sat down a year ago. Gottlieb did not turn his head. He was watching the roses very intently.

"Gottlieb."

"Yes, Louisa."

"You are looking at those roses the way you used to look at a contract."

"I am looking at them because they are very red."

"They have been very red for two months. You are looking at them because you are not looking at me."

He turned his head.

She was smiling. It was the small dry smile she had been giving him for forty-nine years. Her face in the long evening light was the face he had been looking at since she was sixteen, and it was thinner than it had been in the spring, and her eyes were the same eyes.

"Louisa."

"Yes."

"I —"

"I know."

He waited.

"You have been wanting to say something for two months and you have not said it because you do not want to say it out loud and you do not have to say it out loud, Gottlieb. I know what you have noticed. I noticed it before you did, because it is happening to me and not to you, and I have decided not to make a fuss about it because making a fuss would not change anything and it would only ruin the porch evenings, which I am enjoying very much." She took a sip of her coffee. "So we are not going to talk about it. We are going to sit on the porch and drink the coffee and look at the roses, which are very red, and tomorrow we are going to do it again."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"All right."

"All right."

"The roses are very red."

"They are. You have become an old man with a flower garden, Gottlieb. It does not suit you."

"I know."

"Keep doing it."

He kept doing it. They sat on the porch in the long evening light without speaking for a while, and after the light had begun to fade and the smell of the roses had begun to thicken in the cooling air, Louisa reached across the gap between the chairs and put her hand on his wrist where it lay on the arm of his chair, and she left it there. He could feel the bones in her fingers more clearly than he had been able to feel them last year. He did not say anything about it.

He turned his hand over and laced his fingers into hers.

The sky over the rooftops went from peach to copper to a deep bruised red that was, almost exactly, the color of the four rosebushes blooming in the yard below them.

---

A year and five months later, on the morning of the seventeenth of December, 1904, Gottlieb stood in front of the small mirror in the bedroom of the small house and tried to put on his old coat without help.

The coat was the one he had worn to court in Akkerman in 1874. He had worn it to Magdalena's wedding in 1889, and to Wilhelmina's funeral in January of 1899, and to the funeral of his brother August in the spring of 1902, and to every important occasion in between. The wool was thinner than it had been thirty years ago and the lining at the elbows had been mended twice — once by Louisa in the autumn of 1893 and once by Wilhelmina in the autumn of 1898, three months before Wilhelmina died — and the buttons were the original buttons, dark horn, slightly chipped at the edges. He could have bought a new coat for the occasion. Magdalena had offered. He had refused, in the dry exhausted voice of a man who had been refusing the same offer for two months.

He could not get his left arm into the sleeve.

The shoulder was the problem. His left shoulder had been seizing up since November, in the way old men's shoulders seize up in the cold, and he could not lift his elbow high enough to slide it past the lining of the coat. He had been standing in front of the mirror for four minutes trying to do it without making a sound, because if he made a sound, Louisa in the next room would hear, and Hanna in the next room with Louisa would hear, and one of them would come in to help him, and he had decided this morning that he was going to put on his own coat without help on his own fiftieth wedding anniversary if it killed him.

He tried again. The shoulder caught. He bit down on the inside of his cheek and pulled.

From the next room, Magdalena's voice: "Mother, I cannot find the small mother-of-pearl pin. Did you put it in the box on the dresser?"

Louisa's voice, calm: "It is in the box on the dresser, Magdalena. Where I always put it."

"It is not in the box on the dresser."

"Then it is in the small drawer of the dresser. Where I always also put it."

"Why do you have two places?"

"Because I am old and I forget which one I used last time."

A small laugh from Hanna. The sound of a drawer opening.

In the bedroom, Gottlieb pulled harder on the coat sleeve and the shoulder gave a small grinding click and his left arm went into the sleeve. He let out the breath he had been holding. He shrugged the coat onto his shoulders and reached up and began on the buttons.

The buttons were also a problem. His fingers were stiff in the cold mornings and the horn buttons were small and the buttonholes had tightened over the years, and he was on the third button when the bedroom door opened and Louisa walked in.

She was wearing the dark blue dress she had bought for Magdalena's wedding fifteen years ago. Hanna had pinned her hair up in a softer chignon than the everyday tight knot, with a few strands left loose at the temples to frame her face, and Louisa had said, *Hanna, you have made me look like a bride*, and Hanna had said, *Mother, that is the point*, and Louisa had not argued. She crossed the bedroom and stopped in front of him and reached up and took the third button out of his fingers and did it for him. Then the fourth. Then the fifth. He stood very still and let her.

"Gottlieb."

"Yes, Louisa."

"You do not have to go."

He looked down at her. She was looking at the buttons.

"What."

"You do not have to go. I have been watching you protest this for two months and I have just now realized that you are not protesting because you do not want a fuss. You are protesting because you do not want to be looked at, and because you are afraid that being looked at will make you cry in front of half of Eureka, and Gottlieb Isaak does not cry in front of half of Eureka. So if you do not want to go, we will not go. I will send Magdalena to the church to tell the pastor that I am unwell, and the congregation will be disappointed but they will live, and you and I will sit on the porch and drink coffee and look at the rosebushes, which are not in bloom in December but which will be in bloom again in June, and that will be our anniversary."

He stared at her.

"Louisa."

"Yes."

"You have organized this with Magdalena for two months."

"Magdalena has organized it. I have allowed her to organize it. I am allowing her to organize it because she needed to organize it — she lost a sister five years ago and she has been holding herself together with anniversaries and weddings and christenings ever since, and if she did not have something to plan she would fall apart. The anniversary is for Magdalena, Gottlieb. It is not for us. We do not need an anniversary. We have had fifty of them and the fiftieth is the same as the forty-ninth, which was the same as the third." She finished the last button on his coat and smoothed the lapel and looked up at him. "But Magdalena needs it. So if you can do it for her, I would like you to do it. And if you cannot, I will tell Magdalena that I am unwell, and she will be disappointed for an afternoon and then she will be all right, and so will we."

He looked down at her hand on the lapel of his coat.

"Magdalena needs it."

"Magdalena needs it."

"Then I can do it for her."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes." He covered her hand with his own. "But I am very glad you offered."

She smiled — the small dry smile, the same smile she had been smiling at him since 1854 — and she said: "I have been married to you for fifty years, Gottlieb. I know what you need before you know what you need. It is the only useful thing I have learned."

"It is not the only useful thing."

"It is the only useful thing I am willing to take credit for."

She stepped back. She picked his hat up off the bed and handed it to him. He took it.

"Louisa."

"Yes."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome. Now let us go to church."

---

The church was full. Every German family in McPherson County had come. They walked down the aisle to the front pew arm in arm, and Louisa's hand on Gottlieb's sleeve was lighter than it had been a year ago — much lighter, the bones of her fingers very clear through the wool — but her grip was steady, and she walked without leaning, and she held her head the way she had always held her head, which was straight forward and slightly to the left, the angle of a woman listening to something nobody else could hear.

Pastor Reinhardt preached about Cana. The congregation sang *Nun danket alle Gott* in the same German that had been sung at their wedding fifty years ago in Kulm. After the hymn the pastor came down from the pulpit and asked them to stand, and they stood, and he presented them with a Bible — a large dark Bible bound in leather, with a brass clasp on the front and gilt on the page edges. Gottlieb took it with both hands. It was heavier than he had expected. He looked down at it for a moment and then he looked up at Louisa, and Louisa was looking back at him, and the look between them lasted long enough that the front pews noticed.

He did not cry. He had promised himself in the bedroom mirror that he would not cry, and he did not. But his eyes filled, and Louisa saw it, and she put her hand on the front cover of the Bible over his hand and pressed down once and left it there. The brass clasp was cold under their palms.

"*Danke schön*," he said.

The congregation began to clap.

---

The illness arrived in the third week of April, four months later. It came without warning. She had been fine on a Tuesday — tired, but Louisa was always tired now and tired was no longer a thing she complained about — and on the Wednesday morning she did not get out of bed, and she said only that she was going to rest a little longer. By Thursday the doctor was at the small house and his face was doing the small careful thing that doctors' faces do when they have understood and are deciding how to say so, and on Friday morning Gottlieb was sending word to the children.

They came on the next day's roads. Magdalena and Adam from Krem in their own buggy, Solomon riding in from his farm before sunrise, Antonie and Pastor Bruntsch by wagon from the eastern counties, Emanuel by train all the way from Wisconsin because the train was the only way to come from that distance in three days.

By the second week of April the small house at the end of the quiet street was full of grown children and small grandchildren, and Louisa held court from the bed in the back room without ever quite admitting that she was holding court. She greeted each of them. She held the smallest grandchildren on her chest for as long as her arms had the strength. She slept in the afternoons and woke in the evenings and asked for water and sometimes for soup and sometimes for nothing at all.

On the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of April, in the long flat April light coming through the small west window, she opened her eyes and looked at the room and counted the people in it and said:

"Everyone out except Gottlieb."

Magdalena stood up from the chair by the window. Antonie stood up from the footstool at the foot of the bed. David, who had been sitting in the chair on Louisa's right with the new Bible open in his lap to the Psalms, set the Bible down on the bedside table and stood up. Hanna shepherded the children out without a word. The door closed behind them.

Gottlieb sat down in the chair David had left. He took her hand.

The light was the long flat slanting kind that comes through a small window in late April when the day is almost over but is not quite ready to go. Louisa's hair, which Hanna had brushed out for her in the morning, was loose on the pillow. Her hand in his was very thin. The bones of her fingers were clear under the skin.

For a long minute neither of them said anything.

"Louisa."

"Yes, Gottlieb."

"I should have said this fifty years ago, and I did not, and I am going to say it now."

"All right."

He looked down at her hand in his hand and then he looked up at her face on the pillow. The room was very still. From somewhere in the front of the house he could hear Magdalena saying something quietly to one of the smaller children, and Hanna answering, and the small clink of a cup being set down on a saucer.

"I would not have done any of it without you. Not Sophiental. Not the boat. Not the prairie. None of it. I do not mean that I needed your permission, although I did. I mean that the man who did those things was not me by myself. The man who did those things was both of us. And I have never said so out loud, and I should have, and I am saying it now."

Louisa's eyes were dry. The grip of her hand on his fingers was the same grip — the same grip that had reached down over the edge of the bunk on the *Mosel* in the dark in 1878.

"Gottlieb."

"Yes."

"I knew."

He almost laughed. He almost laughed in his dying wife's bedroom.

"You knew."

"I have known since 1854. I did not need you to say it. But I am glad that you said it once, because I would have liked to hear it once. And once is enough."

"Louisa, I —"

"Wait. I am not finished. I want you to do something for me."

"Anything."

"After. When it has happened. I want you to write it down. All of it. Every place we lived. Every child we buried and every child we kept. The contract in Akkerman. The first furrow at Parkston. Wilhelmina. All of it. The grandchildren and their grandchildren will not know what we did unless someone writes it down, and the only person who can write it is you."

"I am not a writer, Louisa."

"You read contracts more carefully than any man in Bessarabia. You can write a story."

"I am seventy-one."

"Then start soon."

He looked at her for a long moment. She was looking back at him with the same clear steady eyes she had been looking at him with since she was sixteen years old in a prayer house in Kulm.

"I promise," he said.

"Good." She closed her eyes. She did not let go of his hand. "Now call David back in. I would like to hear the twenty-third Psalm."

He called David back in. David came in with the new Bible — the Golden Wedding Bible, the leather and brass and gilt — and sat down in the chair on the other side of the bed and opened the book and began to read in his quiet voice. *Der Herr ist mein Hirte, mir wird nichts mangeln.* The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want. The brass clasp had been folded back. The gilt edges of the pages caught the slanting afternoon light. Gottlieb held his wife's hand. Louisa, with her eyes closed, listened.

She lived three more days.

She died on the morning of the first of May. The early light was just beginning to come in through the small east window, and Gottlieb was beside her in the chair he had not left for two days, and David was beside her in the chair on the other side of the bed where he had been reading the Psalms in shifts with Pastor Reinhardt all night. Louisa had been asleep for an hour. Gottlieb was holding her hand and watching her face, and the room was very quiet, and her hand in his went slack between one breath and the next, and the breath after that did not come.

He waited.

The breath did not come.

He waited longer.

He turned his head and looked at David. David's face was already wet. Gottlieb looked back at his wife. He sat there with her hand in his and did not move and did not speak and did not cry, because he had decided in the bedroom mirror in December that he was not going to cry in front of half of Eureka and he was not going to start now in front of his second-eldest son. He sat there until the light through the east window had moved across the bed and across his hands and across the empty face on the pillow, and then he lifted her hand to his mouth and kissed it once and laid it down on the blanket.

"*Schlaf gut, Louisa.*"

Sleep well.

He stood up from the chair. He walked out of the back room into the hallway and he closed the door behind him, and Magdalena and Antonie and Hanna were standing in the hallway already, because they had heard him stand up and they had known what it meant before he had known he was standing, and Magdalena was already crying. He put his arm around her shoulders.

"It is all right, Magdalena," he said. "She is all right. It is all right."

Then he walked past them into the kitchen and stood at the window over the sink and looked out at the four red rosebushes in the front yard, which were not yet in bloom in May but would be in bloom in June, and his shoulders began to shake.

He cried in the kitchen, alone, with his back to the doorway, while his daughters wept in the hallway behind him and his second-eldest son sat in the chair beside the body of his mother in the back room. He cried for about three minutes. Then he stopped, and he wiped his face on the sleeve of his shirt, and he turned around and went back out into the hallway to begin doing the things that needed to be done.
