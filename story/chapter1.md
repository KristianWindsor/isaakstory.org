## Chapter One

# The Alde Kerke

Soest, Westphalia — November 29, 1760

---

In the autumn of 1760, the world was burning.

It had been burning for four years, and in Europe the killing was worst of all. Three weeks earlier, on a frozen field in Saxony called Torgau, Frederick of Prussia had thrown forty-five thousand men against the Austrian lines and won — at the cost of seventeen thousand dead and wounded, and a spent musket ball that knocked him unconscious onto the steps of a church altar, where he spent the night waiting to learn whether he still had an army at all. He did. Barely. To the west, a French army a hundred thousand strong had been camped across the German heartland for three years, and the towns of Westphalia had changed hands more than once. The old King of England had dropped dead in his palace five weeks ago. No one knew if the soldiers would keep coming. No one knew if the money would hold.

And in the town of Soest, on the morning of November 29, a woman in a black dress stood at the door of a church, and waited to be married.

---

The bells of St. Petri began at half past nine, their iron voices cracking the frozen air above the rooftops of Soest. They had been ringing over this town for six hundred years — through plagues, through sieges, through the burning of the Thirty Years' War that had emptied the streets of half its people and never quite let them fill again. Now they rang for a wedding, and the sound carried across the Hellweg, the ancient east-west road, and over the walls of a town that had once been the beating heart of Westphalia and was now little more than a garrison village of three thousand souls.

It was the kind of late November morning when the cold has a smell to it — damp stone and wood smoke and the faint mineral tang of the salt springs that had made Soest famous a thousand years ago. Frost clung to the green sandstone of the church walls. The bare lindens in the Petrikirchhof stood black against a sky the color of pewter. Across the narrow lane, so close you could nearly reach out and touch its massive tower, the Catholic Dom of St. Patrokli loomed in silence — its own bells still. Two churches, two faiths, separated by a few meters of cobblestone.

Inside, St. Petri was cold enough that you could see your breath.

The *Alde Kerke* — the Old Church, as the people of Soest called it — was the oldest parish church in Westphalia, and it looked it. Its Romanesque nave was a forest of thick stone pillars, their surfaces worn smooth by centuries of shoulders brushing past. The vaulted ceiling above was painted — Christ in majesty, saints with faded gilt halos, patterns of vines and stars that disappeared into the shadows where the candlelight could not reach. The windows were narrow, as Romanesque windows are, and on a morning like this they admitted only a thin, grey wash of light that barely reached the flagstone floor.

Thirty people were gathered in the nave. In better times, in the Soest that had been a Hanseatic capital with ten thousand citizens, this church would have been full for a wedding. But those times were two centuries gone. Half the young men of Soest were somewhere to the east with Frederick's army, or buried in the Saxon mud, or simply gone.

And yet here they were. A wedding.

Anna Elisabeth Wittenberg stood at the door of St. Petri and tried not to shiver.

She wore black. Not mourning, but custom. A German bride wore her finest dark dress to her wedding, a tradition older than anyone could explain. Hers was heavy wool, practical, the kind of dress you wore again to christenings and feast days and, eventually, to funerals. At her throat, a scrap of white lace. On her head, a wreath of dried myrtle — the *Myrtenkranz* — its small, dark leaves woven into a crown that declared what the church required be publicly known: that she was entering this contract freely.

She turned, just slightly, and looked back over her shoulder. The neighbor woman holding Johann Michael had wrapped him in a shawl against the cold, and the boy's face — round, serious, two years old — was watching her with the unblinking attention small children give to things they do not understand. Anna Elisabeth held his eyes for a moment. Then she turned back to the door and reached for Andreas's hand. It was as cold as her own.

Beside her, Andreas Micheal Isaak was twenty-one. He wore his best clothes: knee breeches, a waistcoat, a long coat that had been his father's. His hands were rough. He was not a wealthy man. But he had survived what needed surviving, found work, assembled the paperwork, endured whatever public penance the pastor had required, and brought his family to this door. It had taken two years. He was here now.

The pastor stood before them in the doorway.

This was the old way, Luther's way, laid down in the *Traubüchlein* — the little marriage booklet that Martin Luther himself had written in 1529 and that had governed Lutheran weddings for over two hundred years. The vows were exchanged here, at the threshold, in the cold November air, so that the whole community could witness that this thing had been done freely and publicly.

The pastor had already read the banns on three consecutive Sundays from the pulpit of St. Petri. No one had objected. Anna Elisabeth had presented her papers — a letter from her home parish in Lubelskie, six hundred miles to the east in the Polish lands, obtained God knows how across roads choked with Russian armies, confirming that she was free to marry. The paperwork alone was a small miracle.

Now the pastor spoke the words.

*"Andreas, do you desire to have Anna Elisabeth as your wedded wife?"*

*"Yes."*

*"Anna Elisabeth, do you desire to have Andreas as your wedded husband?"*

*"Yes."*

They exchanged rings. Plain bands. The pastor joined their hands.

*"What God has joined together, let no one separate."*

And with that, they were married. Legally, publicly, finally. Johann Michael Isaak — two years old, born in Brandenburg during a Russian invasion, carried westward across the breadth of Prussia — was now a legitimate son.

The congregation parted, and Andreas and Anna Elisabeth walked together through the Romanesque doorway and into the nave — into the cold, candlelit darkness of the oldest church in Westphalia, past the thick stone pillars, up the worn flagstones toward the altar. At the altar, the pastor opened his Bible and read the words from Genesis that Luther had prescribed.

*"Then the Lord God said, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper who will stand by his side.'"*

The pastor prayed. He blessed the marriage. The organ filled the vaults and rattled the old glass in the narrow windows.

It was done.

---

That evening, at an inn near the Marktplatz, there was a modest meal — bread, beer, what meat could be had. The night before, their friends and family had gathered in the street for the *Polterabend*, hurling stoneware and crockery against the cobblestones to shatter it — never glass, only earthenware — while the couple swept up the shards together, a ritual older than Christianity, meant to drive away evil spirits and prove that a marriage begins not with perfection but with broken things made whole by shared labor.

They had already proven that.

A family that had been made in flight was now made whole in law. With a two-year-old boy in someone's arms, and two young people who had already learned that the world does not wait for you to be ready.
