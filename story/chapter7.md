## Chapter Seven

# Wide Open Spaces

---

*The Homestead Act of 1862 promised 160 acres of free land to anyone who would settle it, build on it, and farm it for five years. There was one condition the German-Russians had not anticipated: you could not live in a village. The law required each family to reside on its own claim, separated from its neighbors by a quarter mile or more of open prairie. For a people who had lived in tight-knit colonies for a hundred years — who had shared a single street, a single church, a single schoolteacher — the isolation of the American homestead was a kind of shock. But the land was free, and free land was something no Isaak had ever had.*

---

**Near Parkston, Dakota Territory — April 1878**

Johann's wife had made Bierocks.

They were sitting around his brother's table — Gottlieb, Louisa, the children, Johann and his family — eating the stuffed bread rolls that every German-Russian woman made slightly differently and every German-Russian man considered a matter of serious importance. Johann's wife filled hers with beef and cabbage and onion, the dough golden and tight, and Gottlieb ate three of them before he said a word, because he had been eating train food and ship food and emigrant-hall food for six weeks and his body recognized this meal the way a man recognizes his own language after months of listening to someone else's.

"The soil," Gottlieb said finally, wiping his mouth. "Tell me about the soil."

Johann grinned. He was younger than Gottlieb but looked older now — the Dakota sun had baked him brown and lean, and he moved with the loose confidence of a man who owned his own land and owed nothing on it. "Black," he said. "Like Kulm. The real Kulm."

"Not like Sophiental."

"Nothing is like Sophiental. Sophiental was God's punishment for something we did in Poland."

Gottlieb laughed. It was the first time he had laughed since Bremen, and the sound surprised him. Louisa looked over from the other end of the table, where she was helping Johann's wife clear the dishes, and something in her face softened — not quite a smile, but the memory of one.

"How deep?" Gottlieb asked.

"Deep enough. You can push a rod in to your elbow and not hit clay. It's never been farmed. It's never been anything. Just grass, since the beginning of the world."

"And the rain?"

"It rains. Not like Bessarabia — you don't pray for it the same way. It comes." Johann leaned back in his chair. "The winters are hard. Harder than the steppe. But the land is good, and it's yours, and nobody comes to your door asking for rent."

That word — *rent* — landed on the table between them like a stone. Johann knew the story of Akkerman. Every Isaak knew the story of Akkerman.

"No rent," Gottlieb said quietly.

"No rent. No landlord. No contract that teaches you how to behave." Johann reached across the table and tore a Bierock in half. "Welcome to America, brother."

---

**The First Year**

They had no money. This was not a figure of speech. The through-tickets from Bremen had consumed nearly everything the sale of the Sophiental farm had produced, and what was left after the crossing barely covered seed and a few tools. But they had hands — eleven pairs of them, if you counted Emilie's, which were three years old and not yet useful — and they had the Homestead Act, which asked nothing except that you stay and work.

Everyone worked. Gottlieb and his older sons — Jacob, David, Daniel — broke virgin prairie, cutting through sod that had been accumulating roots for centuries, turning the earth for the first time in its history. The work was brutal. The sod was thick and tangled and resisted the plow the way the Bessarabian sand never had, because this soil was alive — dense with roots and worms and the compressed organic wealth of ten thousand years of grass growing and dying and growing again. It fought back. But when it finally turned, the underside was black and rich and deep, and Gottlieb knelt down and picked up a handful and held it to his nose and smelled it, and the smell was the smell of Kulm, the real Kulm, the Kulm of his childhood, and he closed his eyes and squeezed the dirt in his fist and did not say anything because his sons were watching and he was not the kind of man who cried in front of his sons.

Louisa and Regina planted the *Baschtam* — the kitchen garden, cucumbers and pumpkins and watermelons — and that first summer's garden was extraordinary. *The likes of which we never again had,* Gottlieb would write, and the sentence trembles with remembered abundance. The soil had never been asked for anything, and when they finally asked, it gave back with a generosity that bordered on the absurd — melons the size of a man's head, cucumbers longer than a child's arm, pumpkins so heavy that Solomon, who was twelve and determined to be useful, could barely carry one.

American wages were a revelation. Every member of the family who could work hired out to neighboring farms, and the money that came back was astonishing. *The wages received amounted to more than three times the amount I had to pay in Russia for expenses,* Gottlieb wrote, and the sentence has the quiet shock of a man who has just discovered that the world is not, in fact, arranged to cheat him. Everything the children earned came into his hands, and he counted it at the kitchen table the way he had once counted rubles — carefully, suspiciously, as though the money might disappear if he looked away. But it did not disappear. It accumulated. It became a pair of oxen, a cow, swine. It became twenty-five acres of broken prairie, and then fifty, and then a sod house that was rough and dark but solidly built, and for the first time in Gottlieb's life, the math was going in the right direction.

The old debt from Russia — 330 rubles, the money he had borrowed to pay the rent the Akkerman court had demanded — he paid it off. The favorable exchange rate between dollars and rubles made it possible, and paying it gave him a satisfaction that had nothing to do with money and everything to do with closing a book that should never have been opened.

In 1880, two years after they arrived, Louisa gave birth to a son. They named him Emanuel. He was born in Parkston, Dakota Territory — an American from his first breath, the first Isaak in the family's long history who would never know what it meant to be a subject of a king, a tsar, or anyone else. Louisa was forty-two. She had been bearing children for twenty-four years, across two countries and five relocations, and she held this new one with the practiced tenderness of a woman who knew exactly what she was doing and was not yet finished doing it.

---

**August — November 1878**

Six months after Gottlieb's arrival, almost to the day, August stepped off the train at Yankton with his wife and four children.

Gottlieb was waiting on the platform with a wagon. He had driven down from Parkston the day before, and he stood in the November cold with his hands in his pockets and watched his brother descend from the passenger car — thinner than he remembered, road-worn, carrying a trunk on one shoulder and a sleeping child on the other. August's wife came behind him with the older children, her face set in the particular expression of a woman who has crossed an ocean with small children and is ready to murder someone but has not yet decided whom.

The brothers looked at each other across the platform.

"You're late," Gottlieb said.

"The ship was slow."

"Everything about you is slow."

August set the trunk down and grinned — the first real grin Gottlieb had seen on his brother's face since the campfire in Sophiental, a year and a lifetime ago. "I'm here," he said. "That's what matters."

He was right. He was here. The chain was complete — Johann first, Gottlieb second, August third, each brother arriving to find the one before him already established, already able to point west and say *this is where the land is, this is where you'll sleep, this is where we begin.*

Over the next several years, the chain kept growing. Letters crossed the Atlantic in both directions — from Dakota to Bessarabia and back — and with each letter another family made the decision, sold the farm, obtained the passport, boarded the train. Gottlieb's brothers and cousins arrived in waves: Gottfried, the eldest, came with his grown children. Martin, the youngest, came as a young man looking for land of his own. Cousins from Kulm and the neighboring colonies followed, drawn by the same reports that had drawn Johann and Gottlieb — black soil, free land, no tsar. By the mid-1880s, the prairie around Parkston was filling with German-Russian families who had known each other in Bessarabia, who attended the same Lutheran services on Sundays, who made Bierocks with the same recipes and sang the same hymns and complained about the same weather in the same Swabian-inflected German, and who had, collectively, picked up an entire colony and set it down again on the other side of the world.

They even named a town after the old colony. Kulm, North Dakota. Because what else would you name it.

---

**David and Hanna — 1883**

David met Hanna Hetzler the way most young people met in the German-American communities of Dakota Territory — at church, or at a neighbor's table, or at one of the harvest gatherings where the families came together and the young men stood on one side of the room pretending not to look at the young women and the young women stood on the other side pretending not to notice.

She was not what anyone expected.

Hanna was American. Not German-Russian-pretending-to-be-American — *American*, born in Missouri in 1861, raised in a German-speaking household but with English as her native language, comfortable in this country in a way that the Bessarabian families, with their colony habits and their steppe accents, were still learning to be. Her parents had crossed the Atlantic in 1852, a full generation before the Bessarabian exodus, and they had raised their daughter in the particular confidence of people who consider themselves settled rather than arrived.

David noticed her. Of course he noticed her — she spoke English without an accent, she understood how things worked at the county land office, she could read the signs on the storefronts in Parkston that the rest of the family puzzled over. She was practical and direct and she did not suffer foolishness, which was a trait she shared with every woman David had ever admired, starting with his mother.

They married on August 30, 1883\. David was twenty-three. Hanna was twenty-two. The ceremony was Lutheran — the same rite, the same vows, the same reading from Genesis that had married Gottlieb and Louisa in Kulm, and Andreas and Anna Elisabeth in Soest, and every Isaak couple going back to the beginning. But it was performed in Dakota, on American soil, by an American pastor, and if the words were the same, the world they were spoken into was very different.

Gottlieb approved. He did not say so — Gottlieb was not a man who distributed approval out loud — but he watched David and Hanna together and he saw something that pleased him: his quiet son had married a woman who could navigate the country that Gottlieb himself would never fully understand. David could farm. Hanna could talk to the Americans. Between them, they might manage.

Their first daughter, Josephine, was born in 1885\. Their second child — a boy — came on June 13, 1887\. They named him Albert Friedrich Isaak, and he was born in Dakota Territory, an American from his first breath, and he would carry the Isaak name into the twentieth century and beyond.

But that was decades away. For now, he was an infant in a sod house on the prairie, and his grandfather Gottlieb held him the way old farmers hold babies — carefully, with rough hands, as if the child were something that had come up unexpectedly from good soil.

---

**The News from Bessarabia**

Even in the good years, the old world reached across the ocean.

In 1882, a letter arrived — not a letter of invitation or instruction but the other kind, the kind that came in an envelope that felt heavier than it should. Gottlieb's mother, Anna Elizabeth Louise Schimke, had died in Kulm. She was eighty-three. She had been born before Napoleon's invasion of Russia, had walked to Bessarabia as a girl, had raised twelve children on the steppe, and had outlived her husband by nearly thirty years. She died in the colony, in the house on the land that her husband Karl had divided among too many sons, and she was buried on the hill where the wind never stopped, next to the husband who had walked a thousand miles to give her a home.

The letter had taken weeks to cross the Atlantic. By the time Gottlieb held it in his hands, his mother had been in the ground for a month. He read it at the kitchen table, folded it, and put it in the drawer where he kept the other letters — Johann's, August's, all the letters that connected the old world to the new. Then he went outside and stood in the yard and looked at the Dakota sky, which was the same sky that hung over Kulm, and he thought about the woman who had carried him on her hip while his father argued with his brothers over the land, and who had watched him load a wagon and drive away to Sophiental without saying the thing she must have wanted to say, which was: *don't go.*

The following year, his brother Daniel died. The old colony was sending him obituaries now instead of letters. But the living were coming too — Martin arrived, and cousins, and families from Tarutino and Leipzig, and the prairie around Parkston grew more German every season, until the sound of Swabian German in the general store was more common than English, and the children who had been born in Bessarabia were growing up alongside children who had been born in Dakota, and the two groups could barely tell each other apart.

---

**The Move North — 1886**

Then the sons wanted land of their own, and there was none to be had.

Every quarter section around Parkston had been filed on. The colonization had been that fast — eight years, and the land was gone. The sons would have to go north, into the open country of central Dakota, where the railroad was still pushing into unsurveyed territory.

Gottlieb could have stayed. He was fifty-two. He had built the best farm of his life. The debts were paid, the house was solid, and for the first time since Kulm he was not worried about the rent or the harvest or the Russian army. He could have handed the farm to one of the older boys and sat on his porch and watched the wheat grow and lived out his years in something approaching peace.

But he was Gottlieb Isaak, and Gottlieb Isaak did not let his children go somewhere without him.

*"Children, if you are going to leave, I too do not wish to remain here."*

He found a buyer and sold the farm. The whole family packed again — Gottlieb and Louisa, David and Hanna with Josephine and the infant Albert, Solomon, Magdalena, Daniel, the younger children. They traveled south to Yankton, east to St. Paul, Minnesota, then west on the Northern Pacific Railroad to Hebron, North Dakota.

Hebron. The hills.

Gottlieb and Louisa stepped off the train and saw, for the first time in either of their lives, a landscape that was not flat. The country around Hebron rolled and buckled into rough, broken hills — buttes and coulees and eroded badlands that looked nothing like anything they knew. It looked nothing like the steppe. It looked nothing like Parkston. It looked, if anything, like the surface of the moon, if the moon were brown and covered in scrub grass and had a wind that could strip the skin off your face.

*Then my wife and I first perceived the hills; we were of one mind: "In this place we do not want to die."*

But they had provisions, feed, seed, and a herd of swine, and they were not the kind of people who turned back. They planted potatoes — an enormous amount — and broke fifty acres and built a sod house. David worked alongside his father, breaking the same ground, eating the same dust. Hanna, holding Albert on one hip, surveyed the landscape with the expression of a Missouri-born woman who had married into a family of professional optimists and was beginning to question her judgment.

Then they waited for the rain.

The rain did not come.

*It was real dry, no rain. The time arrived for haying, no grass, and what was planted and seeded, did not come up. The potatoes remained in the ground and not a penny was derived as an income. We were fearful that everything, man and beast, would have to die from hunger during the next winter.*

---

**Mercer County, North Dakota**

Solomon saved them.

He was twenty-one — the boy who had asked too many questions on the train from New York, the boy who had wanted to catch a pronghorn, the boy who had spent his childhood watching and asking and never being satisfied with the first answer. He had grown into a young man of considerable restlessness and even more considerable nerve, and when the Hebron farm failed, he did what no one else in the family had the energy to do: he rode sixty miles west with Johann Klein, looking for better land.

They found it along the Knife and Missouri rivers — flat, open, grass-covered country that looked, at last, like the landscape the Isaaks had been chasing since Soest. A few Irish and English families lived there, mostly hunters. A handful of Germans from Hannover had settled nearby. The higher ground was empty.

*This was exactly what we wanted.*

Gottlieb and Klein bought railroad land. The sons homesteaded government claims around them. David filed his own claim — his first land, his own name on the deed, the first piece of earth in five generations of Isaaks that belonged to one man and could not be divided among ten brothers or sold to a broker or taken by a judge.

The first winter in Mercer County was brutal. The sod house overflowed with visitors — other settlers arriving daily, drawn by the same open land, needing a place to sleep until they could build their own. *During every meal two servings had to be made,* Gottlieb wrote. At night, the visitors slept on hay spread across the floor, body to body, while the wind howled and the snow piled against the walls.

But the land was good. *We were, however, able to console ourselves with the feeling that we had good land, which made us all feel more content.* Good land. It was always good land. The promise that came from the earth itself — the only promise, in Gottlieb's long experience, that had ever been reliably kept.

---

**The Church**

There was no Lutheran congregation when the Isaaks arrived in Mercer County. There was no church. There was no pastor, no prayer house, no building of any kind where the German families could gather on Sunday mornings and sing the hymns they had carried across an ocean and hear the scriptures read in the language they had spoken since Soest.

This was, for David, intolerable.

He was the quietest of the Isaak sons — everyone said so, and his obituary would confirm it: *naturally of a quiet and retiring disposition.* But beneath the quietness was a faith as solid and unyielding as the sod beneath the prairie grass, and a conviction that a community without a church was not a community at all but merely a collection of farms.

He organized the founding of the Evangelical Lutheran congregation himself. Not loudly, not publicly — that was not David's way. He went from homestead to homestead, speaking to families he knew from the crossing, from Parkston, from the old colonies. He asked them what they needed, and what they needed was what he needed: a place to worship. He arranged the first services in a schoolhouse — the Krem Schoolhouse, a rough wooden building that served as classroom, meeting hall, polling station, and, on Sunday mornings, church. The families came in wagons from miles around, the women carrying food for the potluck after the service, the men standing outside in the yard talking about the weather and the wheat until the bell rang — not a real bell, not yet, just someone banging on a piece of iron with a hammer — and they filed inside and sat on wooden benches and bowed their heads and prayed in German to the God who had brought them across an ocean and a continent and who, they trusted, had not brought them this far to abandon them in the grass.

David was there every Sunday. He was there when the first pastor arrived — a circuit rider who served three or four congregations across a hundred miles of prairie, arriving every other week on horseback, dusty and tired and carrying a Bible in his saddlebag. He was there when they took the collection for a permanent building. He was there when they laid the cornerstone. *He was one of the founders of the Eureka Lutheran church and served her faithfully until his death,* the obituary would say, and the sentence, in its plain simplicity, understates what David had done. He had built a church. Not with his hands — though his hands were there for that too — but with the steady, modest persistence of a man who believed that faith was not something you carried in your heart alone but something you built in the world, with lumber and nails and the donated labor of your neighbors, so that it would still be standing when you were gone.

---

**County Commissioner**

Then came politics, and with politics came trouble of a kind Gottlieb had not expected.

He was elected county commissioner — the only German on a board of five, the other four being English-speaking Americans who had been in the territory longer and who regarded the German-Russian settlers with the particular suspicion that established people reserve for newcomers who work too hard and complain too little.

*Oh, what a time I had.*

The county was badly in debt, despite having almost no population. The bills came in thick and heavy — contracts and requisitions and expenses that Gottlieb could see were inflated, padded, designed to extract money from a treasury that had no money to extract. The four English commissioners voted yes on everything. Gottlieb voted no.

They did not appreciate this.

He was a German farmer with an accent and a Bible and a deep, Bessarabian suspicion of any official who told you to submit the money or leave. He had stood before the Akkerman court and watched judges vote as one. He knew what arranged outcomes looked like. And he said no — *most of the time I said "no,"* he wrote — until finally the flow of inflated bills began to slow, not because the other commissioners agreed with him but because it is exhausting to steal from a treasury when one man at the table keeps asking to see the contract.

*Eventually more Germans became county commissioners and the county was released from the burden of heavy debts, but not during my time. I was only there for six years.*

Six years of being the only German in the room, saying no in a language the others pretended not to understand, fighting the same fight he had fought in Akkerman — the fight of a man who believes that words on paper should mean what they say.

David watched his father navigate American politics the way he had watched him navigate Russian courts — with a mixture of admiration and the quiet conviction that there had to be a better way. David's way would not be politics. David's way would be business.

---

**Magdalena's Wedding — October 27, 1889**

That autumn, Gottlieb gave away his daughter.

Magdalena — the girl who had pressed her face to the train window and drawn pictures in the fog, the girl who had taken her father's hand on the deck of the *Mosel*, the girl who had watched everything with those wide, recording eyes — was twenty-one now, and she was marrying Adam Sailer at the Krem Schoolhouse, the same wooden building where David had founded the church.

The marriage certificate was numbered one. It was believed to be the first marriage recorded in Mercer County under the new state of North Dakota, which had been admitted to the Union just days earlier. A German-Russian couple, married in a schoolhouse-turned-church, on the raw frontier of a state that was less than a week old. Marriage certificate number one. The Isaaks had been in this country for eleven years and they were already part of its founding records.

---

**The Portrait — 1900**

In 1900, when Gottlieb was sixty-six and Louisa was sixty-two, they sat for a photograph.

It was taken in a studio — a proper one, with a painted backdrop and a heavy wooden camera on a tripod and a photographer who arranged them carefully and told them not to move, which was unnecessary because Gottlieb and Louisa had never moved quickly in their lives and were not about to start for a man hiding behind a box.

They sat together in the center of the frame — Gottlieb in his best coat, his face as weathered and unreadable as the steppe itself, his hands folded in his lap with the studied formality of a man who is deeply uncomfortable being looked at but is doing it because his wife asked. Louisa sat beside him, solid and serious, her mouth set in a line that could have been disapproval or could have been the expression of a woman who has spent forty-six years married to a man who does not like sitting still and is trying not to smile about it.

Their daughter Antonie stood behind them. She was twenty-four, the baby who had crossed the Atlantic on Louisa's hip, who had no memory of Bessarabia and never would. She was the one who would marry Pastor Bruntsch, who would preserve her father's autobiography, who would be the last of Gottlieb's children to die — in 1958, in Arlington, Virginia, a long way from the steppe.

And there was Emanuel. The American. The first Isaak born on this side of the ocean, twenty years old now, standing at the edge of the frame with the slight, restless energy of a young man who is already looking past the camera toward a life that will take him to Wisconsin and Iowa and Illinois — away from the Dakotas, away from the German-Russian colony orbits, away from wheat and sod and the endless prairie. He would be the one who left. Every family has one.

The photographer took the picture. The shutter clicked. Four people frozen in silver and light — a patriarch who had walked out of one empire and into another, a matriarch who had crossed an ocean with nine children and never once looked back, a daughter who would save the story, and a son who would scatter it.

They did not know any of this yet. They were just a family sitting for a photograph in a studio in South Dakota, wearing their best clothes, trying not to blink. The century was turning. Louisa had five years to live. The autobiography had not yet been written. The Bible that would contain it had not yet been given. The Golden Wedding was still four years away.

But the photograph survived. It always survives. Long after the names are forgotten and the farms are sold and the sod houses crumble back into the earth they were made from, the photograph remains — four faces, one moment, a family that had traveled farther than any of them could measure, sitting still at last, looking into a camera, looking into the future, looking at you.
