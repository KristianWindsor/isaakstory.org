## Chapter Three

# The Steppe

---

In January 1813, the Russian army occupied Warsaw. Napoleon's Duchy crumbled. The Congress of Vienna, convened the following year, divided what remained of Poland between Russia, Prussia, and Austria — the same three empires that had carved it up a generation before. For the German colonists who had settled in the Polish territories under Prussian protection, the Congress changed nothing and everything: the land beneath their feet was the same, but the government above them had shifted once again, and with it any claim they had to belonging.

The Tsar, however, had plans for them.

On November 29, 1813, Alexander I issued a manifesto calling for German settlers to colonize Bessarabia — a vast, treeless steppe between the Danube and the Dniester rivers that Russia had seized from the Ottoman Empire just one year earlier. The terms were extraordinary: sixty-six hectares of free land per family, freedom of religion, perpetual exemption from military service, a ten-year tax holiday, government loans for houses and seed, and a daily food allowance of five kopecks per person until the first harvest. The settlers had to be debt-free and willing to leave behind everything they could not carry.

More than 1,500 German families accepted. They traveled in columns — Kolonnen — by horse and wagon, southeast across the breadth of Europe, a journey of more than a thousand miles that took between two and six months. They were the first Germans to arrive in Bessarabia, in 1814, and they were known ever after as the Warsaw Colonists.

Among them were the Isaaks.

---

Blacka, Western Poland — Spring, 1815

They sold what they could not carry and buried what they could not sell.

Johann Michael Isaak spent the first weeks of April settling his accounts — paying off the last of his debts to the village miller, selling the plow and the heavier tools to a Polish neighbor for less than they were worth, trading the milk cow for a second horse because you could not pull a loaded wagon across a thousand miles of road with one animal. The farm itself — the house, the barn, the fenced kitchen garden that Anna Elisabeth had tended for a quarter century — could not be sold. It belonged to the land, and the land belonged to whoever governed it this year, and that was no longer a question Johann Michael cared to answer. He walked away from it.

Karl August watched all of this with the particular attentiveness of a twelve-year-old who understands that something enormous is happening but has not yet been told the full shape of it. He knew they were leaving. He knew the word *Bessarabien*, though he could not have found it on a map. He knew his father had been to the Russian recruiters' office in the nearest town and had come back with papers — a thick packet sealed with red wax — and that his grandmother, Anna Elisabeth, had held the papers in her hands for a long time without speaking, turning them over as if the weight of them might tell her something the words could not.

She was seventy-five years old. She had been born in Lubelskie, Poland. She had married in Soest, Westphalia. She had been widowed twenty-three years ago, when Andreas died in 1792\. She had followed her son to Poland and watched Napoleon's army march past her door and buried grain in the root cellar and waited, as she always waited, for the next thing to happen. Now the next thing was a wagon and a road that led southeast, toward a country she had never seen, and she was going.

Johann Michael — seventeen now, nearly a man — did the heavy work. He loaded the wagon in the grey April mornings while his father went from house to house in the German quarter, coordinating with the other families who were leaving. They would travel in a *Kolonne* — a column of wagons, twenty or thirty families together, for safety and for the simple human need to not be alone on a road that long. Johann Michael checked the axles, greased the wheels, fitted the canvas cover that would be their roof for the next four months. He packed the tools they were keeping — an axe, a saw, a set of chisels, a scythe — in oilcloth beneath the wagon bed. He packed the Bible and the hymnal in a wooden box lined with straw, and set them where they would not get wet.

Karl August's job was the animals. He fed and watered the two horses. He checked their hooves. He was responsible for the crate of chickens that would ride on the back of the wagon, and for the dog, a brown mutt named nothing in particular, who would walk alongside.

On the last evening, Karl August found his brother behind the barn, sitting on an upturned bucket, looking east across the fields. The wheat they had planted last autumn was coming up in pale green rows — wheat they would never harvest. Someone else would cut it, or no one would.

"What do you think it looks like?" Karl August said.

Johann Michael didn't answer right away. He picked up a clod of the dark Polish soil — rich, heavy stuff, the reason their father had come here in the first place — and turned it over in his hands.

"Father says there are no trees."

"None?"

"He says the grass is taller than a man and there is nothing else. No forest. No hills. Just grass and sky."

Karl August tried to imagine this and could not. He had never seen a landscape without trees. The woods beyond the village — birch and oak and linden, thick enough that you could lose sight of the road in summer — were the boundary of his known world. A country with no trees was as abstract to him as a country with no ground.

"What about the house?"

"There is no house. We build it when we get there."

"Out of what?"

Johann Michael looked at him. He was seventeen, and he had their father's face — the same long jaw, the same patient, slightly tired expression of a man who has learned not to expect too much from the world. "I don't know," he said. "Sod, maybe. Or whatever the Russians give us. Father says they give us money for building."

"How much money?"

"Enough, supposedly."

Karl August sat down on the ground next to the bucket. The spring air smelled like wet earth and new growth — the smell of Poland, the only country he had ever known, though it had been called three different things in his twelve years of life.

"Grandmother says the first ones who went last year are already there," he said. "She says they named the towns after battles. Borodino. Tarutino."

"Kulm," Johann Michael said. "That's where we're going. Named after the Battle of Kulm."

"Who won?"

"We did. The Russians, I mean. Against Napoleon."

Karl August pulled at the grass. "Is that a good sign? Living in a place named after a battle?"

Johann Michael almost smiled. "It's a better sign than living in a place named after nothing, which is what this place will be once we leave."

He tossed the clod of soil into the dark and stood up. "Get some sleep. We leave at dawn."

---

They left on a Tuesday in late April, when the roads had dried enough to hold a wagon's weight. The *Kolonne* assembled at the crossroads east of the village — twenty-two families, eighty-some people, a dozen wagons, twice as many horses and oxen, assorted livestock, and everything they owned in the world stacked and lashed and balanced on wooden frames that creaked with every rut in the road.

Anna Elisabeth rode in the wagon. She sat on a folded blanket on the bench seat beside Johann Michael, who drove. She did not look back at the village. She had done this before — left a place she knew for a place she did not — and she understood that looking back was a luxury for people who had a choice. Johann Michael and Karl August walked alongside, as most of the younger people did, because the wagons were too heavily loaded to carry anyone who could walk.

The route led southeast. From western Poland they crossed into Galicia — Austrian territory — skirting the northern edge of the Carpathian foothills. The roads were bad. In places they were not roads at all but mud tracks that swallowed wagon wheels to the hub, requiring the men to unload, lever the wagon free, reload, and continue. They averaged ten or twelve miles a day when the weather held, less when it rained, and it rained often.

The *Kolonne* developed its own rhythm. They rose before dawn, fed the animals, ate whatever bread or porridge could be managed, and were moving by first light. They stopped at midday to rest the horses and eat again, then pushed on until the light began to fail. Camp was made wherever the ground was relatively dry — in a field, beside a stream, under the lee of a hill if they were lucky. The women cooked over open fires while the men tended the animals and checked the wagons for damage. The children gathered wood and water and played in the margins of the road, the way children do even in the middle of a migration, because twelve-year-olds do not stop being twelve-year-olds just because the world is rearranging itself around them.

Karl August collected things. Stones, mostly — interesting ones, unusually shaped or colored — which he kept in his coat pockets until they wore holes in the lining and his mother made him throw them out. He collected feathers, too, and once a spent musket ball he found in a ditch near what must have been a battlefield, though there was no marker and no one could say which battle or which war. He showed it to Johann Michael, who turned it over in his palm and said, "There are enough of those where we're going. Don't keep it." Karl August kept it anyway.

Johann Michael walked mostly in silence. He was old enough to feel the weight of what they were doing — old enough to understand that his father was fifty-seven and his grandmother was seventy-five and that the journey itself might kill them, that people died on these migrations from dysentery and fever and simple exhaustion, that the wagons broke and the horses went lame and the food ran out before the road did. He watched the country change around them — the flat Polish farmland giving way to gentle hills, then steeper hills, then the long, rolling uplands of the Galician borderlands — and he tried to imagine what lay on the other side of all of it.

They crossed rivers whose names no one in the *Kolonne* knew. They passed through villages where the language changed from Polish to Ukrainian to something else — Moldavian, perhaps — and the churches went from Catholic to Orthodox, their onion domes strange and beautiful against the sky. They bought bread when they could and shot rabbits when they couldn't. One of the wagons broke an axle outside a town whose name Karl August never learned, and the whole column stopped for two days while a local wheelwright made the repair and charged them triple for it.

Anna Elisabeth endured. She did not complain, because complaining was not something she did, and because she understood — better than any of them, better than her son, better than her grandsons — that the road was the price of arrival, and that you did not argue with the price after you had already started walking.

---

They reached Bessarabia in late summer, after nearly four months on the road.

The steppe announced itself gradually, then all at once. The hills flattened. The trees thinned and then vanished. The sky, which had been a ceiling, became a dome — enormous, pale, pressing down on a landscape so flat and featureless that the horizon was a perfect unbroken line in every direction. The grass was waist-high and silver-green in the August heat, and it moved in the wind like water, rolling in long slow waves that made Karl August dizzy if he watched too long.

There was nothing else. No fences, no roads, no buildings, no trees. Just grass and sky and the sound of insects and the hot, dry wind that blew from the southeast and tasted like dust and distance.

Karl August stood in the back of the wagon and looked out over the steppe and felt, for the first time in his life, genuinely afraid. Not of anything specific — there was nothing to be afraid of, which was precisely the problem. There was *nothing*. The landscape offered no shelter, no shade, no landmark by which you could orient yourself. It was the largest emptiness he had ever seen, and they were going to live in it.

Johann Michael stood beside him. He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "We'll need to dig a well."

It was such a practical thing to say — so ordinary, so much like their father — that Karl August laughed, and the laugh broke something open in him, and after that the steppe did not seem quite so terrifying.

---

Russian officials met the *Kolonne* at a staging point near the Kogylnik River and directed them to their assigned parcels. The colony had been laid out according to plan — a central road with farm lots extending back on either side, each family receiving their sixty-six hectares of treeless steppe. The settlement was called Kulm, after the battle where the allied armies had defeated Napoleon's forces in August 1813\. It had been officially established the year before, in 1814, by the first wave of Warsaw Colonists, but when the Isaaks arrived, there was almost nothing to show for it — a few sod houses, some freshly broken ground, a well or two, and a wooden cross marking where the church would eventually stand.

---

The government provided what it had promised: money for building materials, seed for the first planting, a daily food allowance until the harvest came in. It was not much. The houses the colonists built that first year were *Erdhütten* — earth huts, dug partially into the ground, with walls of sod bricks and roofs of bundled steppe grass. They were dark, damp, and cold in winter. They leaked when it rained. They were home.

Johann Michel Isaak was fifty-seven years old. He had left Soest as a young man, built a farm in Poland, lost it to Napoleon, and walked a thousand miles to build another one in a country where the wind never stopped and there were no trees to break it. He would spend the rest of his life here, on this steppe, in this colony named after a battle he had not fought in, growing wheat in soil he had never seen before, speaking German to his neighbors and Russian to the officials and praying in a church that did not yet exist.

There was a saying among the Bessarabian Germans, passed down from the first generation to the last: *Die ersten fanden den Tod, die zweiten die Not, erst die dritten das Brot.*

The first generation found death. The second found need. Only the third found bread.

The Isaaks had just arrived. The dying had not yet begun. But the bread — the bread was three generations away, and every one of those generations would earn it.
