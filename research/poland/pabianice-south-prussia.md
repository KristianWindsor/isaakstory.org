# Südpreußen: Pabianice and the Łódź Region under Prussian Rule

## Südpreußen
Pabianice periodically became part of the partitioning powers. After the second partition of Poland in 1793-1807, the town found itself within the province the partitioning power called South Prussia (Südpreussen). A statistical and topographical description prepared by the Prussian authorities at that time stated that Pabianice had 75 buildings, 27 of which were shingled and 48 thatched. There were 39 undeveloped plots. The town also contained 64 barns, attesting to its agricultural character. Pabianice's population was 482, including 15 Jewish. Twenty-nine residents were engaged in crafts. In addition to the parish church, the town also housed an elementary school, a wooden jail, a brewery, and three inns.

The great fire that broke out in Pabianice in 1797 wreaked significant havoc on the town. It destroyed the Church of the Holy Cross and the hospital for the poor, 14 houses, eight barns, and numerous other buildings and farm equipment. The period of Prussian rule did not favor Pabianice's development.

The city became so impoverished that the Kalisz Consistory, seeking the construction of a Protestant church in 1803, declared that "it is impossible to demand anything from the city of Pabianice, because its maintenance is so meager that they cannot make any savings." There was even a proposal to revoke Pabianice's city rights. The administration showed no concern for the city. However, the Prussian government initiated and financially supported agricultural colonization, and to some extent industrial colonization. As part of this effort, several families settled in Pabianice, although it is difficult to determine exactly where they came from or whether they settled permanently in the city. ("Dzieje Pabianice," Łódź 1968)

Administratively, Pabianice was part of the province of South Prussia. Middle-level units were departments, and lower-level units were counties. Initially, Pabianice was part of the Piotrków Department, but soon, following the administrative reorganization carried out after the Third Partition, it found itself in the Kalisz Department and became part of the Sieradz County.

The department was also divided into tax inspection offices (cameras) that oversaw the towns. Pabianice was subordinated to officials from the Piotrków tax office. In 1796, the Pabianice estates of the Kraków chapter became the property of the Prussian treasury.

## The Newcomers

People from German lands began arriving in Pabianice with the beginning of Prussian rule. The parish register, dated August 21, 1793, records the baptism of Anna Marianna, daughter of Christian and Anna Marianna Sitkow, newcomers from Prussia. The registers, dated July 30 and August 4, 1797, respectively, record the baptism and burial of the child of Christian and Marianna Rexer, who were labeled heretics, meaning they were not of the Catholic faith. The family of Jan Henryk and Krystyna Ufman was associated with the brewery at the Pabianice manor house, and their son was born in early August 1797. Another entry, dated May 24, 1800, concerns two-year-old Bogumił, son of Karol Haux and Marianna. Soon after, death also struck 33-year-old Marianna Hauxowa, commonly known as Garbarka, who was buried on June 21, 1800. This name indicates the occupation of the Haux family. That same month, Jan Bether and Agnieszka's over three-year-old daughter died. It's worth noting that this was a mixed marriage, as Jan was a Protestant and his wife a Catholic. In September 1800, death claimed the lives of two minor daughters, those of Jan and Anna Keler (or Heler).

With the seizure of the Pabianice estate by the occupying power, Prussian administration was established. The term "Amtus Pabianicensis" began to be used in parish records. The office of revenue recorder at the Pabianice office ("in Amtu Pabianicensi") was held by Samuel Godfryd Szymink from the town of Neywedel in Neymark, a Protestant who died on June 4, 1800, at the age of approximately 26. Jan Schultz, intendant of the Pabianice office, died in the Pabianice manor on July 4, 1801. He was buried in the cemetery designated for Protestant burials, located next to the Church of the Holy Cross. The next Prussian official in the Pabianice office was Jan Gotlob Wolmar, supervising the judiciary, mentioned together with his wife Charlotte Fryderyka on December 23, 1801, in connection with the death of their son, who was just over one year old.

Entries from August 1800 in the marriage register indicate a group of newcomers from Prussia and other German lands residing in Pabianice. These included Kwast, Matner, Erhart, Helner, Boethner, Winkler, and Weylich. Protestants with Polish-sounding surnames also appeared, such as Musiałkiewicz from Strzelno, mentioned in September 1801, alongside Cyncer (Cyner), who came from the same town. Rexer and Weimar were also mentioned at the same time. Further entries, such as one from January 1805, mention Kurc and Laps, further Protestants.

The Germans' connection with Pabianice was often short-lived, sometimes serving as a stopover on a longer journey. This is clearly illustrated by the note from August 22, 1803, about the death of nine-month-old Małgorzata, daughter of Franciszek Pall, a colonist passing through Pabianice.

After the Second Partition, Prussian troops arrived. On July 1, 1793, the daughter of Józef Krędzierski, a soldier of the Kingdom of Prussia, was baptized.

Prussia's defeat in the war with France in 1806 ended Prussian rule in the Polish lands occupied during the Second and Third Partitions. Under the terms of the Peace of Tilsit of [1807], these lands became part of the newly established Duchy of Warsaw. (R. Adamek and T. Nowak, "650 lat Pabianice. Studia i szkice", Pabianice 2005)

## The End of the "Pabian State"

The dissolution of the Polish state also brought an end to the "Pabianice State," meaning the property of the Kraków Chapter with its capital in Pabianice. However, the Chapter did not give up. M. Baruch wrote: In 1793, Prussian troops entered the borders of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and occupied the area up to the Pilica River. Thus, the Pabianice estates came into Prussian possession. The Grodno Sejm was to authorize the annexation by formally ceding the occupied territory. Fearing for its property, the Kraków Chapter sent a plea to King Stanisław August, requesting that the Prussian government reserve compensation for the Pabianice estate in the ongoing negotiations.

"Seven hundred years have passed," the letter reads, "and we now possess the considerable estate of Pabianice, situated in the Sieradz and Łęczyca voivodeships, granted to us by Władysław Herman and Judith, his wife, Polish monarchs. These revenues alone support the Church's most important needs and daily gifts."

In response from Grodno on October 9th, following the signing of the treaty with Prussia on September 25th, the king pledged his support to the chapter in the matter of compensation for the loss of property. The full text of this document is as follows:

"Most Reverend Cathedral Chapter of Krakow. It is not without heartbreaking feelings, and this sad one from the WMC. I am receiving news that their ancient chapter, well-deserved for their country, is losing its most significant endowment under Prussian rule. I consider it just that such a great WMC loss in other clerical estates remaining in Poland and left to the Commonwealth's discretion, should be sufficiently compensated. A general project of our clergy is now being developed, encompassing our interests, where the WMC and your chapter are not forgotten." Both out of their royal duties and out of their favorable intentions, they will sincerely strive to ensure that their chapter receives, if possible, a full compensation for the loss they have suffered. I thank you kindly for your prayers to God for me, and I also ask for your continued prayers, which, if ever, I am needed especially in these critical circumstances. Having said that, I wish you all the best from the bottom of my heart from God. – Stanisław August Król.”

Bishop Kossakowski, to whom the chapter also expresses its gratitude for his effective and sensitive advocacy, made particular efforts at the Grodno Sejm to secure indemnity for the Pabianice estate.

The result of these efforts was a parliamentary resolution, which in Polish translation reads as follows: "Since the Kraków chapter, due to the transfer of its property to the possession of His Royal Majesty the King of Prussia, in accordance with the cession treaty, will lose almost all of its inheritance after four years, we therefore decide that His Excellency the Bishop of Livonia, as administrator of the property belonging to the Kraków bishopric, after four years shall pay to the Kraków chapter from the income of the episcopal estates under his administration, an equivalent of the amount the chapter loses, and this within the time limit specified in the treaty with Prussia; we entrust the strict execution of this to the State Treasury Commission, according to the list of income in lease contracts from recent years, which are to be verified by this Commission against the actual state of affairs."

Thus, the Pabianice estate was to remain in the possession of the chapter until 1797, after which it became the property of the Prussian treasury, and, in accordance with the above-mentioned resolution, the chapter was granted a monetary equivalent equal to the income from the lease, which sum was to be paid from the income of the Kraków bishopric's estate.

For a more complete understanding, it should be explained that the Four-Year Sejm, in its Constitution of July 27, 1789, passed a resolution to seize the Kraków bishopric's property for military funds. From then on, the Treasury Commission was responsible for managing the proceeds from the administration of these properties.

However, before the four-year deadline expired, Poland was divided for the third time ([1795]). Along with Lesser Poland, the former property of the Kraków bishopric passed to Austria, and this was under the control of the Austrian treasury, to which the chapter now felt compelled to appeal for compensation, as stipulated by the Grodno Constitution.

Meanwhile, the Prussian government communicated to the chapter the Prussian king's rescript of July 28, 1796, regarding the incorporation of clergy and starosty property, as well as a letter from the royal chamber in Piotrków dated October 10 of that year implementing the aforementioned rescript. In December, the Piotrków chamber took possession of the Pabianice estate from the administrator. The chamber even demanded a refund of its income for the previous six months.

Seeing itself deprived of its inheritance, the chapter sent pleading letters to Baron de Margelik, governor of Galicia, asking him to intercede with the Austrian monarch for indemnification, either according to the constitution of the Grodno Sejm, i.e., from the estates formerly owned by the Kraków bishopric, or from the estates belonging to the clergy of South Prussia and located within the Austrian state. In these letters, the chapter presents a picture of the material losses associated with the loss of the estates, citing the economic development (two towns and 50 villages, generating a net annual income of 30,000 Rhenish złoty), the expenditures made (expensive geometric measurements), and the natural resources (iron ore deposits, abundant water and forests) of the confiscated estate.

When the appeals yielded no results, in 1797 the chapter delegated Chancellor Gawroński to seek the desired compensation. The delegate traveled to Vienna, sparing no effort and effort in dealing with the ruling circles. The chapter, for its part, missed no opportunity to redress its wrongdoing. When asked by the Austrian government whether the number of canons should be reduced, the chapter made its response dependent on the size of the indemnity fund for lost property.

Finally, after exhausting all resources, a petition was submitted in 1798 in the name of Emperor Francis II. In it, the chapter fervently requests temporary compensation in the amount previously distributed among the canons from the Pabianice revenues, namely 2,000 Polish złoty, or 500 Rhine złoty per week. It also cites the emperor's promise of indemnity given at a hearing to deputies from the Prussian clergy, who held estates within Austrian borders. This refers to the presentation of all the estates of Galicia, newly incorporated into the Rakusz monarchy.

However, the chapter with the Austrian government achieved nothing. The excuse, undoubtedly, was that the Pabianice estates had been seized by the Prussian government, to which compensation should be sought, and that the Habsburg government did not consider the resolutions of the Grodno Sejm on this matter binding on itself.

In fact, the next step was to appeal to the actual occupier. On February 18, 1799, the chapter sent a memorial in French to the Prussian king, demanding indemnity for the confiscated property. The memorial was sent to Prussia through the Eastern Galician administration, but predictably, it was unsuccessful. Perhaps the chapter wanted diplomatic negotiations to begin between the two governments; by thus bringing the issue to a wider audience, it was easier to negotiate a deal. But their hopes were deceived: despite all their strenuous efforts, despite the righteousness of their cause, the chapter received no compensation for the property worth millions.

While the Prussian government, citing the wording of the Grodno Treaty, rejected the demand for indemnity for the landed estate, it nevertheless recognized the justification for the claims regarding the value of the property left behind. In 1798, the chapter wrote to Count Hoym, the minister in charge of South Prussia, requesting reimbursement of the value of the movables left behind in the Pabianice estate, the costs incurred in preparing measurements and geometric plans, and the return of income for the year and four months by which the estates had been transferred to the Prussian government prematurely, or by the deadline specified in the Grodno Treaty.

After five years of efforts, the Prussian government granted only claims for the return of the value of movable property left behind in the Pabianice estate. Indeed, in 1803, Canon Hołowczyc received 30,000 złoty from the Prussian government, which the canons promptly divided among themselves as "extraordinary income" (titulo extraordinariae consolationis).

## Prussians in the Region

### Piotrków Trybunalski

Michał Rawita-Witanowski: (…) Piotrków found itself under Prussian rule, whose troops, under Mollendorf's command, entered the city on April 12, 1793. Immediately, on the same day, by a decree dated Piotrków, King Frederick announced the annexation of the entire Sieradz Voivodeship under his rule.

At the behest of the new authorities, on the 18th quarter of the current year, the landowners gathered in Sieradz to appoint deputies authorized to swear allegiance to the King of Prussia. The homage ceremony was to take place on May 4th in Poznań, with the Sieradz deputies instructed to "present a humble petition for the continuation of the landowners' courts."

In that same year, 1793, the Prussian monarch visited the newly conquered land for the first time. According to the "Berlińska Gazette," which contains the official description of this journey, on October 25th, King Frederick William arrived in Piotrków via Rawa and Ujazd, where he arrived at 10 a.m. "The local Jews," the diary says, "sent 30 young men of their own nation to meet His Majesty, disguised as Cossacks, and erected a triumphal gate in the Jewish part of the city, at which the King deigned to accept a small poem from the Jewish elders." The following day, the King hosted a dinner and, in the evening, a ball in the town hall, attended by 500 people from the city and the surrounding nobility. On the morning of October 28th, the King left Piotrków and traveled via Pławno to Częstochowa.

From the very beginning of Prussian rule, the Chamber and Regency, existing in Łęczyca, were relocated to Piotrków due to the lack of suitable premises. And if we recall that, according to the administrative arrangements of the time, each Department had two magistracies: the Chamber for administration and the Regency for the courts, it turns out that Piotrków was also the original seat of the Department. This is confirmed by the great seal, impressed on a German document dated September 5, 1795, signed by Commander Reuss, with the inscription around its rim: "Süd Preussiche Regierung zu Petrikau."

Only in 1796, due to the proximity of the Austrian border on the Pilica River (Sulejów-Podklasztorze), the Regency and the Camera were moved to Kalisz by order of the Prussian minister von Voss.(..)

Ultimately, the so-called South Prussia was divided into three Departments: Warsaw, Poznań, and Kalisz. The latter, covering 228.5 square miles, consisted of 14 counties, or "landrace districts" (landräthliche kreise), among which was Piotrków County. There were also three tax inspectorates: Kalisz, Sieradz, and Piotrków, and numerous forest districts (forstämter), including Piotrków County.

The second level of administrative authority after the Kameras in the counties with executive power and the right to care for peasants were the Landrats, with district councilors (kraisrath) assigned to help them – to supervise the cities.

As for the judiciary, the lower magistrates, dependent on the regency, were the Judicial Commissions in the counties (kreis-Justiz – Kommissionen), from which, by order of November 16, 1795, criminal cases were removed and "Inquisitorates" were established, which dealt with investigations.

Beneath the seemingly benign form of the regulations, edicts, patents, and other "papers" that were showered upon the city, replete with assurances of His Royal Majesty's "fatherly care" for his dear and faithful subjects in the newly annexed provinces, the exclusive nature of state goals and the increasingly tightening tax clampdown clearly emerge. These regulations, "published" in two languages, transformed Polish into vulgar jargon, impossible to understand without the German text. Phrases such as: "Announcement regarding the burial of the dead and the digging of graves according to the regulations 3 cubits deep" or "patent... regarding taxes on those living on music"—or finally, "Announcement... regarding the decent use of stamp paper"—are masterpieces of linguistic correctness, compared to the sloppiness that permeates the texts of other government regulations.

Piotrków's administration, as a royal city, was limited in its rights and became completely dependent on the Kalisz Chamber. By an edict of 18 April 1794 and an ordinance of 19 March 1795, the city was stripped of the right to elect lay judges. From then on, they were appointed by the regency. Pursuant to a decree of 28 September 1795, a police mayor, nominated by the Chamber, began to function. In Piotrków, this position was held by Antoni Wicini, who held the title of "Director."

By undermining local government in this way, the city was left to the exploitation of German officials. And their hordes completely flooded Greater Poland. To bolster the German element, the Prussian government brought nearly 9,000 of its officials to the occupied provinces. They swarmed into the former starostys, then called "amts," and the estates confiscated from the clergy, or "domains," by Prussian officials. Brandenburg peasants were even brought in from the Spree to perform public works, and a whole host of colonists migrated to the Vistula and Warta rivers.

Foreigners were enticed to settle in the cities with various privileges. In Piotrków, where skilled craftsmen were plentiful, German newcomers were granted free consent, a three-year discount on consumption tax, three-year tax exemption, and exemption from military service. One need only peruse the Piotrków guild records to see the sheer scale of the influx of German craftsmen to our city. (Michał Rawita-Witanowski, "Monograph of Piotrków Trybunalski," 2017)

### Łódź

Henryk Stanisław Dinter: (…) In 1793, as soon as the spring thaw had subsided and the ruts on the roads had dried, Prussians appeared in the vicinity of Łódź and in the town. They passed through in small detachments, exploring the new domains of their king. They did not fraternize with the local population, keeping their distance.

However, the first encounters dispelled the understandable anxiety of the people of Łódź: the Prussians did not raid their homes, nor did they requisition anything. They took forage, grain, cattle, and poultry from the manor. They paid at the inn.

After a brief lull, in the summer, soldiers reappeared with some "pludrakers," wandering the furrows, drawing on papers, measuring, and examining. Rumors began to spread, and panic ensued when several officials, along with an interpreter, arrived at the mayor's house in Prussian carts. They ordered a City Council meeting to be convened. They ruled that meetings could not be held in the mayor's house, so they moved the meeting to an inn in the market square, and only then did they read to the councilors the deed stating that Frederick William II, by the grace of God the reigning King of Prussia, had taken possession of these lands. At the same time, they ordered the mayor to repeat this to the populace and officially proclaim Łódź a city of Southern Prussia.

Prussian officials then began their interrogation, carefully noting everything the mayor and councilors told them. This written interrogation paints a picture of Łódź at the time. (…)

Minister Voss was considering in Berlin whether Łódź should remain a city. We quote the content of the correspondence on this matter. A letter from the Piotrków Chamber to Minister Voss dated December 23, 1793: "[…] This city is of very little importance. Since all improvements require capital, it seems premature to plan anything now, given the great poverty of the citizens and the lack of any internal sources of income. First of all, a municipal treasury should be established, the capital of which would be provided by better utilization of the large and beautiful municipal forest through the establishment of a glassworks or similar enterprise."

Letter from Minister Voss to the Piotrków Trybunalski Chamber dated April 22, 1794: "[…] The main issue has not been examined: whether it would not be possible and whether it would not be better to change Łódź into a village? In this case, establishing a fund would not be necessary, and it would only be about better utilization of the forest without any facilities requiring initial capital. Since farming and cattle breeding are already the main source of income for the inhabitants, among whom there are only four craftsmen, who are actually needed in the city, it would be necessary, first of all, to disclose the unspecified amount of land. If this amount is sufficient to support the inhabitants, you will treat both the Dominion and the citizens about incorporating the village into the rural district and inform me of the result."

The Polish population of the incorporated territories had much difficulty navigating the new administrative divisions and the intricacies of Prussian bureaucracy in general. The new system of government was radically different from that of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. Prussia was a bureaucratic-police state, ruled centrally by the king. The king often regulated even the smallest matters in remote corners of the country. This was the case, for example, with the determination of the salary of a watchman in the Łódź Magistrate. Unable to resolve this minor matter on their own, the king finally established the watchman's salary in 1804, after lengthy correspondence between the Prussian "amts."

(…) Most distressing for the Polish population, accustomed to political freedom and unaccustomed to state bureaucracy, was the restriction of all aspects of life by Prussian officials. For example, a bishop could not conduct a church visitation without the assistance of a special official; the City Council could not pass a single resolution without the police mayor, appointed by the Prussian authorities, a German. Absolutely everything had to be coordinated and recorded.

Shortly after the occupation of Polish lands, the German language began to be introduced, and Germans were appointed to official positions. These were not yet manifestations of the ethnic oppression employed by the Prussians during Bismarck's time, but the Polish subjects of the Prussian king were not granted all the rights enjoyed by Germans.

In terms of social structure, the Prussian state did not introduce any fundamental changes, continuing to cultivate the estate system, but intervening in intra-estate relations and the balance of power between the estates. Peasants were under the state's protection, as recruits were drawn from among them. Townspeople had more limited rights than those established by the Constitution of May 3rd. The state was supreme, so even a nobleman was not guaranteed the inviolability of his person and home. Printing was subject to strict censorship.

With respect to the Jewish population, the Prussian regime brought about state-legal discrimination, at least to such a degree unknown in Poland. The Prussian state considered the growing Jewish population to be economically detrimental.

This attitude of the Prussian authorities characterizes the legal norm of 1750, which governs the treatment of Jews. After the First Partition, the Prussians extended this law to the seized lands. According to this law, Jews were divided into "protected" and "others." Only the "protected" had the right to reside in Prussia; the "others" were to be expelled. Among the "protected," the law distinguished between "ordinary" and "extraordinary." "Ordinary" had the right to reside in cities and could pass this "privilege" on to one of their children. "Extraordinary" had the right to reside only for themselves. The granting of broader rights depended on the amount of a Jew's wealth. The law prohibited Jews from engaging in guild crafts, engaging in peddlery, and engaging in certain other trades.

Immediately after the first partition, only Jews who could demonstrate a net worth of 1,000 thalers were granted the right to reside; the poor were generally expelled from Prussia. Jews remaining in the Prussian state were prohibited from living in the countryside and had to move to cities. In some private Polish towns, there was once an explicit ban on Jewish settlement, or, as in Łódź, there was no such ban, but the owner's hostile attitude toward the Jewish population made it difficult for them to settle in the city. The Prussians immediately lifted these bans and restrictions.

The Prussian government essentially continued to maintain feudal ownership, but with respect to the property of the Catholic Church, it pursued a special policy dictated not so much by socio-economic considerations as by political and state considerations. First, the Prussian authorities imposed taxes on church property at a rate of 50% of income, and in 1796, after the third partition, the Prussian state took over the clergy's property. In compensation, the Church was paid approximately 50% of the income assessed by the state commission.

In order to bind the annexed lands to Prussia, the Prussian government began to support German colonization in these areas. German forces were attracted from all German states, not just Prussia. Settlers were provided with favorable, protective conditions. Significant amounts of land were distributed among the Prussian nobility, but above all, efforts were made to attract peasants and townspeople. Colonists were paid bonuses per acre of cleared land, land was given hereditary possession, and subsidies were granted. The government paid the colonists substantial sums for clearing forests and for development, and exempted them from taxes and military service for several years.

Colonists poured in from all corners of the German lands. This settlement didn't bring any new inhabitants to Łódź itself; the town was too unattractive; it was better to clear the forests and establish a new life than to enter the old and unattractive stagnation.

However, the area around Łódź was becoming increasingly populated by colonists. They were mostly farmers, but there were also a number of craftsmen and weavers, as well as organizers of the forestry industry. The industrious colonists transformed vast expanses of forest around Łódź into arable fields, and their craftsmanship and greater consumer needs than those of Polish peasants living in serfdom contributed to a revival of trade in the area. (...)

Despite a vibrant economy and better conditions than during the bishops' rule, the citizens of Łódź were not satisfied with Prussian rule. Regardless of their deep-seated animosity toward Germans, who were considered alien and always hostile to Poles, Prussian rule was repelled by its bureaucracy and the abuses and arbitrariness of Prussian officials, which were ubiquitous throughout the annexed country. Frederick William III himself, when he observed the practices of his officials during his stay in Warsaw, told his ministers that "a significant portion of Prussian officials abuse their power for violence, instead of using it to defend the oppressed."

But the Prussian king himself fared no better. He gifted crown estates from Polish provinces to Prussian dignitaries or filled them with Prussian tenants—thus driving Poles from these estates. The new owners and tenants oppressed the peasants, treating them like draught cattle. The king, however, clearly declared himself against the enfranchisement of the peasants.

The entire population felt the acute impact of the Prussian kingdom's militarism. Although Prussia was not at war, the conscription rate was increased tenfold compared to the former Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth norms, and local administrations sometimes quadrupled the enlistment. (…)

The citizens of Łódź, like all Poles, expected change from the West. News of Napoleon's change of government, shifting borders, and granting new laws reached Łódź. As usual, the news embellished reality and painted the future in alluring colors. (Henryk Stanisław Dinter, "The Story of a Great Career. Łódź 1332-1860," Łódź 1965)

### Rydzyna

Prussian colonists from the Pabianice area made their mark by clearing forests that had once belonged – during the Piast dynasty – to the so-called Łódź Forest, which included, among others, the present-day Karolewski Forest, Rydzyński Forest, Pabianice Municipal Forest, and Tuszyński Forest. Henryk Dinter wrote: Conditions were not favorable for settlement. The forest lay far from major rivers and trade routes, and its soils did not promise favorable conditions for agricultural development, being predominantly sandy, in places clayey, with occasional bogs and floodplains.

Dense pine forests grew on barren sands, dotted with islands of larch. The semi-sandy and clayey lands were covered with mixed forests with dense stands of linden, maple, ash, and occasional beech. Oaks and hornbeams rose in the clayey areas, densely underlain by hazel, raspberry, and blackberry bushes. Birches, alders, and willows, known as rokit, grew in the numerous marshes and over the floodplains. Wild animals reigned supreme in the forests; bison and aurochs grazed peacefully, bears made undivided use of the forest beehives, lynxes and wildcats spread terror among the countless throngs of hares, herds of deer waded in the forest ponds and floodplains, and moose roamed the quagmires. The forest was home to numerous herds of deer, wild goats, boars, foxes, and wolves, and a variety of birds, amphibians, and reptiles nested there. The names of districts or neighborhoods of present-day Łódź remain from these forest times, such as Glinki, Dąbrowa, Rokicie, Retkinia, Lipiny, Niedźwiedź, Żabieniec, Żubardź, Kurak, and so on.

Already at the end of its rule (1791), the Kraków Chapter brought in Prussian grubbers, the so-called Olęders (from the German word Hauländer, meaning "grubber"), to acquire agricultural land. A list of the first 21 colonists, the founders of the oldest part of the Rydzyna colony, is known. They were: Gottlob Moritz, Johann Schreier, Johann Klause, Adalbert Kruschel, Christian Klatt, Christian Heck, Christian Birke, Gottlieb Beyer, Adalbert Schultz, Martin Schiffler, Johann Moritz, Gottlieb Schultz, Christoph Passein, Johann Mund, Johann Bentsch, Christian Pudrycki, Samuel Giehl, Michael Bentsch, Johann Schaneberg, Johann Heckert, and Johann Kruschel.

The new colony gradually expanded its territory, expanding east, north, and south. Buildings clustered along the present-day roads (Dolna, Długa, and Górna Streets), and cleared arable lands reached the valleys of the Dobrzynka River and its tributary, the Bychlewka River, where the Biesaga mill settlement and the "Potaźnia" manor already existed. Prussian authorities continued the colonization of the area, led by the last tenant of the "Pabia State," Paweł Olechowski. Similarly, with the participation of German settlers, the colony of Pawłowice-Olędry (Pawlikowice) was established.

German-speaking settlers of the Protestant faith received land in perpetual ownership and were subject to rent. In Rydzyny, most land grants amounted to 35 morgens, with only Adalbert Schultz receiving 42 morgens, Samuel Giehl 36, and Johann Kruschel 34. The owner of the mill in Rydzyny (Rydzyny Muhle; a separate settlement) was Paul Maajer. At that time, the mill settlement comprised 12 morgens of land. There was also a mill settlement called Rydzynki, or Galas, which was granted a perpetual lease by the Prussians in 1805.

The most valuable memento of the Olęders is the Protestant cemetery in Rydzyny on Dolna Street. Right next to the cemetery was an elementary school that also served as a house of prayer.
