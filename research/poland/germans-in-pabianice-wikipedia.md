# Germans in Pabianice

The Germans in Pabianice (German: Pabianitz, briefly Burgstadt in 1940 ), who settled there from 1793 onwards, were part of the German minority in Poland. In 1860, they constituted 29 percent of the town's inhabitants, in 1913 this figure had fallen to 14 percent, and by 1921 it was 5.5 percent. By 1931, their share had risen to 9.8 percent.

## Beginnings of German immigration

In 1793, the year of the Prussian occupation, no one in the thirteen-member town council of Pabianice, a small town with 467 Catholics and 15 Jews, spoke German. [1] Some Germans came to the town as officials when it was incorporated into the Prussian state following the Second Partition of Poland. Samuel Gottfried Schimink was a clerk and Johannes Schulz was the intendant in the Pabianice district office. Johannes Gottlob Wolmar was responsible for the administration of justice. Few other Germans are documented at this time.

The next wave of German settlers arrived in the 1820s following the Congress of Vienna. Pabianice was now part of the Kingdom of Poland, which belonged to the Russian Empire. Due to its waterways, favorable transport routes, and abundant timber, Pabianice appeared suitable for the settlement of cloth makers and weavers. This was intended to give Pabianice the status of a factory town. The government in Warsaw sent recruiters to the German textile regions, especially Saxony, Silesia, Prussia, and the Sudetenland. On the 1824 city map, the "New Town" district was marked on the left bank of the Dobrzynka River for the incoming craftsmen. By 1826, Pabianice was home to 30 cloth makers from Germany. Initially, the newcomers were provided with rooms in the castle, the old courtyard of the Kraków chapter. The first handlooms were set up in the castle's rear buildings. Each settler later received a building plot and a loan of 600 złoty for the construction of a wooden house or 1,500 złoty for a brick house. A fulling mill for processing, densifying, and finishing fabrics was established on the Dobrzynka River.

The majority of German-speaking craftsmen gathered in the clothmakers' guild, founded in 1826. The guild's records were kept in both Polish and German, as the majority of its members were of German descent. In 1836, the clothmakers commissioned their first flag, and in 1854, a second. Only the

decorated finial of the second flag, bearing the German inscription "Es lebe die löb Meister der Weberzunft in Pabianice" (Long live the esteemed mastery of the weavers' guild in Pabianice), survives.

The board of the clothmakers' guild consisted almost exclusively of German craftsmen. Among the elders and masters during the Russian period were: Ambrosius Schulze and Gottlieb Krusche (1826–1843), Josef August Führih and Józef Nowotny (1844–1846), Antonius Dinzer and Kilkar (1847–1849), Ambrosius Hazek (1850–1853), Josef August Führih and E. Krusche (1855–1864), Vincencius Dere and Josef Schnabel (1865–1877), Nikolaus Wendler (1878–1885), Franciszek Gryzel (1887–1894), Adolf Jaroszka (1906–1909), and Adolf Hille (1910–1917).

A Prussian survey in 1793 recorded that the town had 75 houses, 27 with shingles and 48 with thatch. Under Prussian rule, the old market remained unchanged, while the new town grew rapidly. By 1803, over 200 German householders were recorded there, housing well over 1,000 people. In 1817, 250 Protestant landowners, including their families, were said to have lived within a one-mile radius, totaling 1,230 people.

An initial plan by the migrants to establish a Protestant church failed with the Prussian withdrawal in 1806. During the next attempt in 1816, it was unclear whether Pabianitz was the only suitable location for a Protestant church. In particular, Werner, a senior official who owned a distillery and held the Pabianitz spirits monopoly, was keen to prevent the establishment of a church in the town, fearing it would jeopardize his sales. He persuaded the voivodeship commission to build the church in Sulzfeld instead. At that time, Łódź was also proposed as the Protestant parish for the region. This city did not yet have any German Protestant immigrants.

## Industrial companies

### "Krusche & Ender"

The highest-earning manufacturers of German descent were the Krusche and Ender families. In 1825, the cloth maker Gottlieb Krusche (born 1769 in Reichenau) came to Pabianice from Saxony. Initially, he only had a hand-weaving mill with nine workshops for the production of cotton goods. In 1850, his son Benjamin had the first steam engine brought in. In 1865, he built a main office at 3 Zamkowa (Castle) Street. The company received numerous awards at international industrial exhibitions (in Warsaw, Moscow, and Paris) for the high quality of its products. In 1872, Karl Ender from Saxony joined Benjamin Krusche as a partner. He soon expanded the weaving mill to process cotton waste. From 1874 onward, the company used the name "Krusche & Ender." In the 1880s, the owners of the cotton manufactory received further awards at industrial exhibitions (in Saint Petersburg, Moscow,

and Warsaw). From 1896 onwards, the company had the right to use the Russian state emblem (two- headed imperial eagle) on its goods. A fine spinning mill was established in 1891, enabling the production of underwear fabrics.

In 1899, the company was transformed into the joint-stock company "Krusche & Ender" of the Pabianice Cotton Manufactories, with a share capital of 3,500,000 rubles. The founders of the joint-stock company were Karl Ender, Hermann Krusche, and Theodor Ender. A year later, the company received a gold medal at the Paris Exhibition. From 1908, the members of the company's board of directors were Theodor Ender (Chairman and General Director), Felix Krusche (Technical Director), Theodor Hadrian (Managing Director), Ludwig Knothe (Technical Director), and Alfred Scholz (Head of the Main Warehouse in Łódź). The main sales office was located in Łódź. The company also maintained warehouses in Warsaw and Russia. In 1911, the company employed 4,303 workers and produced 20,569,371 meters of fabric.

The Krusche & Ender joint-stock company established social and educational institutions for its employees. In 1869, Benjamin Krusche established the first workers' health insurance fund. Construction of workers' housing ("family houses") began in 1882 on the initiative of Auguste Ender (née Krusche). In 1895, the building on Skromna Street (now Wyszyńskiego Street) was erected, which later housed the factory's elementary school. The factory library was established in 1905, and a free bathhouse for workers was opened on Grobelna Street in 1906. The factory hospital, built in 1909 on St. Roch Street, had 50 beds and an outpatient clinic.

The company "Krusche & Ender" also played a significant role in social activities at the city level. In 1900, it donated 10,000 rubles for the construction of the Catholic church in the new town. In 1910, it agreed to contribute 4,000 rubles for the construction of the Catholic children's day care center on St. John's Street. The company donated 10,000 rubles for the construction of the city hospital between 1904 and 1907, and an additional 1,000 rubles in 1908 for the establishment of the psychiatric ward. The company also covered the deficits of the Pabianice Commercial School, contributing 51,320 rubles to the school's funds between 1899 and 1905.

In 1926, the company celebrated its 100th anniversary. On this occasion, Felix Krusche and Stefan Ender presented Polish President Ignacy Mościcki with a commemorative album containing photographs of the company. In 1930, the company "Krusche & Ender" merged with the joint-stock company "Moszczenicer Manufaktur T. Ender & Erben." During the interwar period, the company produced linen fabrics, colored linen, printed fabrics, bed and tablecloths, handkerchiefs, and velour. In 1938, the company's capital amounted to 14.875 million złoty, and the number of employees reached 4,130. That same year, construction began on a factory for the production of technical materials in Baranów near Sandomierz.

### R. Kindler

The third most important German manufacturing family was the Kindler family. Rudolf Kindler was the first member of this family to arrive in Pabianice in 1848, taking the position of manager of the dye works under Benjamin Krusche. In 1859, he became self-employed by installing several dozen looms. In 1861, he founded a hand-weaving mill for woolen and semi-woolen goods, comprising 68 looms and a dye works. As early as 1865, he participated in an industrial exhibition in Moscow, and he received a medal at the Agricultural-Industrial Exhibition in Paris. By 1879, the company employed 650 workers, thus ranking second in the wool industry in all of Congress Poland.

In 1888, Rudolf Kindler's wool manufactory decided to convert into a joint-stock company. The company adopted the name "R. Kindler Joint-Stock Company for Semi-Woolen Goods." The share capital amounted to 1,000,000 rubles. It was divided into 1,000 shares, each with a nominal value of 1,000 rubles. This was typical for a family business. The founding members were Rudolf, Ludwig, and Oskar Kindler.

Further successes came in the 1890s. At the All-Russian Industrial and Crafts Exhibition in Nizhny Novgorod in 1896, the company acquired the right to use the state coat of arms on its products. The Russian Empire formed the Kindler company's market. Around the turn of the century, the company invested in Pabianice. The facilities in the area of ​ Castle and Ogodowa Street (today Traugutta Street) were expanded. In 1913, the R. Kindler Joint-Stock Company became the market leader in the wool industry of Congress Poland.

The R. Kindler Joint-Stock Company also made efforts in the social and educational-cultural sphere by establishing corresponding institutions on the company premises. Around 1872, Rudolf Kindler founded a health insurance fund for the workers. In 1879, Julius Kindler established a fire brigade consisting of nine uniformed firefighters equipped with special gear. Around 1898, a food cooperative for civil servants was founded. In 1904, a building complex for the city hospital was erected on Boczna Street (now Żeromskiego Street). Maintaining the hospital's 45 beds cost 21,000 rubles. The hospital's chief physician was a well-known social worker, Witold Eichler. In 1906, an elementary school for workers' children began operating. Supporting both schools was a library founded in 1905, which housed 3,000 volumes.

The R. Kindler company financially supported civic initiatives in Pabianice. In 1900, the company donated 4,000 rubles for the construction of the Catholic Church of St. Mary, as well as all the oak doors for the church, which cost 3,000 rubles. In 1910, the company donated 2,000 rubles for the construction of the Catholic children's day care center. Between 1904 and 1907, it supported the construction of the municipal hospital with 9,050 rubles. Additionally, in 1908, it donated 570 rubles

for the establishment of the psychiatric ward. The R. Kindler joint-stock company also covered the deficits of the Pabianice commercial school. Between 1898 and 1905, it paid 37,692 rubles into the school's coffers.

The company's development was interrupted by the outbreak of the First World War. Losses rose to 5 million gold francs, and in Russia to 100,000 pounds sterling. During this difficult time, the company sorely missed the energetic entrepreneur Oskar Kindler, who had died in Warsaw on December 23, 1918. As a member of the State Council, he had championed the interests of industry in the Kingdom of Poland. Despite all efforts, the company was unable to recover after the war and ultimately had to declare bankruptcy. In 1927, the English firm "F. Willey & Co. Ltd." and the state treasury jointly established the Pabianic Textile Works, formerly R. Kindler, as a public limited company.

### Schweikert and Resiger

German manufacturers also played a significant role in the chemical industry. In the late 1880s/early 1890s, Ludwig Schweikert and Robert Resiger founded a chemical manufacturing plant. Emanuel Frölich soon joined them, and thanks to him, production could be expanded to include new products. The company adopted the name "Anilin- und Chemiefarbstoff und Pharmazeutische Manufaktur Schweikert & Frölich" (Aniline and Chemical Dye and Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Plant Schweikert & Frölich). From this time onward, a period of continuous development began. The pharmaceutical division was established before 1898. Eventually, the company became a focal point for Swiss capital.

In 1899, the companies Schweikert and Frölich merged with the Swiss public limited company "CIBA AG" from Basel, forming the "Pabianicer Aktiengesellschaft für Chemische Industrie" (Pabianic Public Limited Company for Chemical Industry). Its status was approved by Tsar Nicholas II. The share capital of 750,000 rubles was divided into 1,500 shares with a value of 500 rubles each. The main shareholders were Ludwig Schweikert and Emanuel Frölich.

The funds raised through the establishment of the joint-stock company enabled further investments. In 1900, construction began on a single-story main building stretching the entire length of Sachsenstraße (now Piłsudzkiegostraße). In 1910, work commenced in the department that would produce sodium sulfide and formic acid. Antichlorine production also began, and construction started on a wastewater treatment plant on the grounds of the Pliszka mill settlement. The company was among the largest chemical enterprises in Congress Poland (in terms of production value and number of employees). Its primary market was Russia, where a branch of the chemical works was established in Moscow around 1900, along with a sales office.

The company's development was interrupted by the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. The German occupiers confiscated some of the products, semi-finished goods, and machinery. Production was restricted. Ludwig Schweikert was arrested, and after his release, he left for Russia, where he died in Moscow in 1916. Production only resumed after Poland regained its independence.

During the interwar period, the "Pabianicer Aktiengesellschaft für Chemische Industrie" produced organic and synthetic pigments, chemicals, and pharmaceutical preparations. The company had sales offices in Danzig, Warsaw, Bielsko, Lviv, Poznań, and Vilnius.

### R. Saenger

The development of the paper industry in Pabianice is closely linked to the activities of manufacturers of German descent. The paper mill was founded by Leonhard Fessler in 1863. In 1874, the company was acquired by Robert Saenger from Warsaw. Initially, it produced wrapping paper for the industries of Pabianice and Łódź, as well as cardboard, tubes, and spools for spinning mills. After Robert Saenger's death in 1879, the company was taken over by Artur Steinhagen (a relative of the owner).

The company belonged to three people: Oskar Saenger, his mother Marinna Saenger, and his sister Baroness Ike-Dunikowski. The general trend toward industrial consolidation prompted the owners to establish a joint-stock company. In 1898, the company was registered as "Robert Saenger Pabianic Paper Manufactory Joint-Stock Company." The company directors were Marinna and Oskar Saenger, as well as Andreas Ike. The share capital of 750,000 rubles was divided into 1,500 shares with a value of 500 rubles each. The company employed 160 workers per shift. By 1903, the number of employees had grown to 360.

According to records from 1908, the R. Saenger company produced high-quality packaging, writing, workshop, and mouthpiece paper. The company employed 380 workers – both men and women. Until the outbreak of the First World War, the company produced 450 to 500 tons of paper per month. In addition to the products listed above, the company also manufactured excellent quality poster and album paper. The company remained undamaged between 1914 and 1918. The machinery was not dismantled by the German occupiers.

The Saenger family was keen to expand by acquiring additional paper mills. During the interwar period, the Pabianice company was merged with paper mills in Myszków and Warsaw and operated under the name "Steinhagen and Saenger Paper and Cellulose Manufactory Joint-Stock Company." The Pabianice factory was known for its good relationship between employer and workers, as well as its support of social events.

### W. Krusche

The machine industry in Pabianice originated in 1895 when Waldemar Krusche founded his factory. He came from a well-known family of manufacturers. The company owned its own iron foundry and produced parts for textile machinery and agricultural equipment. Alex Krusche became a co-owner of the machine factory and iron foundry in 1908. By 1913, the company employed 180 workers.

During the interwar period, W. Krusche's company manufactured looms and agricultural equipment (threshing and chaff cutters). The company specialized in the production of milling machines, as well as drilling and grinding machines. Further branches of the company were located in Katowice, Krakow, Poznan, and Warsaw.

## Evangelical Parish

The majority of immigrants entering Poland were of the Protestant faith. In 1827, 655 Protestants lived in Pabianice, representing 30.4% of the population. By 1848, 1,416 Lutheran residents (34.3 %) were registered, and by 1909, the number of Protestants in the town had risen to 5,591, although their share had fallen to 14.4%. In the interwar period, Protestants constituted approximately 10 % of the population (2,725 in 1921, 4,688 in 1931).

A temporary Protestant chapel was established around 1803 in the castle, which at that time served as the seat of the Prussian administration. The congregation was served by the field chaplain Friedrich Wilhelm Schramm, stationed in Petrikau. An independent Evangelical-Augsburg congregation was founded in 1818. Samuel Friedrich Jäckel was appointed the first pastor of Pabianice on September 27, 1818. From 1822 onward, the pastorate remained vacant for the next five years, and the congregation was served by neighboring village cantors and pastors. As a result, the congregation declined and had to be re-established in 1827. In 1827, the authorities counted 320 Lutheran families, 16 of whom lived in Pabianice. The parish comprised 32 villages, primarily German settlements such as Effingshausen, Hochweiler, and Königsbach. In the same year, Pastor Gottfried Hayn was elected as the parish's pastor. It was decided to build the Lutheran church on the plot of land at the Neumarkt (New Market), and its construction was completed in 1827. The shingle-roofed rectory, built in 1828, was located east of the church, near the Dobrzynka River. When it became apparent that the building was in danger of collapsing due to the marshy ground, it was recommended that the church be demolished.

After the death of the 26-year-old Pastor Hayn, Pastor Daniel Gottlieb Biedermann was installed. One of his main tasks was the completion of the church building, which was achieved at the end of 1830. Following renovations, the church was consecrated on November 25, 1832, by Superintendent Ernst Johann August von Modl from Kalisch. In the following years, the church interior was restored. In 1844, three bells were ordered from the Richter company in Kalisch. Bernhard Wohlfart from Pabianice was commissioned to build an organ. At Pastor Biedermann's instigation, manufacturer Gottfried Herwig in Pabianice donated an altarpiece depicting the Last Supper. In 1853, Gottlieb Krusche donated a marble baptismal font.

An Evangelical cemetery was established, for which the parish received land adjacent to the Catholic cemetery on the provost's land along the Dłutow road. In 1838, the Evangelical elementary school was founded, located in the parsonage. By 1843, the parish already had six schools, one of which was in the town.

Towards the end of Pastor Biedermann's tenure in 1864, the parish comprised 780 families with 3,000 souls. After his death, Pastor Wilhelm Reinhold Zimmer was elected. His greatest achievement was the renovation of the church (1875–1876). The reconstruction of the bell tower gave the church a

new facade, adorned with statues of the apostles Paul and Peter by the sculptor Alexander Pruszyński. The old wooden bell tower was dismantled and re-erected in the cemetery. The interior of the church was painted white with oil paint. With the addition of a second chancel, the church now had approximately 1,000 seats. In 1876, Rudolf Kindler donated two large mosaic windows from Saxony, which were located on either side of the altar and, framed by ornamentation, depicted the Baptism of Jesus by John and Mary at the feet of the risen Christ. The altarpiece, a work and gift from the painter Adalbert Gerson, shows Jesus in Gethsemane. In 1896, a new organ was installed by the Walter Brothers company in Gurau and dedicated on Christmas Eve. The Evangelical-Augsburg Church Choir was founded in 1867, and the brass band in 1881. Pastor Zimmer also rendered valuable service to the town by creating a public park and avenues in the mid-1870s. He further dedicated himself to education, for which the government awarded him a medal. At the end of his tenure, the parish comprised a church, six prayer houses, seventeen cemeteries, ten elementary schools, and a cantor's residence. A total of 1,100 children were educated in the cantor's residence and the schools. Zimmer's successor, Pastor Rudolf Moritz Schmidt (1896– 1938), was the author of numerous religious books in German. In 1904, the Protestant congregation numbered approximately 12,400 members, making it the largest in the country. To alleviate the large influx of worshippers at the main services, additional services had to be introduced. Thus, early morning and evening services were held. From 1911 to 1914, Eugen Engel assisted Pastor Schmidt as second pastor. Bible studies, children's services, evangelistic events, mission meetings, mission festivals, and festival weeks were also introduced. During Schmidt's tenure, numerous new institutions and associations of a religious and social nature were established. In 1899, a Protestant day care center for children between the ages of 3 and 7 was founded. In 1900, a Protestant retirement home was opened on Wodnastraße, financed by congregational donations.

In 1902, a new parsonage was built near the church on Schlossstrasse, which is considered one of the most beautiful parsonages in the country. In 1903, the Protestant congregation decided to build a community center to house the children's day care center, the cantor's school, and the confirmation hall; this was completed in 1904. Also in 1904, an Art Nouveau mausoleum was erected in the cemetery. Sophie Kindler donated it to her deceased husband, Ludwig Kindler, and endowed it with an annual stipend of 5,000 rubles. In 1911, it was handed over to the Protestant congregation as a cemetery chapel. The Protestant Young Women's Association and the Young Men's Association, headquartered on Wodnastrasse, were founded in 1904. In 1909, the Protestant Women's Association was established, whose primary goal was the care of the poor and sick.

The Protestant parish remained very active during the interwar period. In 1927, the parish celebrated its 100th anniversary. To mark this occasion, the parish council published a book about the history and activities of the Protestant community in Pabianice entitled "One Hundred Years of Divine Grace and Work on the Evangelical Lutheran Parish in Pabianice: Have They Been a Blessing to You Too?" At the end of the 1930s, the Committee of the Organization of Polish Lutherans was formed, which initiated Protestant services in Polish. (The first Polish service had been held in 1900.) From 1938 onward (after the death of Pastor Schmidt), Pastor Julius Rudolf Horn served as pastor and Erich Lembke as vicar in the parish.

## Catholic parish

Some of the immigrants were Catholic. Like the Protestants, they strove to establish their own religious organizations. In 1871, they founded the men's choir "Leo," which had its own rehearsal space at 17 Fabryczna Street (now Waryńskiego Street). By 1934, the choir had 131 members. Bernhard Reinhold was the chairman, and Bruno Arndt was the conductor. In 1883, the Catholic-German women's choir "Cäcilie" was founded. Its rehearsal space was located at 9 Poniatowski Street. The choir had 123 members. Leonhard Rensch was the chairman, and Robert Neumann was the conductor. In the 1930s, "Cäcilie" joined forces with other choirs to build its own building on Garncarske Street (now the complex of Vocational Schools No. 3).

In 1898, German Catholics, together with Poles, began construction of St. Mary's Church in Neustadt. One of the altars was donated by German-speaking Catholics, and three stained-glass windows above the main altar were donated by the Protestant factory owner Felix Krusche. Franz Jütter served as the parish priest from 1908 to 1919. In The Roman Catholic 1918, the German Catholic Association was founded. The association had 410 members. Felix Hans was the chairman, and Priest Johann Wagner was its patron.

## German culture, education system, sports

The German minority in Pabianice also established secular educational, cultural, and sports clubs and organizations. The first of these were founded during the partition of Poland.

In the 19th century, an elementary school with German as the language of instruction already existed. In 1864, it was located at Schlossstraße 6. At the beginning of the 20th century, Berthold Schulz worked there as a teacher. Later, from 1918 onward, the school functioned as a primary school. It was called Primary School No. 9.

During the First World War, the German Gymnasium in Pabianice was founded by the German Gymnasium Association. It was located at 6 St. John's Street. The school was coeducational. Its headmaster was Gotthold Hanelt. In 1932, the gymnasium moved to a newly constructed building on Legionów Street (today Primary School No. 9 on Partyzancka Street).

Among the active German associations were the sports clubs. The first was the Citizens' Rifle Guild, founded in 1852 by Germans. One of the founders of this organization was Benjamin Krusche. According to its statutes, shooting festivals were organized every year to determine the Shooting King, who received a rifleman's belt. The members of the Citizens' Rifle Guild wore green uniforms and hats with feathers. The drills and competitions were held at the shooting range (today Wolnościpark) leased from the town council. After 1914, the activities of the Citizens' Rifle Guild ceased. The club was re-established in 1927 under the name Pabianice Sports and Rifle Club. In 1928, the club members petitioned the mayor of Pabianice, Władysław Gacki, to regain possession of the shooting range. Following a lengthy legal battle, a verdict was reached that returned the shooting range to the Pabianice town council. In 1933, a shooting range was set up in the fields of the village of Karnyszewice.

The second sports club was the Pabianice Gymnastics Club, founded in 1864 by the German residents of Pabianice. The club's headquarters were located on Długastraße (today Pułaskiegostraße 36) in a building constructed specifically for gymnastics in 1907. Hermann Thommen (director of the chemical factory) was elected honorary chairman. The club had six sections. The Pabianice Gymnastics Club was an honorary member of the "Amicita" Gymnastics Club in Basel.

In 1921, the next sports club of German character, the Pabianice Sports Club "Burza," was founded, located at Orlastraße 4. By 1934, the club had 152 members. The chairman was Theofil Zobel. The club's headquarters were located at Śiętokrzyskastraße 37. The club had two sections: football and korfball.

A sports club with both German and Polish members was the sports club of the employees of the company "Krusche & Ender." The club was founded in 1922 on the initiative of the company's chairman, Richard Kanenberg. The "Krusche & Ender" club was the most important sporting institution in the city. From the beginning, the office of president was held by Felix Krusche, the chairman of "Krusche & Ender." By 1934, the club was divided into eight sections. It had a gymnasium, an indoor sports hall with a volleyball court and a running track, a ski jump and throwing facilities, as well as a 5-hectare sports field in the city center on Schlossstrasse. Besides the football stadium, there was also a sports field for various games, a six-lane running track with a length of 400 meters, ski jumps and throwing facilities, two tennis courts, a shooting range, and a covered spectator box. In addition, the sports club had a room on Pierackiegostrasse.

Among the secular associations was the Pabianice Men's Choir, founded in 1888. In 1934, it had 160 members. Julius Filtzer was chairman and Franz Pohl conductor. The Pabianice Men's Choir was located at Pierackiegostraße 3.

## Political activity

During the Partitions of Poland, the majority of Germans could be described as loyal Russian citizens. The Polish November Uprising (1830–1831) against the Russians was incomprehensible to the Germans and was seen as a rebellion against a legitimate ruler. The uprising threatened the economic livelihoods of factory owners, whose livelihoods were based on trade with the Russian Empire. Only after assimilation did some of the colonists participate in the January Uprising (1863–1864).

It turned out that the outbreak of the First World War and the German-Russian conflict were very unfavorable for German manufacturers. As a consequence of the German offensive, Pabianice fell under German rule in 1915. Due to the closure of the border with Russia, the major markets for Pabianice's industry were lost. The German manufacturer Ludwig Schweikert, who disagreed with this course of events, was captured and, after his release, forced to emigrate to Russia. He thus relocated the administration of his chemical company to Moscow.

The German occupiers appointed new authorities in the town. The industrialist Alex Krusche (1876– 1961) became mayor, and Josef Hans his deputy. The industrialist Paul Graeser became second mayor. Many Germans from Pabianice were elected to the town council, including Adolf Krusche (the chairman), Theodor Ender, Theodor Hadrian, Oskar Kindler, and Oskar Krusche. The official languages ​were German and Polish.

Right from the start of the occupation, a systematic destruction of the factories began, with machinery, semi-finished products, and finished goods being transported to Germany. Those companies owned by Germans suffered the greatest losses; for example, at the Krusche & Ender company, materials and raw materials worth $1,950,000 were confiscated without compensation. In 1916, workers were captured in the streets and later deported to Germany as forced laborers. In February 1917, reflecting his disagreement with the policies of the German occupiers, Alex Krusche resigned from his position as mayor. He was replaced by Dankwerst, an officer in the German army, who held the position until October 1917.

The German occupation of Pabianice ended in November 1918. The stationed German soldiers were disarmed by Polish independence organizations.

During the interwar period, German factory owners were loyal to the Polish government and enjoyed its support. The Germans of Pabianice were active in their own political organizations and participated in municipal and parliamentary elections. Among the stronger parties was the German People's Association, whose chairman in Pabianice was Bernhard Horn. In the 1930 elections, the German electoral bloc won a seat in the Senate (the upper house of the Polish parliament) for Josef Spickermann.

The situation deteriorated in the 1930s. After 1933, hostilities arose between Poles and Germans due to the growing nationalism of both ethnic groups. Before the outbreak of World War II, there were National Socialist groups in Pabianice. Besides the German People's Association, there was also the Young German Party in Pabianice, whose chairman was Emil Schmidt. In 1939, the nationalist

activists were arrested, and the German Gymnasium (high school), the Pabianice Gymnastics Club, and the German People's Association were dissolved. On September 8, Pabianice was occupied by the Wehrmacht. Alex Krusche became mayor again in 1939.

In 1942, the German Wehrmacht liquidated a ghetto established in Piabance for 9,000 Jews. The Krusche and Ender factory served as a collection point, and 5,600 Jews deemed fit for work were transported to the Łódź Ghetto. 150 Jewish patients of the Jewish hospital, as well as other Jews who resisted, were murdered. [2]
