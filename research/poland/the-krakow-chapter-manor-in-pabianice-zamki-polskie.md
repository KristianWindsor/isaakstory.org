# Pabianice — The Court of the Kraków Chapter

## History of the Castle

The first residence of the Kraków Chapter’s regents—originally a wooden structure—existed on this site as early as the Middle Ages. Mentioned by Jan Długosz in his *Liber Beneficiorum Dioecesis Cracoviensis*, the building likely began as a solitary defensive tower surrounded by a moat and a palisade. Probably during the 15th century, Canon Bernard Lubrański (d. 1499)—who held the local estates—erected a defensive wall and a magnificent gatehouse tower, though the manor complex remained predominantly wooden. Further work to enhance the structure's defenses and grandeur was undertaken in the early 16th century; for instance, during the tenure of Mikołaj Krzycki (d. 1504), the tower was fitted with a clock, and somewhat later, Jan Latalski (d. 1536) built a new drawbridge leading to the castle island. At that time, the building comprises at least seven chambers, a common room for the servants, and a room with a fireplace.

Despite some investments in expanding and furnishing the existing manor, the chapter recognized the need to replace it with a masonry structure; consequently, a brickyard was established behind St. Anne’s Church in 1531 to supply the project. The direction of development chosen by the canons was validated by the great city fire of 1532, which caused the medieval manor to burn down and suffer partial destruction. It was rebuilt as a wooden structure, though it incorporated certain elements of permanent masonry construction. The decision to erect a completely new masonry building stemmed from the initiative of Stanisław Dąbrowski (d. 1575), who envisioned it serving not only as his residence but also as the administrative headquarters for the cathedral stewards and a secure repository for valuables.

> **Stanisław Dąbrowski**
> 
> He was likely born in 1501 into a wealthy noble family in Dąbrowa, in the Sieradz region. In his youth, he studied at the Jagiellonian University and in Padua, where he earned a doctorate in both canon and civil law. Upon returning from sunny Italy, he embarked on a rapid ascent toward ecclesiastical titles and honors. He served as archdeacon of Gniezno, Łęczyca, and Kurzelów; provost of Wolbórz; scholasticus of Wojnicz and Moskorzów; and canon of Gniezno and Kujawy—becoming a canon of Kraków in 1554. He twice administered the Diocese of Gniezno, sat in the General Sejm in Piotrków, served as a delegate to the Synod of Piotrków, and was selected by the Gniezno chapter to attend a gathering regarding the Council of Trent.
> 
> At the peak of his ecclesiastical career, he assumed the roles of administrator of the Pabianice estates and leaseholder of the Pabianice property; he was the primary initiator of the construction of a new fortified residence, a fact evidenced by the inclusion of coats of arms bearing the initials "S.D." above the windows of the new building, alongside the arms of the Kraków Chapter. In 1573, due to advanced age and illness, Stanisław Dąbrowski stepped down from managing the Pabianice estates. A year later, he was delegated to attend the coronation of Henry III of Valois alongside the archbishop, representing the Gniezno Chapter. He died following a brief illness on January 2, 1575. His remains were laid to rest in Wawel Cathedral, where two monuments dedicated to him can still be seen today: one in the Maciejowski Chapel and the other in front of the Vasa Chapel.

A new manor house was built between 1565 and 1571. Its design and construction were overseen by Wawrzyniec Lorek—possibly an Italian—who received a fee of 50 florins and a quantity of goods in kind (presumably grain) for his work. He was assisted in the interior decoration by local craftsmen known from surviving records: the painter Melchior (responsible for the decoration of the great hall), the carpenter Hanusz, and the stonemason Jerzy from Lutomiersk. By 1571, the building was ready for use, delighting its patrons with the quality of its craftsmanship and its elegant form—which drew inspiration from distinguished local models such as the Cloth Hall in Kraków and the town hall in Sandomierz. Shortly after construction was completed, the Chapter took steps to fortify the Pabianice stronghold; the manor was enclosed by a wooden palisade and an extensive system of moats and ditches. A castle guard was also established, comprising at least ten mounted men. The purpose of this guard—as argued at the time—was not so much to resist a military force (against which it would have been too weak) as to support any judicial or legal actions carried out by court bailiffs involving the local nobility.

In 1572, the Kraków Chapter provided six medium-caliber cannons for the defense of the manor and the city. The previously mentioned castle guard was also expanded; it now comprised thirty armed men equipped with thirty complete sets of iron armor—though only twenty-seven pairs of armored sleeves—as well as thirty spears and an equal number of shields. The Chapter entrusted this matter to Canon Stanisław Dąbrowski, while also directing that the supervision of all weaponry be assigned to a man experienced in military affairs. Ultimately, however, after much persuasion, the cathedral parish priest Piotr Przerębski assumed responsibility for organizing the defense; though reluctant—given his lack of military experience and preoccupation with other matters—he undertook the task of fortifying the manor, the town of Pabianice, and even the estate’s outlying farms against attack and violence. He posted day and night guards, trained the youth of Rzgów, Pabianice, and the surrounding villages in the use of arms, and organized drills and reviews to prepare them for defending the people and their property.

> By a decision of the Chapter, the castle garrison was composed of *sołtysi*—or "chosen men" (*wybrańcy*)—specifically one in every twenty subjects, modeled on the *wybrańcy* system found on royal estates. In return for their service, they were exempt from all burdens, labor obligations, and the duty to provide quarters and provisions for passing troops (*stacja*). For every *wybraniec*, nineteen other subjects were required to perform all labor duties and pay the necessary tributes. In 1606, it was determined that, given the dangerous and turbulent times, the *wybrańcy* might prove insufficient to defend the Pabianice estate. Consequently, the Chapter resolved to supplement the twenty *wybrańcy* stationed at the Pabianice castle with an additional ten footmen. The entire garrison, now numbering thirty men, was issued uniforms, an expense covered by 300 zlotys from the Chapter's treasury.

Administrators representing the Kraków Chapter were based in Pabianice until 1796. During this period, the manor house underwent no significant changes to its décor or spatial layout, save for a single major modernization involving the addition of a quarter-circular tower to the main structure and the relocation of the entrance to the eastern wall. Under the Partitions, the building—now the property of the Prussian government—served as the seat of the local administration; however, in 1822, when the government granted privileges to German weavers settling in the area, these newcomers were allocated space for their workshops, among other benefits. The castle halls were made available to the arrivals, and it was within this old manor—amidst the clatter of hand-operated weaving looms—that the Pabianice textile industry was born, heralding a new era of development and material prosperity for the town.

In the 1830s, following extensive renovations, the former seat of the chapter was converted into a town hall and police detention center; during the Nazi occupation, it served as offices for the civil and military administration. After World War II, the castle building was handed over to the Pabianice City Museum, which has occupied it ever since.

## Description of the Castle

The two-story building was erected on a regular, nearly square footprint measuring 16.2 by 16.8 meters, featuring two corner alcoves and a avant-corps containing a quarter-circular cylindrical staircase (added in the 19th century). Its four-sided roof is concealed behind a decorative attic parapet pierced by openings for water drainage—gargoyles. The form of the attic varies, differing for each elevation: on the north and south sides, it runs parallel to the roof ridges and is roughly the height of a story, whereas the attic on the side walls conceals the gables of the recessed roof, mirroring the undulating contour of its cross-section.

The interiors of the manor’s lower level feature barrel vaults with lunettes, and the layout of the ground-floor rooms mirrors that of the upper floor. On the southern side, above one of the windows, the inscription *SIC TRANSIT GLORIA MUNDI* ("Thus passes the glory of the world") is carved; additionally, some window openings are set within ornamental frames featuring the coats of arms *Aaron* (the three crowns of the Kraków Chapter) and *Poraj* (a five-petaled rose). The ground floor primarily housed administrative spaces where visitors were received, as well as the treasury. A large room to the right of the entrance hall once contained a green tiled stove, a masonry chimney, benches along two sides, and a simple table. Ceremonial activities and daily life centered on the upper floor, which featured a large chamber illuminated by five windows that served as a dining hall. In the two rooms of the rear section, notable features include oriel windows that project beyond the face of the elevation and are supported by vaulting spanning the space between the buttresses. One of these—situated above the moat—served as the former *locus secretus* (latrine) for the resident canons.

Traces of the manor’s original defensive design are visible in its corner pavilions, which were equipped with narrow loopholes. On the north side, the structure is supported by buttresses that protect the stability of the walls against the adverse effects of the—formerly—marshy ground. Together with other outbuildings (an apiary, stables, a kitchen, a brewery, a bathhouse, and a prison), it was enclosed by an oak palisade featuring a small brick tower topped with a clock. Its defenses were further bolstered by two towers: one situated by the Dobrzynka stream and connected to the main tower by a drawbridge, and another located at the gate facing the town. The Pabianice manor represents the northernmost example of a *kastel*-style fortified structure—a type popular primarily in neighboring Slovakia but also found in Poland, notably in Frydman (in the Spisz region) and Szymbark (in Lesser Poland).

## Sightseeing

At the beginning of the 21st century, the manor received a magnificent gift from the local government: a comprehensive renovation of the building. This project included, among other things, uncovering the 1917 polychrome paintings by Jan Szulc, restoring Renaissance-style woodwork, and installing new flooring, plasterwork, windows, and gutters. Following the renovation, the Museum of the City of Pabianice ceremoniously returned to the building on June 5, 2005, featuring permanent exhibitions covering archaeology, history, ethnography, and natural history. Among its most fascinating exhibits are undoubtedly the complete attire of a tribal witch doctor from Zambia, ritual masks, weapons used by African warriors and hunters, and a Roman legionary’s helmet discovered in nearby Siemiechów.

**Practical information:**

- Admission to the museum requires a ticket; the price is nominal.
- Visitors view the exhibits independently but under the watchful eye of the staff—something that is irritating and discourages a longer stay. Visit duration: 30–45 minutes.
- Photographing and filming the exhibits for non-commercial use is free of charge.
- Pets are not allowed in the museum (with the exception of guide dogs).
- The museum is not accessible to people with disabilities.

### Getting There

The manor stands on Zamkowa Street—the town's main thoroughfare—near the Old Market Square (which today is merely an ordinary square lacking any tourist appeal). A bus and tram stop, "Zamkowa / Kościół św. Mateusza" (Zamkowa / St. Matthew’s Church), is located right next to it.

- Parking is available at the Old Market Square.
- No provision has been made for cyclists; there are no bike racks. At best, you can lock your bike to the ornate railing by the entrance steps, leaving it in full view of anyone passing by on the sidewalk.
