## What this extract contains

This book collects **Russian imperial state acts**, so it documents almost nothing about German life inside Poland or the Duchy of Warsaw — a separate, non-Russian jurisdiction until 1815. What it offers instead is empire-wide background from 1652 to 1799: how Russia recruited, organized, and governed German-speaking colonists in the decades before the family's 1814–15 move, which is the system they were about to enter. Two 1814 documents mark the move itself — No. 63, authorizing Warsaw-area colonists to resettle in Bessarabia, and No. 64, on the first arrivals' refusal to be sworn in — and both also appear in the Life in Bessarabia extract.

**On the 1814–1816 gap.** The collection is nearly silent on the migration years themselves, and that gap is in the source, not in this extract. Only three documents are dated 1814–1816: Nos. 63 and 64, both reproduced here, and No. 65 (1815), which is Volga-exclusive. Nothing at all is dated 1816 — the collection jumps from 1815 to 1817. The Kulm community homeland book and the early-settlement account in `bessarabia/` fill that silence; the next relevant act here is No. 67 (1817), in the Life in Bessarabia extract.

## Historical background from the compiler's Introduction (paraphrase)

The compiler's Introduction (V. F. Diesendorf, 2006) frames the 17th–19th-century background against which the Isaak family's own migration (Brandenburg/Prussia → northern Poland, 1790s → Pabianice, 1800) took place, even though the book itself — a collection of *Russian* state acts — has no jurisdiction over Prussian- or Napoleonic-era Poland and so contains no documents about German colonial administration in Poland itself. The relevant background facts drawn from the Introduction:

- Germans had settled in Moscow since the mid-17th century (the "German Quarter"/Sloboda), and their numbers and role in Russian state service grew steadily under Peter I and Catherine II.
- Peter I and Catherine II are characterized by the compiler as "Eurocentrists" rather than specifically Germanophile: Peter drew in foreign specialists and, after the Great Northern War, inherited a German-noble Baltic elite; Catherine II (herself German-born) initiated the large-scale invitation of German-speaking agricultural colonists into Russia from 1763 onward, but the compiler notes she "thereafter took very little interest in their fate."
- Paul I is credited by the compiler as decisive for the survival of the fledgling Volga and Novorossiya (New Russia) colonies founded under Catherine — he ordered detailed surveys of the colonies' condition and provided support that Alexander I and Nicholas I continued.
- This is the general historical backdrop — an established, decades-old Russian state apparatus for receiving and organizing foreign (chiefly German) agricultural colonists — into which the Warsaw-area colonists (including families such as the Isaaks) sought to fold themselves in 1814, per Document No. 63 below.

This is a paraphrase of the Introduction's argument, not a verbatim reproduction (the Introduction is a copyrighted 2006 essay). The full original English translation is in the full version listed above.

## No. 1. DECREE OF TSAR ALEKSEI MIKHAILOVICH ON THE ALLOTMENT OF LAND FOR THE GERMAN QUARTER IN MOSCOW (1652)

85. — October 4. On the allotment of land for building in the German Quarter.

By decree of the Great Sovereign, Tsar and Grand Prince Aleksei Mikhailovich of All Russia, Afonasy Ivanov son Nesterov, and the clerks Fyodor Ivanov and Bogdan Arefyev, built a new foreigners' quarter beyond the Pokrovsky Gate, beyond the Earthen City, alongside the Yauza River, where German houses had formerly stood, under previous Great Sovereigns, before the Moscow devastation, and they allotted land for house plots in that German Quarter, measuring it out according to the instructions given to them from the Zemsky prikaz [Land Office], by order of the Court Steward Bogdan Matveevich Khitrovo, countersigned by the clerk Maksim Likhachev. For Germans in military service, the first category [was] 40 fathoms in length by 20 in width; the second category 30 fathoms in length by 15 in width; the third category 15 fathoms in length by 10 in width; and for Doctors, [land] as in the first category; for Apothecaries, as in the middle category; for German masters of diamond-work, and gold-work, and silver-work, and gold-thread or lace work, according to a petition endorsed with the notation of the Duma clerk Mikhail Volosheninov, all as in the middle category; and for German merchants and widows, [land] was matched to their former Moscow house-plots; for Germans of the lesser categories: sergeants, and corporals, and regimental wagon-masters, and Germans in military service of all minor ranks, 10 fathoms in length by 8 in width, according to the memorandum sent from the Zemsky prikaz, countersigned by the clerk Maksim Likhachev. Plots for houses are to be given to German women, widows, and all manner of lesser German folk who have no houses of their own in Moscow, 8 fathoms in length by 6 in width; the names of who was given how much land in length and width for house plots, and of what rank, whether German men, women, or widows, and how many large streets and lanes were laid out, and of how many fathoms in width, is recorded by name in the construction registers.

*Complete Collection of Laws of the Russian Empire (PSZRI). Coll. 1. Vol. III. P. 273.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 1.

The first separate settlement of foreigners in Moscow appeared beyond the Yauza River in the second half of the 16th century. This Foreign, or German, Sloboda was burned in 1610 by the troops of False Dmitry II. After this, émigrés from Western Europe, a large proportion of them German, began to settle within the city itself; however, the Law Code (Sobornoye Ulozheniye) of 1649 forbade them to purchase houses within the Zemlyanoy Val (Earthen Rampart), that is, directly within the territory of Moscow at that time. In accordance with this decree of Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich, foreigners were resettled outside the city limits, on the site of the former German Sloboda. This settlement received the name of the New Foreign, New German, or simply German Sloboda. See: Kovrigina V.A. The German Sloboda of Moscow and Its Inhabitants at the End of the 17th — First Quarter of the 18th Century. Moscow, 1998. Pp. 25—28.

## No. 2. DECREE OF IVAN V AND PETER I ON THE INVESTIGATION OF RUSSIAN ORES IN GERMANY AND ON THE INVITATION OF FOREIGN MINING SPECIALISTS INTO RUSSIAN SERVICE (1695)

1512. — May 7. By name. — On sending the foreigner Matvei Poppu to Germany with samples of lead and silver ore for testing, and on inviting into Russian service foreigners knowledgeable in the art of mining.

The Great Sovereigns have ordered that 10 pounds of lead and silver ore, sent from the Musketeers' Office, be sent from Moscow overseas to the German states and given to the foreign merchant Matvei Poppu, and that he, Matvei, be instructed to have a genuine assay made of that ore; and [to determine] how much lead and silver will come out of that ore, and whether, according to the assay, it would be profitable to work that ore on a large scale, and whether, in large-scale smelting, many additional materials and compounds will be needed, and how much of such compounds will be required per 100 poods, and what the price would be by the foreign rate, and whether, having regard to that ore, by comparison with such works and ores, it can be said whether the ore that was taken from near the surface, would be better and yield more silver if taken from deeper down — and concerning all of this those ore-smelting masters are to be instructed to set it down properly in writing; and if, according to their assay and inspection, they come to recognize that there will in future be profit from that ore and that profitable works can be established, then he, Matvei, having made inquiry, is to seek out and engage knowledgeable and needed ore-smelting masters, [determining] whether any of them will be willing to serve the Great Sovereigns in that work, and what monthly salary from the Great Sovereigns they will ask, and for how many years they will hire themselves out; and concerning all of this, having obtained it in writing from those masters, [he] is instructed to send to Moscow, to the Siberian prikaz, [a report of] how much silver and lead will result, together with those written inspection findings.

*PSZRI. Coll. 1. Vol. III. P. 204.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 2.

During the Petrine period many German specialists in mining and metallurgy arrived in Russia. One of the first and most famous among them was Ivan Ivanovich (Johann Friedrich) Blüher, a mining master from Freiberg (Saxony), who was in Russian service from 1699 to 1728. In 1703 he founded the Petrovsky cast-iron and cannon works in Karelia, which gave rise to the town of Petrozavodsk; he subsequently worked on the construction of mining works in the Urals and the search for ore deposits in the south of Russia and the Caucasus. See: Zablotsky E. Mining // Germans of Russia: An Encyclopedia. Vol. 1. Moscow, 1999. P. 603; Korepanov N. Blüher // Ibid. Pp. 216—217.

Poppe, Matvei Lavrentyevich (Hans Matthias) (?—1711) — a Hamburg merchant, settled in Moscow in 1682, had a household there, and carried out various commissions for the Russian tsars, including the recruitment of foreign specialists.

## No. 3. MANIFESTO OF PETER I ON THE INVITATION OF FOREIGNERS TO SETTLE IN RUSSIA (1702)

1910. — April 16. Manifesto. — On summoning foreigners to Russia, granting them freedom of religion.

It is well known in all the lands which the Almighty has subjected to Our rule, that from the time of Our accession to this throne, all Our efforts and intentions have been directed toward governing this State in such a manner that all Our subjects, through Our concern for the general good, might come more and more into a better and more prosperous condition; to this end We have taken great pains to preserve internal tranquility, to defend the State from external attack, and in every way to improve and expand trade. For this same purpose We were compelled, in the very administration [of the state], to make certain necessary changes serving the good of Our land, so that Our subjects might the more readily and conveniently learn skills hitherto unknown to them, and thereby become more proficient in all commercial matters. For which reason We have most graciously issued, and intend hereafter to issue, all orders, arrangements, and institutions especially necessary for the promotion of trade with foreigners; but inasmuch as We fear that these matters are not yet in such a state as We would wish, and that Our subjects cannot yet enjoy in complete tranquility the fruits of Our labors, We have therefore also considered other means by which to secure Our borders from enemy attack and to preserve the rights and prerogatives of Our State and the general peace of Christendom, as befits a Christian Monarch. To attain these good ends, We have taken particular care for the best organization of the Military Establishment, as the mainstay of Our State, so that Our troops might not only consist of well-trained men, but also live in good order and discipline; but in order to improve this the more, and to encourage foreigners who can contribute to this end and assist in such improvement, together with other craftsmen useful to the State, to come to Us, and to remain both in Our service and in Our land, We have ordered this Manifesto, with the points written below, to be proclaimed everywhere, and, once printed, to be published throughout Europe. 1). It is not unknown how long ago, already during Our reign, We abolished and did away with the ancient custom by which free entry into Russia was entirely forbidden to foreigners; wherefore We not only confirm this, but extend it, so that any and every person having the intention of coming here to enter into service in Our army, and who has beforehand obtained new certificates from Our General Commissar in Germany, whom We intend accordingly to maintain there, shall, upon presenting himself to the first frontier Governor or Viceroy, together with the retinue and baggage he has with him, be provided from there to Our capital with relay-horses at no charge, and shall likewise enjoy full safety along his way. Wherefore We shall, from this date, communicate to all Our Viceroys, Governors, and Commanders, both on the borders and along the roads leading here from Kiev, Smolensk, and Pskov, Our confirmatory decrees and instructions to that effect, so that henceforth no hindrance or trouble whatsoever shall be caused to arriving officers, but that, on the contrary, every voluntary readiness to be of service shall be shown them; likewise merchants and craftsmen intending to enter Russia are to be received with every kindness. 2). And whereas here in Our Capital free performance of worship has already been introduced for all other Christian sects, even those not in agreement with Our own Church; for this reason it is hereby newly confirmed as well, in such manner that We, by the power granted to Us by the Almighty, do not wish to compel the human conscience, and gladly leave it to every Christian, on his own responsibility, to care for the salvation of his soul. And thus We shall watch closely that, as by former custom, no one shall be troubled in his public or private performance of worship, but shall be maintained therein and protected against every hindrance. Should it happen that in some place of Our State, or with Our armies and garrisons, there be no proper clergyman or church of the appropriate confession, then it shall be permitted to everyone not only to perform divine service in his own house, himself together with his household, but also to receive into it those who wish to gather with him, so that, according to the general order of the Christian churches, they may with one voice praise God and thus conduct divine worship. And should there be, with Our armies, individual officers, or entire corps consisting of regiments and companies, with whom preachers are found, then they too shall without doubt enjoy all those benefits, privileges, and liberties which We have granted to such churches here in the Capital, in Arkhangelsk, and in other places; and which are customary not only in the performance of the actual preaching service, but also in the administration of the Holy Sacraments and other church rites; and We further permit, at the request of such fellow believers, that they build new churches in other places as well. 3). And so that foreigners may not be deterred from coming to Russia by fear of being subjected to institutions, judicial proceedings, or kinds of punishment incompatible with their own countries' laws and customs, We have therefore hereby and by virtue hereof ordered that a Secret Council of War Collegium be formed from among foreigners skilled in matters of the Military Establishment, to consist of a President, Councilors, Secretaries, and other chancery officials, inasmuch as We intend to incorporate it into Our Military Establishment, and have ordered this to be published in an order by means of the press; and upon this Collegium shall depend all and any matters concerning the foreign Military Establishment, of whatever kind and nature they may be, as well as the Foreign Kriegs-Commissariat, the military treasury, and their officials; and the aforementioned President is to render account of his administration to Us alone; and is likewise bound to await from Us alone instructions and orders in matters of necessity. As for judicial matters beyond this, the first instance shall remain with the regiments, over whose decisions review shall be exercised by the General Military Court, and further proceedings shall follow as is the practice in other foreign regular armies and as shall, among other things, be contained in the military articles; which military articles and other regulations and statutes serving the Military Establishment, the President of Our Secret Council of War, in general deliberation with the whole Secret Military Collegium, shall draw up, and shall first submit to Us for consideration, and thereafter, once printed, shall publish. Furthermore, not only shall all and any persons, of higher and lower rank, who are enrolled in Our Foreign Military Establishment, whether officers or other officials and rank-and-file, together with all their servants or those who in any manner belong to their retinue, pertain to them, or depend on them, be, in respect of military or other matters — whether of property or personal contracts or suits — subject to the court and judgment of Our Secret Council of War Collegium; but We also subject to that same court and judgment all other foreigners who, having entered Our service, shall in any way be dependent on the military department, so that they too need not fear being subjected to a court not corresponding to their own countries' laws, customs, and mores; in such cases We permit that Our Secret Council of War Collegium administer justice, first according to Divine law, and then according to Roman civil law and other customary law of nations, mercifully, relying on the fact that in doing so Our Tsarist prerogatives, rights, and appurtenances shall always be observed. 4). And so that all those who enter into Our service may be assured that they will not be deprived of the freedom to leave Our service, We hereby assure them that upon discharge from Our service they will always be treated in the manner customary among other European Monarchs; with which all and everyone whom it concerns shall comply. Given in Our Capital, Moscow.

*PSZRI. Coll. 1. Vol. IV. P. 192–195.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 3.

This manifesto is the most important state act on attracting foreigners to Russia adopted in the pre-Catherinian period. Its initiator is considered to be the Livonian nobleman Johann Reinhold von Patkul (1660—1707), a fierce opponent of Swedish rule in the Baltic region, who entered Russian service in 1702. After the severe defeat by the Swedes at Narva (1700), it was not difficult for him to persuade Peter I of the advisability of broadly attracting foreign specialists to Russia, above all in military affairs. The formation, provided for in Article 3 of the manifesto, of a special Secret Military Collegium composed of foreigners is an unprecedented phenomenon in the history of modern law, dictated by the urgent need to create a modern army in the country as quickly as possible. The manifesto was published in the West, above all in the German lands, and had great resonance, causing a powerful influx into Russia of specialists from various fields. See: Fleischhauer I. Die Deutschen im Zarenreich: Zwei Jahrhunderte deutsch-russische Kulturgemeinschaft. Stuttgart, 1991. Pp. 43—46.

## No. 4. CHARTER OF PETER I TO THE LIVONIAN NOBILITY (1710)

2301. — September 30. Charter to the nobility of the Principality of Livonia — confirming their former rights, and especially the privilege granted by the Polish King Sigismund Augustus at Vilna in 1561, concerning their noble rights, statutes, liberties, dignities, and lawful estates, both those in their possession and those which, though hereditary, had been unlawfully taken from them.

We, Peter the First, by the Grace of God Tsar and Emperor of All Russia,

and so forth, and so forth, and so forth.

We hereby declare that whereas Our noble Knighthood and Landed Gentry of the Principality of Livonia, together with that whole Province, by God's merciful governance and Our just and victorious arms, in accordance with the capitulation previously granted by Us, have surrendered to Us and become Our subjects, and have taken a solemn oath of allegiance to Us and to Our lawful Heirs, and thereafter, through Our dear and faithful Privy Councilor Baron von Löwenwolde, most submissively petitioned that We might graciously confirm and grant to them and their heirs all their ancient privileges acquired and held down to the present time, especially the privilege of Sigismund Augustus granted at Vilna in 1561, their knightly rights, statutes, liberties, and appurtenances, their rightful possessions, and both those properties presently held by them and those unlawfully taken from them. Wherefore, taking into most gracious consideration that the aforesaid Knighthood and Landed Gentry of the Principality of Livonia have always shown to their former Sovereigns, to their great benefit and to their own everlasting glory, zealous loyalty and good service, and have faithfully promised, by virtue of the aforesaid oath, likewise to render the same to Us and to Our lawful Heirs on every occasion, and always to uphold with honor the duty of faithful subjects, We have justly resolved, in response to their most humble and just petition, to grant, by Imperial favor, that the said Knighthood and Landed Gentry and their posterity might thereby be all the more encouraged to confirm their sworn subject loyalty with zealous good service, and, as the times may require, with their blood and their lives; and We hereby, to the said faithful Knighthood and Landed Gentry of Ours in Livonia and to their heirs, most graciously confirm and establish, for Ourself and for Our lawful Heirs, by this and by virtue hereof, all their previously acquired privileges, under which they have already submitted to Us, and especially the privilege of Sigismund Augustus granted at Vilna in 1561, [their] statutes, knightly rights, liberties, appurtenances (insofar as these are compatible with the present Government and time), rightful possessions and properties, both those which they actually hold and enjoy, and those to which they have claim from their ancestors by their rights and appurtenances; and We promise that they and their heirs, as is right and just, shall in all this be fully and continually maintained and protected; reserving, however, without prejudice or harm, Our own Sovereign right and that of Our States, and their Highness and rights, according to which all and every stipulation is likewise to be upheld; and We hereby expressly command Our high and low officials holding command in those places, and those who are bound to Us by obedience and oath, that they shall cause no hindrance or prejudice whatsoever to Our faithful Knighthood and Landed Gentry in Livonia contrary to their privileges, knightly rights, statutes, liberties, and appurtenances, nor permit any to be caused, but rather, wherever occasion may require, shall defend and protect them therein. This original document We have, for greater security and confirmation, signed with Our own hand, and have ordered it to be strengthened with Our Imperial seal.

*PSZRI. Coll. 1. Vol. IV. P. 575–577.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 4.

The charter was adopted after Russia's conquest of Livonia (1710) during the Great Northern War of 1700—1721.

Sigismund II Augustus (1520—1572) — King of Poland, participant in the Livonian War of 1558—1583, in the course of which (1561) he subordinated Livonia to Poland, guaranteeing the Livonian nobility the preservation of their rights and privileges.

Löwenwolde (Löwenwald), Gerhard Johann von (?—1723) — in Russian service from 1708; after the capitulation of Riga (1710) he was appointed Russian plenipotentiary in Livonia.

## No. 5. CHARTER OF PETER I TO THE ESTONIAN NOBILITY (1712)

2495. — March 1. Charter to the Gentry and Landed Gentry of the Principality of Estonia — confirming their ancient privileges, rights, and Statutes.

We, Peter the First, Tsar and Autocrat of All Russia,

and so forth, and so forth, and so forth.

We hereby declare and make known. Whereas Our faithful subjects, the whole Gentry and Landed Gentry of the Principality of Estonia, through their dispatched noble, steadfast, and courageous Landrats, Reinhold Baron von Ungern-Sternberg and Bengt Heinrich von Bystrom, have most submissively petitioned Us for the confirmation and establishment, in due allegiance, of their ancient privileges, rights, courts, justice, recesses, Statutes, and praiseworthy provincial customs, which they received from their respective authorities — namely, from King to King, from Hochmeister to Hochmeister, from Hermeister to Hermeister, and from Sovereign to Sovereign — and have freely exercised down to the present time, We, by Imperial grace and favor, did not wish to refuse them; and inasmuch as We are Ourself fully assured of their constant and most loyal fidelity to Us and to Our Imperial Heirs, We do hereby, and by virtue of this Our open charter, confirm to them the free and open exercise of the Evangelical faith, and together with it all their ancient privileges, as well as agreements, concessions, rights, courts, recesses, statutes, Christian provincial customs and usages, in which We wish to defend, protect, and maintain them against all, as they received and held them from King to King, from Hochmeister to Hochmeister, from Hermeister to Hermeister, and from Sovereign to Sovereign. We likewise most graciously promise them that they and their descendants shall always be maintained and preserved in all of this; and to that end We do hereby strictly command all Our high and low Commanders of places, and all those bound to Us by dutiful subjection and obedience, that they shall themselves cause no hindrance or prejudice to them in this regard, nor permit it to be caused by others, but shall rather, in cases of need, defend and maintain them therein. In confirmation whereof We have signed this with Our own hand and have ordered Our Imperial seal to be affixed hereunto.

*PSZRI. Coll. 1. Vol. IV. P. 810.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 5.

The charter was adopted after Russia's conquest of Estland (1710) during the Great Northern War of 1700—1721.

Ungern-Sternberg, Reinhold (Reno) von (1656—1713) — Landrat (leader of the nobility) of Estland, president of the Landrat collegium. Bistram, Bengt Heinrich von (1667—1724) — Landrat of Estland. They formed the deputation of the Estland knighthood that received this charter of grant in St. Petersburg.

## No. 6. MANIFESTO OF PETER I ON PERMITTING FOREIGNERS TO ENGAGE IN MINING (1720)

3621. — July 30. Manifesto. — On admitting foreigners to the building and expansion of mining works.

Whereas in Our States and lands there now appear, ever more abundantly, favorable signs of ores and useful minerals, which, by God's blessing, give genuine hope of great profit. 9434. — August 27. Senate decree. — On the oath of allegiance for foreigners wishing to swear perpetual allegiance to Russia.

In session, the Governing Senate, upon signing the composed oath concerning perpetual allegiance for foreigners, Ordered: that of this oath, having printed up to 500 copies in German and in Russian at the Academy of Sciences, copies be sent to the Military, Admiralty, and Foreign Collegiums, to the Governorates of Riga, Reval, and Vyborg, and to the Provinces of those Governorates along with the decrees; and that if foreign persons should wish to enter into perpetual allegiance to Her Imperial Majesty, they are to swear thereto according to this oath, and those of them who are found to have no knowledge whatsoever of the Russian language are to take the oath in their own language, and both that oath and the oath printed in Russian are to be signed, and of these one copy is to be kept in the places where the oath is administered, and the other sent to the Governing Senate.

Oath of allegiance.

I, the undersigned, formerly a subject [of another power], do promise and swear to Almighty God that I will be a faithful, good, and obedient servant and perpetual subject, together with my family, to the Most Illustrious, Most Mighty Great Sovereign, the Empress Elizabeth Petrovna, Autocrat of All Russia, etc., etc., etc., and to Her Imperial Majesty's high and lawful Heir, His Imperial Highness the Right-Believing Sovereign, Grand Duke Peter Fedorovich, who has been appointed by the will and autocratic power of Her Imperial Majesty, and to those hereafter to be appointed by Her Imperial Majesty's autocratic power and deemed worthy High Heirs to the succession of the Throne; and that I will not depart abroad anywhere without the Most High permission and decree of Her Imperial Majesty, nor enter into foreign service; nor will I have any harmful understanding with the enemies of Her Imperial Majesty, nor maintain any forbidden correspondence within or outside the Russian State, nor in any way act against the duty of a faithful subject of Her Imperial Majesty; and that I will, to the utmost of my understanding, strength, and ability, safeguard and defend all the rights and prerogatives (or advantages), now established by law and hereafter to be established by law, belonging to Her Imperial Majesty's High Autocracy, power, and authority, and will spare not even my life in this, should the need arise; and moreover I will endeavor to the utmost to further everything that may pertain, in any circumstance, to the faithful service of Her Imperial Majesty and the good of the State. And as to any harm to Her Majesty's interest, damage, or loss, as soon as I learn of it, I will not only report it in timely fashion but will strive by every means to avert and prevent it. And when, for the service and benefit of Her Majesty, some secret matter, of whatever nature it may be, shall be entrusted to me to be kept secret, I will keep it in complete secrecy and will disclose it to no one who ought not to know of it and to whom I am not instructed to disclose it; this I must and will faithfully uphold, so help me Almighty God in soul and body. And in conclusion of this my oath, I kiss the Words and the Cross of my Savior. Amen.

### No. 9.

This act attests that the idea of inviting foreigners to Russia, actively pursued by Peter I, gained new impetus during the reign of his daughter, Elizabeth Petrovna. In time she went even further than Peter in her plans, intending to broadly attract European colonists to develop the country's vast, sparsely populated territories. The realization of these intentions was hindered by the Seven Years' War of 1756—1763, and they began to be implemented only after its conclusion, during the reign of Catherine II. A similar solemn oath to Catherine and to the heir to the throne, Paul Petrovich, introduced in 1763, was not confirmed by the highest organs of power, and is therefore absent from the Complete Collection of Laws of the Russian Empire. **Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 10.

In the 17th and early 18th centuries, in the German Sloboda, as everywhere else in Moscow, it was customary to bury the dead near churches. Empress Elizabeth Petrovna, who protected herself from reminders of death, forbade burials along the streets through which her carriage usually passed. This ban also affected the cemeteries near the churches of the German Sloboda, which were restored by this decree of Peter III, Elizabeth's successor. The present-day Vvedenskoye (German) Cemetery in Lefortovo arose after the plague epidemic of 1771, when Catherine II required that the dead be buried only outside the city limits. The remains from the destroyed churchyards of the German Sloboda's churches were transferred to this cemetery. See: Miklashevskaya E.P., Tseplyaeva M.S. Famous Germans of Lefortovo. History in Faces. ## No. 11. MANIFESTO OF CATHERINE II INVITING FOREIGN COLONISTS TO SETTLE IN RUSSIA (1763)

11,880. — July 22. Manifesto. — On permitting all foreigners entering Russia to settle in whichever Governorates they wish, and on the rights granted to them.

We, being aware of the vastness of the lands of Our Empire, observe among other things a considerable number of places, most advantageous for the settlement and habitation of mankind and most useful, that still remain vacant, many of which conceal within their depths an inexhaustible wealth of various metals; and as there are forests, rivers, lakes, and seas suitable for commerce in sufficient abundance, there is likewise great capacity for the multiplication of many manufactories, factories, and other works. This gave Us cause to issue, for the benefit of all Our loyal subjects, a Manifesto on December 4 of last year, 1762; but as in that Manifesto We only briefly declared Our consent regarding those of foreign nations wishing to settle in Our Empire, in supplement thereto We command that the following regulation be announced to all, which We most solemnly establish and command to be carried out:

1. We permit all foreigners to enter Our Empire and settle wherever they wish, in all Our Governorates.

2. Such foreigners may come and present themselves not only at Our Residence, at the Chancellery of Guardianship of Foreigners established for that purpose, but also in other border towns of Our Empire, wherever is more convenient for anyone, before the Governors, and where there are none, before the chief town officials.

3. Among the number of foreigners wishing to settle in Russia there will be some who do not have sufficient means for their journey; such persons may present themselves to Our Ministers and Residents at foreign Courts, by whom they will not only be sent to Russia immediately at Our expense, but will also be provided with travel money.

4. As soon as foreigners arrive at Our Residence and present themselves at the Chancellery of Guardianship, or at any other border town of Ours, they must declare their firm intention as to what their wish consists of — whether to enroll in the merchantry or in the guilds and become townspeople, and in which town, or to settle in colonies and small towns on free and advantageous lands for agriculture and other many advantages. All such persons will, according to their wishes, receive an immediate determination concerning themselves; and where, and in exactly which places in Our Empire, free lands suitable for settlement are to be found, may be seen from the following register — although there is an incomparably greater quantity of vast lands and all manner of amenities than the number declared, on which We likewise permit settlement, by whomever, wherever among them, chooses for their own benefit.

5. As soon as any foreigner arrives in Our Empire for settlement, and presents himself at the Chancellery of Guardianship established for them, or at Our other border towns, he must first, as prescribed above in point 4, declare his wish, and thereafter every such person must take, according to his faith and its rites, the customary oath of allegiance to Us in fidelity.

6. But so that all foreigners wishing to settle in Our Empire may see how great is Our benevolence for their benefit and advantage, We are pleased to grant: 1. To all who arrive in Our Empire for settlement, free exercise of their religion according to their own statutes and rites without hindrance; and to those wishing to settle, not in towns, but separately on vacant lands, in colonies and small towns, permission to build churches and belfries, having the requisite number of Pastors and other clergy, excepting only the building of monasteries; noting, however, that none of those living in Russia under Christian confessions shall, under any pretext, induce or draw anyone into agreement with their own faith or community, under pain of the full severity of Our laws — excepting from this those of various ranks living under the Mohammedan faith adjacent to the borders of Our Empire, whom We permit not only to be suitably induced into the Christian faiths, but also permit anyone to make into their own serfs. 2. Such persons arriving from among foreigners for settlement in Russia shall not be obliged to pay any taxes to Our treasury, nor to perform any ordinary or extraordinary services, nor to provide billeting, and, in a word, shall be free from all levies and burdens, as follows, namely: those settling with many families and entire colonies on vacant lands, for 30 years; and those wishing to reside in towns, likewise to enroll in guilds and the merchantry, in Our Residence of St. Petersburg, or in the nearby places of Livonia and Estonia, Ingria, Karelia, and the Finnish towns, as well as in the capital city of Moscow, for five years; in other Governorate, Provincial, and other towns, for ten years; but in addition, every person arriving in Russia not for temporary residence but for settlement shall be given free lodging for half a year. 3. To all foreigners arriving in Russia for settlement, every assistance and satisfaction will be given to those inclined to agriculture or any other handicraft, and to the establishment of manufactories, factories, and works — not only will a sufficient quantity of suitable and advantageous lands be allotted for this purpose, but every necessary assistance will be provided, according to the means of each, with particular attention to the need and benefit of newly established factories and works, especially those not yet established in Russia to this day. 4. For the building of houses, for the acquisition of various livestock for household management, and for the instruments, supplies, and materials necessary for agriculture and handicraft, the necessary sum of money will be issued from Our treasury without any interest, but with a single repayment, and that after the lapse of ten years, over three years in equal installments. 5. To those settling in separate colonies and small towns, We leave their internal jurisdiction to their own good governance, on the condition that Our authorities will have no part in their internal arrangements, but in other respects they are obliged to obey Our civil law. If, however, they should at some time wish to have from Us a special person for their guardianship, or for their safety and protection, until they become acquainted with the neighboring inhabitants, together with a well-disciplined military safeguard, this will be given to them. 6. To every foreigner wishing to settle in Russia, We permit the import of his property, of whatever it may consist, without payment of any duties whatsoever, provided, however, that it is for his own use and needs, and not for sale. And if such a person, beyond his own use, brings something in the way of goods for sale, We permit the duty-free import of no more than goods to the value of 300 rubles per family, provided they remain in Russia not less than 10 years; otherwise, upon their return journey, the current import and export duties are to be collected on those goods. 7. Foreigners settled in Russia shall not, for the whole time of their residence, be assigned against their will either to military or to civil service, except for the ordinary local [zemstvo] service, and that only after the lapse of the prescribed years of exemption; but should anyone wish of his own free will to enter military service as a soldier, such a person will be given, upon assignment to a regiment, 30 rubles as a reward, in addition to the ordinary pay. 8. Foreigners who present themselves at the Chancellery of Guardianship established for them, or at Our other border towns, as soon as they declare their wish to travel into the interior of Russia for settlement, shall be given both provision money and carts free of charge, as far as their intended destination. 9. Should any of the foreigners settled in Russia establish such factories, manufactories, or works, and begin to produce thereon goods of a kind not previously made in Russia, We permit the sale and export of such goods from Our Empire for 10 years without payment of any internal, port, or border duty. 10. And should any foreign capitalists, at their own expense, establish factories, manufactories, and works in Russia, We permit such persons to purchase the requisite number of serfs and peasants for those manufactories, factories, and works. 11. To foreigners settled in Our Empire in colonies or small towns, We permit the establishment, at their own good discretion, of markets and fairs, without any levy or payment of duties to Our treasury.

7. All the aforesaid advantages and provisions shall be enjoyed not only by those who come to Our Empire for settlement, but also by their surviving children and descendants, even should the latter be born in Russia, the number of years being counted from the day of their ancestors' arrival in Russia.

8. After the lapse of the aforesaid years of exemption, all foreigners settled in Russia shall be obliged to pay the ordinary taxes without any burden, and to perform local [zemstvo] services, as do Our other subjects.

9. Finally, should any of those who have settled and entered into Our allegiance among the foreigners wish to leave Our Empire, We always grant them freedom to do so, with this explanation, however, that they are obliged to render to Our treasury a portion of all the property lawfully acquired in Our Empire, namely: those living from one year up to five years, a fifth part; and those from five up to ten years and beyond, a tenth part; and thereafter to depart, wherever they wish, without hindrance.

10. Should some of the foreigners wishing to settle in Russia, for whatever particular reasons, require yet other conditions and privileges beyond those prescribed, they may address themselves, in writing or in person, to Our established Chancellery of Guardianship of Foreigners, from which a detailed report will be made to Us on all matters, and We will then, according to the circumstances presented, render a decision all the more favorable, such as they may hope for from Our sense of justice.

Register of free lands in Russia suitable for settlement.

1) In the Tobolsk [Siberian] Governorate, near Tobolsk, on the Baraba steppe, where there are several hundred thousand desyatinas of fertile land advantageous for settlement, with forests, rivers, and fisheries.

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 11.

This act was preceded by the manifesto of Catherine II permitting foreigners to settle in Russia, of December 4, 1762 (for the text see: German Colonists in the Age of Catherine / Comp.: E.E. Lykova, M.I. Osekina. Moscow, 2004. Pp. 10—11). Unlike the present manifesto, it was general in character and had no direct consequences.

It is difficult to find an analogue in history to the present manifesto, either in the scale of its conception or in the list of privileges promised to future settlers. Hence the unprecedented influx of colonists — more than 30,000 in just a few years. However, many of the manifesto's promises were never fulfilled. The most damaging consequences stemmed from the irresponsible assurances that in Russia a use would be found for settlers of the most diverse occupations, and that they would be free to choose their place of residence (Art. 1, 4, 6, para. 3). In reality, the overwhelming majority of settlers — among whom were people of all manner of professions who had believed Catherine's promises — were forced to take up agriculture in the arid steppes of the Volga region, where even experienced German peasants managed to establish themselves only decades later. Entirely unfamiliar conditions of life and labor, lack of proper arrangements, frequent raids by nomads, the ruin of the newly formed colonies during the rebellion of Ye.I. Pugachev, and the arbitrariness of recruiting agents and tsarist officials — this is far from a complete list of the causes that ultimately resulted in high mortality among the first Volga colonists. This is documented by the absolute decline in population between 1773 and 1788 in a whole series of colonies in the Volga region (see: Dizendorf V.F. Ten Years in "Renaissance." Moscow, 2000. Pp. 589—592).

The lack of forethought, and in many respects the reckless adventurism, of Catherine's colonization plans is vividly illustrated by the register of lands, allegedly set aside for settlement, appended to the manifesto. On one point it coincided with reality: many colonies did indeed form in the areas of the left bank of the Volga indicated here in paragraph 3. However, beyond that, the register names the Baraba steppes, the Ust-Kamenogorsk district, and the Orenburg region (paragraphs 1, 2, 4), where German settlements arose only at the end of the 19th century, as well as Valuysky Uyezd of Belgorod Governorate (paragraph 5), where they never appeared at all. On the other hand, the register is silent about the possibility of settling the first colonists on the right bank of the Volga, in Livonia, near St. Petersburg, Chernigov, and Voronezh, as actually occurred.

The permission granted in the manifesto (Art. 6, para. 1) for colonists to build churches with bell towers was of fundamental significance: at that time in Europe, having a bell tower meant that the church that built it possessed public rights; churches that did not have public rights had to locate their prayer houses in inner courtyards and could not equip them with bell towers. See: Suttner E. Catholics (German-speaking) in the Russian Empire and the USSR // Germans of Russia: An Encyclopedia. Vol. 2. P. 38.

The manifesto was translated into a number of languages and distributed in many European countries, but it was primarily the inhabitants of the German lands, ruined by the Seven Years' War of 1756—1763 and traditionally having closer ties with Russia, who responded to it.

## No. 18. REPORT OF G.G. ORLOV ON THE SETTLEMENT OF FOREIGNERS ON PRIVATE LANDS NEAR ST. PETERSBURG, CONFIRMED BY CATHERINE II (1765)

12,503. — November 1. Supremely confirmed report of the President of the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers, Count Orlov. — On the settlement of foreigners in Ingermanland on private lands, under agreements with their owners.

Report. Upon the announcement to the colonists residing in Oranienbaum of the Supreme consent of Your Imperial Majesty that those among them who wished might be settled near St. Petersburg, not only have many shown inclination to do so, but some, as is already known to Your Imperial Majesty, have in fact already concluded contracts with the Steward of Your Imperial Majesty's village of Tsarskoye Selo, Court Councillor Udalov. This inclination on their part gives sure hope that those arriving in future, following this example, will also be willing to settle in Ingermanland; and in order to give foreigners still greater inclination to settle in the areas near St. Petersburg, and thereby to bring the solicitous intention of Your Imperial Majesty for the welfare of her subjects more quickly to the desired conclusion in this matter as well, would Your Most Gracious Majesty be pleased to order the Supreme confirmation of the following provisions:

1. In order to bring agriculture in Ingermanland into a better condition than before, and especially in order to expand and establish it on those lands which either lie entirely vacant or are only leased out as hay-mowing grounds for a small rent, it is most graciously permitted to noblemen, as well as to merchants and free foreigners residing here, to induce foreigners arriving in Russia to settle, on their own or on the Sovereign's vacant lands situated in Ingermanland, on condition that those who agree to settle voluntarily be settled and established under separate agreements made between themselves, at their own expense, without requiring anything from the treasury.

2. And so that the colonists may not think that this permission seeks to limit their freedom, it is for that reason to be established by law that those very foreigners who agree voluntarily to settle on the lands of private persons, whether on their own lands or on Sovereign lands specially allotted to them for that purpose, shall always remain free people, on the same basis as the original colonists, under the guardianship of the Chancellery established for that purpose, whose authority and oversight in this case shall extend no further than to see that the contracts concluded by both sides — whether with private persons or with the Court Chancellery, or with other government offices, should the need arise — are observed inviolably, and that nothing harmful to the interests of the State or prejudicial to the published Manifesto concerning foreigners be included in them. For this reason such contracts are to be submitted to the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers for approval, where they shall also be kept; and the parties to those contracts shall be given exact copies, signed by those present, while private settlers shall also receive copies bearing the seal of that Chancellery. Consequently, all such foreigners who by special contracts undertake to settle in Ingermanland may bring their complaints, whether against government offices or in other districts, to the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers, as the place expressly established for their protection.

3. Excepting those lands which, at the last land survey, were separated from the manorial estates and are now leased out by the Chancellery of Road Construction, insofar as it shall be deemed proper to leave some of them for the settlement of foreigners under the Court Department, the remainder shall be distributed by the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers to those willing to take them, on the following conditions:

4. That on the lands given to them they settle solely emigrating foreigners and free persons already residing here, under voluntary agreements made with them, at their own expense, being permitted to build for their own arrival as each shall see fit; in connection with such building anyone may also employ his own hired Russian workers, provided only that these are not settled for the purpose of farming, because these lands, in consideration of the freedom of the foreigners settled upon them, cannot be granted on the same basis as to others; but on condition that they shall not be ceded to anyone under any pretext, though the whole of the land — not in parts — may freely be sold or mortgaged, provided the successor undertakes to observe all the conditions set forth in the contract.

5. When the settler himself, or his heir or successor, through purchase or borrowing, is for whatever reason unable to fulfill the concluded contract, he shall not be compelled to do so; instead, the land, together with the settlers, shall be taken into treasury administration, and the buildings (except the settler's own, which he is free to keep) shall, if the colonists have not paid for them, be reimbursed from the treasury at whatever cost they came to; and so that there be no doubt in these accounts, settlers must take receipts from their colonists for all expenses incurred on their behalf, for without this settlers may find themselves at a loss.

6. If, however, after the period for payment set forth in the Manifesto has elapsed, or as the settlers and colonists shall have agreed, the latter do not pay what is owed, and the settlers accordingly renounce ownership, the treasury shall satisfy them in the manner described above, and the colonists shall be obliged to pay as shall be prescribed to them. But if the colonists are unable to pay what is owed because the settlers have not fulfilled everything under the concluded contract, and this is proven, then in such a case nothing shall be paid to the settlers from the treasury as a penalty; instead, they must either be satisfied under the contract, with payment deferred at the discretion of the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers, or the matter shall be transferred to treasury administration. On the other hand, the colonists are likewise obliged to fulfill without exception the promise they made to the settlers, save in cases of misfortune, which must also be stipulated in the contracts; and if, moreover, they act contrary to their undertaking out of obstinacy or negligence, they shall not only be compelled, for the satisfaction of the settlers, to obedience and fulfillment of the agreement, but shall also be punished according to the degree of their fault and offense, since these contracts are to be concluded by the good will and consent of each party; consequently they must be observed inviolably by both sides.

7. Since land shall be distributed to those willing to take it free of charge, on condition that they settle upon it foreigners emigrating to Russia; but if, after 10 years have passed, colonists have not been settled on the lands given to them, such lands shall be taken back into the treasury, with recovery of a holding fee of 15 kopecks per desyatina of good land for each year; and as for woodland, if it is cleared, 10 rubles per desyatina shall be charged once for timber-quality wood and 3 rubles for firewood-quality wood, in addition to the 15 kopecks per desyatina payable generally for the whole land; and even if several families are settled but so much land nevertheless remains vacant, that surplus land shall be dealt with in the same manner as described above.

8. And if anyone, having taken land, fails within the first two years to build even a single homestead on it or to settle even a single family of foreigners, and another applicant is found who did not receive land in the original distribution and petitions for that unsettled land after the two years have passed, representing that he has several families of foreigners ready for settlement, and this representation proves true, then that land shall be taken from the first party, with recovery of the holding fee for the two years, or for however long he made use of it, and given to the one who has people ready for settlement — but not before a contract has been concluded; and if, however, at that same time the first settler produces a contract he has concluded with several foreigners, the land shall not be taken from him, and the latter applicant shall be refused.

9. After 10 years from the granting of the land into ownership, on which several families of foreigners have been settled and the whole of that land has been divided among them according to the settler's discretion, the settler shall recover from the colonists, in three equal annual installments, first the treasury money that was spent on them before their arrival in St. Petersburg, as well as the subsistence money issued to them before the conclusion of the contract with the settler; but apart from this, no further exactions whatsoever shall be required, for the remaining exemption years, either from the settler or from the settlers themselves.

10. When the settlers themselves or their heirs no longer wish to retain those settled lands in their possession, or when no successors remain to them, such lands shall be taken into treasury administration, but on condition that if the Crown, for whatever reason, cannot or does not see fit to enjoy the benefits which the settlers had stipulated for themselves, then the settlers (peasants) shall be obliged, in place of those benefits, after the exemption years have passed, to pay into the treasury solely 25 kopecks per desyatina of good land each year; in all other respects no taxes whatsoever shall be collected from them; for which reason, before concluding contracts with settlers, this provision must be made known to them and their signed acknowledgment obtained.

11. As for those foreigners who shall be settled by various landowners on their own lands situated in Ingermanland, such settlers must, immediately upon concluding the contracts, pay into the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers the full amount of money that was spent on them from the treasury; and beyond this, the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers shall have no further business with such foreigners, either during the exemption years or afterward, except to protect them from abuses by their landlords and from breaches of contract on either side.

12. Such landowners, on whose lands the said foreigners shall live in houses, are not obliged, apart from the ordinary taxes levied on their manors and villages, to pay anything into the treasury for them even after the exemption years have passed, since the benefit expected from this settlement, in my opinion, consists not in increasing revenues but in the advancement of agriculture. As for billeting, that shall remain at their discretion and shall depend chiefly on the contracts concluded with the foreigners; for which reason such settlers must state clearly and specifically in the contracts concluded with the colonists whether the colonists voluntarily agree, after the exemption years have passed, to bear any levies in aid of their landlords' serfs; for whatever is not precisely and specifically mentioned in such agreements, the landowners may in no way exact from them.

13. In the sale and inheritance of these lands on which foreigners shall be settled, action shall be taken exactly according to Point 4. For this reason they must be settled separately from other peasants, or at least the land allotments given to them under the concluded contracts must be surveyed and marked with boundaries, and plans, immediately after the survey, submitted to the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers, so that settlers may be able to sell such lands together with the settlers, or, should some impossibility prevent the owner from maintaining the contracts, to cede them to the Crown. In the event of an offense by colonists warranting corporal punishment, the landowners shall not deal with the matter themselves, but shall seek redress against them from the Chancellery of Trusteeship for Foreign Settlers. In all other respects, both settlers and colonists must act unfailingly according to the obligations mutually given to one another; for whichever of them first breaches these shall be deemed the principal cause, and guilty of all the ill consequences arising therefrom, and shall accordingly be compelled to bear alone the whole burden and losses.

Beyond the expansion of agriculture in Ingermanland, I consider the above-described provisions, Most Gracious Sovereign, to be beneficial also in that, by this means, the treasury may avoid no small expense. In all else I submit this to the Supreme goodwill of Your Imperial Majesty, and shall await the Most Gracious decree.

Resolution. Let it be so.

*PSZRI. Coll. 1. Vol. XVII. Pp. 373-377.*

### No. 18.

The creation of German colonies in the environs of St. Petersburg (Ingria) was pure improvisation on the part of the Russian authorities — at first they intended neither to establish settlements in these places nor to settle colonists on private lands at all. The decision on this was made at a moment when the various and difficult-to-resolve problems connected with the simultaneous transport of thousands of colonists from St. Petersburg to the distant Volga region, and with providing them there with crown lands and everything necessary for life, had already become clear. On the other hand, the future colonists, having made the long, difficult journey to Oranienbaum, readily agreed to settle in Ingria. There is no doubt that far more of them would have remained here had there been enough free fertile land in these places and, of course, had the Russian authorities not obstructed this, stubbornly pursuing their original goal — to develop, by the hands of the colonists, the vast sparsely populated territories on the southern outskirts of the country. For these reasons the St. Petersburg colonies did not subsequently see any particular development. On the other hand, the settlement of foreigners on private lands became a widespread practice in the 19th century.

The Livland colonies resulted from improvisation by the Russian authorities in exactly the same way as the St. Petersburg ones (see note to doc. No. 18). ## No. 20. REPORT OF G. G. ORLOV ON SUPPLYING THE COLONISTS WITH SUBSISTENCE MONEY, WITH THE RESOLUTION OF CATHERINE II (1766)

12,651. — May 19. Supreme resolution on the report of the President of the Chancellery of Guardianship of Foreigners, Count Orlov. — On supplying the colonists with sustenance and everything necessary for their establishment, in kind or in money, whichever is more convenient.

Report. On May 4, 1766, the Chancellery of Guardianship of Foreigners, considering that from the very beginning of the departure of foreigners to Russia for settlement, subsistence money has to this day been paid in St. Petersburg, in Oranienbaum, and also during their conveyance to the place of settlement, at the rate of 15 kopecks per day for men, 10 for women, 6 for their children of both sexes from 15 down to 2 years of age, and 2 kopecks for those aged 2 and under, and that this payment has been confirmed neither by the Supreme command of Your Imperial Majesty nor by a Chancellery decree, but only since the very first arrival of foreigners, at their urgent request that, following the example of the payment established by the Minister in Hamburg, in view of the high cost of provisions there, subsistence money should likewise be paid here, the Chancellery of Guardianship of Foreigners was compelled to retain the arrangement established in Hamburg on that same basis, with only a very small reduction, chiefly so as not to cause dissatisfaction among these first arrivals nor create an obstacle to the future arrival of foreigners; wherefore their promises of frugal living and of saving the remainder of the subsistence money for future household establishment were taken into consideration. But as it has now been sufficiently observed that the colonists, notwithstanding the considerable cheapness of provisions here as compared with Hamburg, for the most part use the subsistence money not with such thrift as their own benefit would require, but squander it largely on wine and other excesses, whereby, apart from the disorders connected therewith, they fall into considerable debts, quite burdensome to themselves, for this reason, in order to put a stop to these abuses, the Chancellery of Guardianship of Foreigners has, among other things, resolved: that for all foreigners actually present here and those expected in future, both during their stay here and en route to the place of settlement, subsistence money should henceforth be paid to men and women equally at 10 kopecks, and to their children of both sexes, counting those from 17 to 10 years old, at 6 kopecks, from 10 down to 2 years old at 4 kopecks, and to those 2 years old and infants at 2 kopecks per day, for the Chancellery considers this proportion, even given the present high cost of living here, quite sufficient for subsistence; and that this reduction is highly beneficial to the colonists themselves, so that, having the necessary maintenance here without excess, they should not indulge in luxury and thereby incur less debt, [the Chancellery] exhorting them with sound arguments to avoid idleness and apply themselves diligently to labor. But for my part, without the Supreme permission of Your Imperial Majesty, I could not agree to this — not because the reasons presented by the Members of that Chancellery are unjust or insufficiently grounded, but solely in order to forestall the complaints against the Chancellery that would inevitably follow from the colonists, or else to solicit Your Imperial Majesty's most gracious confirmation for its justification of the payment made hitherto; because the amount of subsistence money that has been paid here and en route to the place of settlement up to now was not determined by the Chancellery of Guardianship of Foreigners, but was established, as mentioned above, by the Minister who was in Hamburg, Mr. Musin-Pushkin, and subsequent colonists, pointing to the first arrivals, have come to imagine they have the right to demand the same payment, notwithstanding the published Supreme Manifesto of Your Imperial Majesty, in which no specific amount whatsoever is set for either subsistence or loan money for establishment, but only the necessary sustenance and the assistance and comfort required according to each person's condition are promised; which indeed it is impossible to fix, not only in advance at the issuing of the Manifesto but even now for all time to come, because prices everywhere, both for provisions and for other items needed for the colonists' establishment, are subject to constant change, not to mention the variation in loans that must be made for almost every family depending on people's circumstances.

Wherefore, presenting this to Your Imperial Majesty, I most humbly request Your most gracious decree.

Resolution. Since the Chancellery has indeed observed the disadvantages set forth in its decree, not only for the treasury but also for the colonists themselves, We consent to this, and command that henceforth in similar cases both Our interest and the colonists' own benefit be observed, not allowing them to fall into harmful idleness and luxury, and providing them with food and everything necessary for their establishment, in kind or in money, whichever proves more convenient, cheaper, and more beneficial for the colonists as circumstances permit, according to necessary need, without excess or deficiency, without fixing for all time the amount of these expenditures, which are subject to constant change.

*PSZRI. Coll. 1. Vol. XVII. Pp. 699-700.*

Published in: *German Colonists in the Age of Catherine* / Compiled by E. E. Lykova, M. I. Osekina. Moscow, 2004. Pp. 127-130.

### No. 20.

The present act displays the evident duplicity characteristic of many documents about colonists from the period of Catherine II. Thus G.G. Orlov writes of "very burdensome debts" for the colonists arising from their uneconomical use of subsistence money, while Catherine, in her resolution, writes that the colonists should be supplied with both food and "everything necessary" for setting up their households "without excess."

In reality the position of the Russian authorities on these questions was far from unambiguous. Subsistence money was classified as a non-repayable expenditure — subsequently colonists were required to repay it only upon leaving Russia or moving to another social estate (see docs. No. 47 and 62). Therefore there simply could have been no particular concern on their part about "burdensome debts" in this case. Only the authorities had a direct interest in economizing on these funds, as is evident from the document.

By contrast, expenditures on setting up households, according to Article 6, point 4 of Catherine II's manifesto of 22 July 1763 (doc. No. 11), were repayable, and here the authorities indulged in quite evident "excesses" in the Volga colonies — at least at first. They issued colonists very large monetary loans, the colonists fell into debt bondage, and as a result the return of the great majority of them to their homeland became practically impossible. See: Pleve I.R. Op. cit. P. 136.

### No. 21.

This was the first act to speak of the creation of colonies near Chernigov, although there is evidence that agitation among potential settlers in this direction was carried out even before its issuance (see: Pleve I.R. ## No. 22. ANNOUNCEMENT OF CATHERINE II ON THE TEMPORARY SUSPENSION OF THE ADMISSION OF FOREIGN COLONISTS (1766)

12,793. — November. Supremely confirmed announcement to foreigners wishing to emigrate to Russia for settlement.

Her Imperial Majesty has to this day taken no small satisfaction in seeing how the great benefits and advantages promised in Her Imperial Majesty's Supreme Manifesto published in the year [1]763 to all foreigners resettling in Russia, and especially the exact fulfillment thereof, have in actual fact produced equally great success; but as this summer such a multitude of foreigners emigrated to Russia for settlement as had not even been expected, not counting those people whom, owing to the lateness of the season, the Russian ministers and commissioners located in various foreign lands were compelled to refuse dispatch to Russia this year: consequently, effects quite contrary [to what was intended] have already resulted, which — to say nothing of the many other circumstances involving losses to the treasury — are especially displeasing to Her Imperial Majesty because they are highly burdensome to the colonists themselves, since it is not possible quickly to build houses for all of them nor to supply them with other necessities, and many of them, living for a considerable time in lodgings in idleness and receiving subsistence money, will be compelled to fall deeply into debt: and therefore Her Imperial Majesty, out of her innate magnanimity and love of humanity, being concerned that in future such persons desiring to settle in Russia should not, out of ignorance, emigrate all at once in excessive numbers and thereby subject themselves to an even greater burden of debt than those who emigrated this year, has been Supremely pleased to resolve to suspend for the future the emigration of foreigners to Russia for settlement, until such time as all the colonists who have emigrated hitherto, having received houses, the necessary implements, livestock, and all other requirements, shall actually have entered into work and shall have their own means of subsistence.

As soon as all the colonists already in Russia shall have been brought to such a state as described, the Chancellery of Guardianship of Foreigners, by the Supreme command of Her Imperial Majesty, will not fail to notify the public in good time, describing in full detail on what basis the emigration of colonists, their conveyance to St. Petersburg and further to the place of settlement, as well as their maintenance en route and during their stay at every place until they begin their household and work, are to be arranged, so that in future no disputes, deceptions, or confusions, which have sometimes arisen up to now [DOCUMENTS 57]

from ignorance and misunderstanding of the true force of the Manifesto, but more from the deliberate misinterpretation thereof out of self-interest by certain well-known persons, shall be able to occur; and for the satisfaction and relief of the settlers expected in future, building will be carried out in advance, so that the settlers arriving in future will find at the place of their future dwelling, if not the full number of houses for everyone, then at least enough ready houses that all of them may not only freely lodge and live peacefully in them until the remaining ones are built, but may also be able to carry out farming and other household work.

*PSZRI. Coll. 1. Vol. XVII. Pp. 1067-1068.*

Published in: *German Colonists in the Age of Catherine* / Compiled by E. E. Lykova, M. I. Osekina. Moscow, 2004. Pp. 133-135.

### No. 22.

By the time this act was adopted it had become finally clear to the Russian authorities that the settling-in of the tens of thousands of colonists who had already arrived would require much time and resources, and the reception of new settlers was prudently postponed to better times. Over the remaining 30 years of Catherine II's reign, those better times practically never came. Only in the last years of Catherine's reign was a small number of new colonists settled in southern Russia.

It did not prove possible to stop the reception of settlers immediately at the end of 1766 — the contract with the *vyzyvatel'* Beauregard had been concluded through 1768, and he, in defiance of the prohibition, sent another 100 families to Russia. ## No. 24. DECREE OF CATHERINE II ON THE TRANSFORMATION OF THE SCHOOL AT THE ST. PETERSBURG LUTHERAN CHURCH OF ST. PETER INTO THE CHIEF GERMAN SCHOOL (1783)

15,826. — August 29. Nominal [decree], given to the Senate. — On the school established in St. Petersburg at the Evangelical Church of St. Peter being the chief public normal school for those using the German language, and on the establishment thereat of a special directorate or board over all public schools of the German language.

Our Senate is aware that We, having resolved to establish public schools everywhere throughout Our Empire, set up for that purpose a special Commission under Our own supervision, entrusting it not only with composing a plan of instruction suited thereto, but also with devising a method of instruction, together with the publication of textbooks, so that perfect uniformity might be observed therein and the most convenient means devised. The said Commission, continuing its labors with success, has already achieved the actual founding of several Russian public schools and of the chief school itself; but as among the regions subject to Our scepter there are also those whose inhabitants use the German language, We have undertaken to extend Our concern for the establishment of public schools also for the benefit of these Our subjects. With this intention We turn Our attention to the school established in St. Petersburg at the Lutheran Church of St. Peter, and furnished with Our Imperial charter, which, to Our satisfaction, has already borne good fruit and considerable benefit to society, and from which We expect the same in the matter now at hand. We therefore command that it be the chief public normal school for Our subjects using the German language; and as its school assembly, preachers, and chief teachers have recognized that the methods of instruction devised for normal schools are the easiest and most reliable, these are to be adopted and used in all German schools in both Our capitals, in Riga, Reval, Vyborg, and in other Governorates wherever such schools are needed; and therefore the teachers to be appointed therein in future are obliged to be trained in that method, either at this chief normal German school of St. Peter, or at one being established under its administration on the same basis elsewhere, or at another similar one established under the supervision of the Commission on the Establishment of Public Schools. We do not forbid the local chief normal school to retain the classes already established in it beyond the general plan, or to add such others as circumstances may require, provided that for the teaching of all these classes no methods other than those proper to normal schools are adopted, and that all arrangements likewise be drawn from the same source. For better supervision and management We establish at the St. Petersburg chief German school a special directorate, or board, over all public schools of the German language in Our Empire, composed of the warden of the Lutheran Church of St. Peter, of its two preachers, and of the overseer of the normal school attached to this church, adding to them one further member of the same confession, and appointing to this position at present Our Active Chamberlain Count Munnich. This directorate, or board, is to be subordinate to the Commission on the Establishment of Public Schools, from which it is to receive special instructions concerning the details of that directorate's duties, to which it is to submit for resolution any doubtful cases, and through which, where necessary, to request Our commands. The chief efforts of this directorate, or board, must be directed toward the speediest composition and publication of school textbooks for use in all public schools of the German language. These books must, as far as possible, correspond to those introduced or to be published for the Russian public schools, differing only in language; the printing thereof We reserve exclusively to the said directorate, for its own benefit and that of the schools under its supervision. ## No. 28. REPORT ON THE ST. PETERSBURG COLONISTS, CONFIRMED BY PAUL I (1797)

18,006. — June 20. Most Highly confirmed report of the Expedition of State Economy, Guardianship of Foreigners and Rural Husbandry. On levies from the St. Petersburg colonists; on satisfying them with land at 30 desyatinas per tiaglo (household labor-unit); on establishing practical instruction in agriculture for the settlers; on the propagation of the potato in Russia; on the establishment of artificial meadows; and on the non-resettlement of colonists to other Governorates.

Report. In undertaking active care for the foreign resettlers, primary attention has been directed to the colonies situated in St. Petersburg Governorate, which, by virtue of their location, are subject to the direct observation of the Expedition, and are to serve as an example of good order and rural husbandry, so that, in accordance with the beneficent intentions of Your Imperial Majesty, all the foreign colonies settled in Russia may be brought into a better condition.

For the most reliable and detailed knowledge of the colonies of this Governorate, in addition to the notifications and inquiries received by the Expedition, one of its Members, Active State Councillor Khitrovo, was commissioned to make a personal inspection, to go into the particulars of their husbandry, to identify their shortcomings, and to compile a detailed description, which is hereby most humbly submitted to Your Imperial Majesty. In this Governorate there are four colonies: the first, in Sofia District on the great Tsarskoye Selo road, founded in 1765; at its settlement 19,335 rubles 14 kopecks were expended; it has 22 households, in which there are now 86 male and 93 female souls. By agreement, 660 desyatinas of land were to have been allotted to them; but only 524 desyatinas are in their possession, of which one and a half desyatinas per household are plowed in the field, and the same amount behind the households for potatoes, while the rest of the land is under a small pasture, forest, and meadows. By the agreement they were obliged, after the passage of 10 years, to pay 1 ruble 22½ kopecks for each desyatina, and to repay the debt, after the 15 exempt years, over the following five years; but owing to their insolvency, by the Nominal decree of November 13, 1793, it was ordered that 61 kopecks be collected from them for every desyatina of suitable land; and toward payment of the debt, 10 rubles per household; which payment they have made without arrears from DOCUMENTS___________________________________________________________________________________ 63

1792, that is, 322 rubles 26 kopecks for land, and 220 rubles for the loan, 542 rubles 26 kopecks in all. And by this reckoning, each colonist pays 6 rubles 30 kopecks per year.

The second colony, Izhora, was settled in two villages in 1765, the first with 12, the second with 16 households, and in both there are now 106 male and 810 [108?] female souls. 25,234 rubles 91¼ kopecks were expended on their establishment. They were to have been allotted 840 desyatinas of suitable land, but 1,340 desyatinas were surveyed for them under the designation "suitable," of which only a small part is fit for tillage; up to 1½ desyatinas per household in the field are allotted, after setting aside land for planting potatoes. By agreement they were obliged to pay the land tax and repay the debt on the same basis as the Tsarskoye Selo colonists, and, by reason of an equal insolvency, the same decree of 1793 prescribed that 30¾ kopecks be collected from them per desyatina in land tax, and 5 rubles per household toward repayment of the loan, which they have paid into the treasury without arrears since 1792; that is: from 840 desyatinas, 258 rubles 30 kopecks in land tax, and 140 rubles in debt payments, 398 rubles 30 kopecks in all; consequently each colonist owes 3 rubles 75 kopecks per year.

The third colony, called Saratovka, has existed since 1765 on the bank of the Neva River; it has 60 households, in which there are 227 male and 231 female souls. 78,297 rubles 86½ kopecks were expended on the settlement; they were to have been allotted 2,100 desyatinas of land, but they received into their possession only 1,656 desyatinas of suitable land, of which 1½ desyatinas per household are plowed in the field, and the same amount under potatoes. By the agreement they were obliged to pay, after the passage of 10 exempt years, land money at 1 ruble 22½ kopecks per desyatina per year; and to repay the sums issued as a loan, after those same 10 years, over the following 6 years; however, owing to their lack of means and the accumulation of arrears, in 1793, on August 17, by Nominal decree issued upon the report of the Treasury Chamber, the voluntary consent of these colonists was confirmed, whereby they would pay land money for 1,500 desyatinas deemed truly suitable at 61¼ kopecks per desyatina, and toward payment of the loan money and prior arrears, 5 rubles per household; in fulfillment of which, since 1792 they have paid into the treasury properly and without arrears 300 rubles, and 918 rubles 75 kopecks in land tax, so that per soul it comes to 5 rubles 37 kopecks per year, without small fractions. Active State Councillor Khitrovo notes that the resettlers of this colony are less prosperous than the others, and their households are in worse repair, a considerable cause of which may be regarded as the crown tavern situated there. The Expedition most humbly requests the Most High command of Your Imperial Majesty for its removal from the Saratovka colony, together with an instruction for the future that no crown taverns be established in any of the colonies in St. Petersburg Governorate.

The fourth colony, Yamburg, was founded in 1767 in three villages, in which there were 92½ families; of these, pursuant to the aforementioned decree of August 17, 1793, issued upon the report of the Treasury Chamber, owing to the scarcity of land suitable for tillage, 272 souls of both sexes were resettled to Ekaterinoslav Governorate, so that in all three villages there now remain 22 households, in which there are 81 male and 63 female souls; 81,970 rubles 38¾ kopecks were expended from the treasury on the establishment and transport of these settlers; they were to have been given 35 desyatinas per household, 3,237½ desyatinas in all, but they were allotted 2,927 desyatinas of land of various quality, and now, among those who remain, only 509 desyatinas are considered suitable. These colonists were obliged by agreement to pay, after the passage of 13 exempt years, 1 ruble 22½ kopecks per desyatina, and to repay within three years the money expended on them, after 10 exempt years; however, the complete impossibility of this given their condition prompted the instruction in the aforesaid decree that, following the resettlement to Ekaterinoslav Governorate, the colonists remaining behind should be satisfied with land, given a five-year exemption, after which they would pay a tax equal to that of the Izhora colonists, at 30¾ kopecks per desyatina, and that the treasury debt, together with arrears, amounting to 137,493 rubles 85 kopecks, should not be collected from them. Thus, at the proper time, they will pay 156 rubles 51¾ kopecks in land money, and each colonist will owe 1 ruble 93¾ kopecks per year.

There is a sufficient quantity of forest attached to these colonies, with the exception of Yamburg, where almost all of it has been destroyed. At the first, on the Tsarskoye Selo road, there is also timber forest divided into 22 plots according to the number of households, while the firewood forest is held in common by the whole colony, from which 8 sazhens per household are cut in the month of February. In the possession of the Izhora colony there is little timber forest, from which the necessary quantity is issued to each family by communal decision; from the firewood forest, 8 sazhens per household are cut in February. At Saratovka the firewood forest is divided into 60 plots, and each household is permitted to cut 4 sazhens of three-log firewood in February.

From all the circumstances most humbly described here for Your Imperial Majesty, the Expedition concludes, from the conduct of former matters relating to the St. Petersburg colonies, that if to this day they have not brought the State the benefit that was hoped for at their establishment, this arose chiefly from the failure at that time to examine in due detail both the actual needs and the means employed to that end. The very construction of the settlements now existing shows how little thought was given to their agricultural condition and to providing the means for rural husbandry. The unsuitability and insufficiency of the lands allotted to them are the cause of the non-return of treasury funds expended without due examination. The innocent settlers established here, laboring diligently by the strength of their hands in the sweat of their brow, are unable within a year's time to extract from the land a tax worthy of their Sovereign and provisions for their own subsistence. A single glance from Your Imperial Majesty, as Father of the Fatherland, will revive these toiling people.

On this unshakeable foundation the Expedition most humbly submits the following opinion: 1. Would it please Your Imperial Majesty, having Most Graciously confirmed the present tax payments of the St. Petersburg colonies according to the latest decrees, to permit the Expedition, at a time convenient for these colonists and upon a perceptible improvement of their condition, to endeavor to bring their land assessment, on the principles Most Highly confirmed by Your Imperial Majesty concerning appanage estates, first to a third, and then to a half, of the annual income from each desyatina of arable land, assuming an ordinary grain sowing, an ordinary harvest, and ordinary grain prices. 2. That the Expedition examine the accounts by which the treasury debts charged to those colonies have been calculated, debts known to it only from summary statements, and that, by force of Your Imperial Majesty's Most High confirmation, it might seek out and demand payment wherever traces of them are found. 3. Having regard to the proximity of the colonies to the Capital of Your Imperial Majesty, to arrange matters so that no less than 30 desyatinas of land fall to each tiaglo, distributing it as follows: 4 desyatinas for the homestead, vegetable garden, and potatoes; 3 desyatinas for plowing in each of the three fields; and 17 desyatinas for meadows, forest, pasture, and for the increase of families until the next census. Such an abundant quantity of land, if it cannot be worked by the colonists themselves for lack of hands in the colony, may afford the opportunity to establish practical instruction in agriculture for the settlers. Foreign resettlers, having brought the art of husbandry from their homeland and having learned by experience to apply it to our climate and the nature of our soil, could serve as instructors for laborers sent to them for training by landowners and dispatched from crown villages. A sufficient quantity of land would permit various experiments toward the further propagation of the potato in Russia and toward the establishment of artificial meadows hitherto unknown among us. In this the Expedition must without fail see to it that each tiaglo actually works its thirteen desyatinas. 4. A consequence of this may be the resettlement of some of these colonists to new lands, but only in accordance with their own voluntary wishes; for which purpose a statement received by the Expedition from the Department of the Surveying Senate is appended here. 5. Without this, the inconvenience already observed will arise, namely that not only those summoned and settled, but also the tillers newly born, will turn to trades or crafts unrelated to agriculture; of which there is evidence in the description of Active State Councillor Khitrovo appended here. 6. Although the resettlement of colonists who wish it is attended by expenses that will inevitably be required, which will not be recovered except from future revenues from them, the colonists remaining in St. Petersburg Governorate after the resettlers' departure will not reduce the treasury taxes by the number who have left, because they pay not per soul but according to the quantity of land in their use. 7. Having freed the St. Petersburg colonies from various present departments, for closer and more convenient oversight of them and for administration corresponding to their actual benefit, to appoint over them an overseer or manager dependent solely on the Expedition, who, overseeing rural policing, internal good order, and a faithful accounting of State taxes, could impart practical instruction in agriculture, animal husbandry, and rural economy, conducive to the enrichment of the settlers and proven by actual experiments. 8. The manager appointed to the St. Petersburg colonies, being answerable for everything, is obliged to remit all treasury dues without arrears to the District Treasuries or to other proper offices, so that the colonists need have no dealings whatsoever with the District Treasurer, the Rural Commissioner, or the Ispravnik (district police officer). 9. Concerning everything pertaining to his duties, and concerning various circumstances and incidents that occur, the manager must at all times report to the Member of the Expedition having special charge of the St. Petersburg colonies.

Such is the opinion which the Expedition most humbly submits for the Most High Sovereign consideration, and awaits the All-Highest decree of Your Imperial Majesty.

Resolution. The colonists are not to be resettled to other Governorates, but are to be satisfied with the proportion of land laid down, in the Expedition's opinion, out of the adjoining and nearby crown tracts; as for everything else set forth in this report, it is to be carried out and observed.

### No. 33.

Dorpat University was founded as early as 1632, during the period when Dorpat was under Swedish rule. After the city was captured by Russian troops during the Russo-Swedish War of 1656–1658, classes were moved to Reval, and in 1665 they ceased altogether. The university's activity resumed in 1690, when Dorpat once again belonged to Sweden. In 1699 the university was transferred to Pernau, where it functioned until the city was captured by Russian troops (1710) during the Great Northern War of 1700–1721. Despite repeated petitions by the Livonian nobility and promises from Peter I, and later Empress Anna Ivanovna, the question of restoring Dorpat University remained unresolved until the adoption of the present act by Paul I. In 1800 Paul leaned toward another option mentioned in this document — organizing a university in Mitau — and in the end Dorpat University opened only in 1802, already under Alexander I. The university's charter, adopted in 1803, essentially corresponded to the plan approved by Paul, but there was of course no longer any question of naming the university after the emperor who had been killed in 1801, its true patron. Dorpat University was distinguished both by a degree of university autonomy unheard of in Russia and by its adherence to German university traditions. Teaching there was conducted mainly in German, and up to 80% of the students were Germans. The university played a leading role in training Lutheran pastors for Russia and, more broadly, in shaping the Russian-German intelligentsia. Many world-famous scholars studied and taught there (see the Biographical Commentary). From the 1830s the authorities began a process of Russifying the university. This intensified especially after it was made subject to the university statute of 1884, which sharply restricted university autonomy. As a result, many German professors were forced to leave Russia. In 1893 Dorpat was renamed Yuryev, and Dorpat University became Yuryev University, finally losing its German character. See: Ulrich M. Dorpat University // Germans of Russia: An Encyclopedia. Vol. 1. Pp. 706–708.

## No. 63. DECREE OF ALEXANDER I ON THE RESETTLEMENT OF GERMAN COLONISTS FROM POLAND TO BESSARABIA (1814)

25,522. — January 22. Personal decree given to the Minister of Internal Affairs. — On permitting German colonists to resettle from the Duchy of Warsaw to Bessarabia.

The German colonists in the Duchy of Warsaw, seeking a peaceful refuge in the Russian Empire given its present situation, have expressed a wish to resettle to Bessarabia at their own expense. In deference to this wish of theirs, I have found it well to permit them to carry it out on the same basis as that on which the Bulgarians and Serbs settled in Bessarabia. Taking into consideration, however, that they have suffered many misfortunes owing to various circumstances, and that some of them may prove unable to accomplish this at their own expense without any outside assistance, I leave it to your judgment, in carrying out this resettlement, to determine whether it will be necessary to provide them with some assistance from the treasury, both for subsistence and for setting up their households. You are to determine the measure of this assistance with due regard for the benefit of the treasury, bearing in mind that this settlement in general is intended to be arranged not at the expense of the treasury but at the colonists' own expense, and that temporary assistance is to be rendered to them only in cases of the most extreme necessity. In cases of necessity on this subject, I authorize you to address representations to the Committee of Ministers. The local authorities shall be obliged to assist you in this matter. Entrusting this to your special care, I expect assured success in it; you are to report to Me on this from time to time, bringing to the attention of the Committee of Ministers all measures that you undertake.

### No. 63.

The appearance of this act is connected with the fact that, as a result of the Russo-Turkish War of 1806–1812, Turkey had to cede Bessarabia — the territory between the Dniester and Prut rivers — to Russia. Bessarabia was inhabited mainly by Romanians (Moldavians); its southern districts were sparsely settled, and part of the population had fled. This prompted the recruitment of foreign colonists, including Germans, to the region.

German colonists had been settled in a number of districts of the Duchy (Principality) of Warsaw at the beginning of the 19th century, when these districts belonged to Prussia. In 1812 the duchy, seized by Napoleon, served as a base for the French army's invasion of Russia; during the war years its inhabitants suffered from military operations. At the time this decree was issued, the future fate of the Duchy of Warsaw had not yet been determined. Following the outcome of the Congress of Vienna of 1814–1815, a substantial part of it passed to Russia, while Prussia once again received the districts that had previously belonged to it.

The Warsaw colonists, who resettled to the Bessarabia Region in 1814–1816, founded in Bendery District (later Akkerman District) 11 Lutheran colonies and one Catholic colony (Krasnoye), named in honor of the sites of battles of the Patriotic War of 1812 and the subsequent foreign campaigns of the Russian army. These colonies formed two districts: Maloyaroslavets — Maloyaroslavets I (Kirgiz, Wittenberg), Kulm (Madar, Paulsberg), Tarutino (Anchokrak), Krasnoye, Fère-Champenoise I (Alt-Fère-Champenoise, Old Champenoise); Klyastitsy — Klyastitsy (Chaga), Leipzig (Skinos), Berezina (Kagalnik, Rotunda), Borodino (Sak), Paris (Vesyoly Kut), Arciz I (Alt-Arciz, Old Arciz), Brienne (Peterwunsch).

## No. 64. DECISION OF THE COMMITTEE OF MINISTERS ON ADMINISTERING THE OATH TO THE BESSARABIAN COLONISTS AND APPOINTING CLERGY TO THEM (1814)

25,622. — July 17. Resolution of the Committee of Ministers. — Concerning the Warsaw colonists who refuse to take the oath, and on granting the clergy in the Bessarabian colonies the same benefits enjoyed by the clergy in the colonies of the Novorossiysk territory.

The Committee, having heard the attached memorandum of the Minister of Internal Affairs concerning the Warsaw colonists who refuse to take the oath, having confirmed DOCUMENTS____________________________________________________________________________ 173

the opinion of the Minister of Internal Affairs, authorized him to make the appropriate arrangements for its implementation.

Memorandum of the Minister of Internal Affairs.

The Military and Civil Chief of Bessarabia, Engineer-Major-General Harting, reports that the Warsaw colonists who have already arrived at the place of settlement, numbering 31 families, have refused to take the oath of citizenship.

Those colonists give as the reason for this that they did not receive in Bessarabia the 270 rubles in silver, the pair of oxen, the two horses, and the two cows per family that had allegedly been promised to them in Warsaw by the Governor-General there.

Engineer-Major-General Harting, recognizing that exhortation through the clergy of their respective confessions may have the greatest effect on them, has already applied to the Kherson Military Governor requesting that a Catholic priest and a Lutheran pastor be sent there temporarily for this purpose. Meanwhile, he requests the appointment of permanent clergy for the colonists resettling to Bessarabia.

He further requests instructions as to how he should proceed if the colonists persist in their obstinacy and do not take the oath.

Reference. I. By the privileges most graciously granted to the Warsaw colonists, which were communicated to me by the Warsaw Governor-General, it was promised that indigent families would receive a loan, in addition to subsistence money, of 270 rubles each; other families would receive, according to their means, whatever was needed for initial establishment. But it was not stated in the privileges that this money should be designated in silver, or that any further assistance beyond this had been promised them, and the Warsaw Governor-General wrote nothing to me on this matter.

By the Imperial decree given in my name on January 22 of this year, it was confirmed that assistance to the Warsaw colonists should be rendered only in cases of the most extreme necessity.

II. By the Imperial Manifesto of July 22, 1763, it was ordained that every foreigner arriving to settle in Russia should take an oath of citizenship.

However, both by this Manifesto and by the special rules issued pursuant to the report of the Ministry of Internal Affairs on the reception of colonists for settlement in the Novorossiysk territory, most graciously confirmed on February 23, 1804, all emigrants are permitted to leave Russia upon paying their debts and the prescribed dues.

These rules further provide that if any of those who have arrived for settlement proves disobedient and unsubmissive to the established authorities, such a person, after collection from him of what is owed to the treasury, is to be expelled abroad.

III. By the journals of the Committee of Messrs. Ministers of August 25, 1809, and March 23, 1810, most graciously confirmed, the following benefits have been granted to the clergy appointed to the colonists in the Novorossiysk territory:

1. Salary is paid from the treasury: to the Protestant pastor, 400 rubles, and to the Catholic priest, 300 rubles per year, for 10 years, which are granted to the colonists as a grace period exempting them from payment of taxes.

2. A loan is also given from the treasury for setting up a household:

To Catholic clergy, as they have no families, 300 rubles, and to Lutheran clergy, 600 rubles.

3. Land is allotted: to pastors, 120 desyatinas of arable and hay land, and to priests half that amount, plus 3 desyatinas for a house and garden.

In addition to this, 4) Imperial orders were given for the construction in the colonies of houses for the clergy and their assistants to live in. For travel there they are provided by the treasury with post-horse money, and to many of them a special relocation allowance was most graciously granted, namely 600 rubles each.

Opinion of the Minister. Given the significant number of colonists emigrating to Bessarabia, whose number already amounts to 1,072 families and about 5,500 souls of both sexes, the appointment to them of clergy of their own confessions is in any case absolutely necessary for the maintenance of their morals.

I propose that these clergy should be granted the same benefits as have been granted, by the most graciously confirmed resolutions of the Committee of Messrs. Ministers, to the clergy attached to the colonies in the Novorossiysk territory, with assistance to be given according to their needs, not exceeding what was previously allotted.

For the present, it will be necessary to appoint one priest and one pastor.

As for the colonists' refusal to take the oath of citizenship, by which they probably hope to obtain the excessive assistance they demand, taking into consideration: 1) that there has been no precedent for such a refusal in previous resettlements, and 2) that these people, without taking the oath, cannot be either loyal subjects or genuine inhabitants of state lands, — I consider it necessary, in order to avoid further disturbances, and especially useless expenditures from the treasury, to authorize the Civil and Military Chief in Bessarabia, should exhortation prove ineffective, to expel abroad the instigators of such obstinacy, after taking back from them everything that may have been given to them by the treasury for their establishment; and should this example fail to affect the rest, to proceed in the same manner with all who refuse to take the oath.
