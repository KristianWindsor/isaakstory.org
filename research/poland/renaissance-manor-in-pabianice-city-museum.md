# "The Castle" — Renaissance Manor in Pabianice

The Renaissance manor, colloquially known as the "Castle," is one of the unique and valuable monuments of 16th-century Polish architecture.

It is situated in the Dobrzynka River valley at 1 Stary Rynek Street, on the northern side of the street. A wooden manor house—the residence of the *regens* (administrator) of the Kraków Chapter—already existed on this site in the Middle Ages; it is mentioned by Długosz in his *Liber Beneficiorum Dioecesis Cracoviensis*. The structure was surrounded by branches of the Dobrzynka River, a moat, and a palisade. However, the wooden buildings of old Pabianice were highly susceptible to fire. During one such blaze—which broke out at a fair in 1532—fire consumed almost the entire town, including the manor. In 1550, the Chapter passed a resolution to construct a new, imposing house to serve as a residence for canons visiting on various matters. It had to be sufficiently spacious, featuring living quarters, bedrooms, and other rooms required by visiting inspectors and the estate administrator (*regens*). The building also housed the treasury and all documents and records related to the estate's administration. Orders were issued to officials from this location, and tax collectors brought here the levies gathered from the local population. The new masonry manor was erected between 1565 and 1571.

Construction was initiated by the then-leaseholder, Canon Stanisław Dąbrowski. Wawrzyniec Lorek served as both architect and builder. Administrators of the Kraków Chapter maintained their headquarters here until 1795. Following the Second Partition of Poland, the manor became the seat of the Prussian administration. In 1822, it was made available to the weavers and cloth makers settling in Pabianice. Baruch describes this era as follows: *"(…) when the government of the time granted various privileges to the German weavers settling here, they received, among other things, free space for their workshops. The castle halls were made available to the newcomers, and it was then—within the old castle walls, amidst the clatter of over a dozen hand looms—that the Pabianice industry was born, heralding a new era of development and material prosperity for the town."* Until 1832, one of the halls served as a chapel for Protestant immigrants. From 1833, the manor functioned as the town hall; it also housed a police detention center. During World War II, it was designated as the headquarters for the Nazi occupation authorities. After the war, in 1948, the building was handed over to the museum. It housed a permanent historical exhibition on the history of Pabianice and hosted temporary exhibitions, while concerts were held in the first-floor fireplace room. In 1997, renovation and conservation work began based on a design by Professor Henryk Jaworowski of the Technical University of Łódź. The project aimed to stabilize the building's spatial structure, preserve historical interior features, and upgrade technical infrastructure. The manor was reopened to visitors in 2005. It currently continues to house a museum.

In the 16th century, the Pabianice structure was referred to as a tower, a fortified house, a residence, or a stone house; the term "manor" (*dwór*) was applied instead to the entire complex, which included outbuildings scattered among courtyards and gardens, as well as the wooden residence of the estate's deputy starosta. The fortified house was surrounded by earthen ramparts and a wooden fence, featuring an entrance gate that was partially constructed of masonry. From the 17th century onwards, the term "Castle" came into use for the complex, though over time this designation narrowed to refer specifically to the masonry manor house itself. For its seat in Pabianice, the Kraków Chapter chose a design reflecting the forms of rural noble manor houses with defensive characteristics that were prevalent at the time. The structure of the estate center—based primarily on a manorial farm—closely resembled the largely self-sufficient manor complexes typical of that era. These complexes featured extensive outbuildings arranged around the residential core, all enclosed within a ring of earth-and-timber fortifications. The Pabianice manor stands out among similar structures due to the originality of its exterior form, which has undergone minimal alteration. It is also one of the few manor houses with a construction history documented in archives and a known builder.

The building is laid out on a roughly rectangular plan, featuring corner pavilions (alcoves) at opposite corners (northeast and southwest). Adjoining the western wall is a rectangular avant-corps and a quarter-circular tower. This single-story building, which includes a basement, is constructed of brick and finished with plaster. Its recessed roof is concealed behind attic walls. The design of the attic varies across the different elevations; on the north and south sides, it runs parallel to the roof ridges and rises to a height roughly equal to that of a full story (approximately one-third of the building's total height). On the side elevations (west and east), the attic resembles the gable treatments common to the era, concealing the ends of the recessed roof while echoing the undulating profile of its cross-section. This unifying horizontal attic band acts as a counterpoint to the vertical emphasis of the walls. The walls are articulated by arched recesses framed by contrasting vertical buttresses that cast deep shadows, thereby distinguishing the smooth surfaces of the corner pavilions. The western wall is further embellished by a lower avant-corps—topped with a gable-roof and an ornate gable—and a quarter-circular turret housing a staircase. Original profiled sandstone window surrounds have been preserved in the reveals of the first-floor windows. On them, one can see small heraldic shields bearing the Aaron coat of arms—the three crowns of the Kraków Chapter—and the Poraj coat of arms—a five-petaled rose belonging to Canon Stanisław Dąbrowski—as well as a shield displaying the canon’s initials, SD. Carved in stone in Renaissance majuscule lettering on the lintel of one of the windows on the western façade is the inscription *SIC TRASIT GLA MVNDI* (*Sic transit gloria mundi* – Thus passes the glory of the world).

The manor house erected by Lorek underwent no major alterations over the ensuing centuries. Its appearance is documented—from the second half of the 17th century through the end of the 18th century—in numerous descriptions found in inspection records commissioned every few years by the Kraków Chapter. A 1809 site plan of the castle complex depicts the manor with an outline matching its current footprint. The courtyard to the south—where Zamkowa Street now runs—was once enclosed by outbuildings and gardens, while a gate to the west led to a bridge crossing the river. Although the manor's surroundings have changed, the building's massing remains close to its original form. The only significant change was the relocation of the entrance; originally situated on the southern façade in the bay closest to the corner pavilion (*alkierz*), it was moved for a time in the late 19th century to the pavilion's southern wall, and later shifted to its eastern wall. In the early 20th century, a quarter-circular turret housing a staircase was also added to the western side.

Inside, the tour begins on the lowest level. The basement rooms, deepened during the most recent renovation, feature barrel vaults. This space is slated to house exhibits showcasing the city's earliest history. The ground-floor rooms—also featuring vaults similar to those in the basement—are accessed via a vestibule where a hearth (fireplace) was discovered during a 1947 renovation. The rooms on the first level originally served administrative functions: this was where visitors were received, levies were collected, and the treasury was located. The large chamber to the right of the vestibule once contained a "green tiled stove and a masonry chimney," as well as "benches on two sides and a simple table." Among the original ground-floor interiors, the Renaissance brick flooring—hidden for centuries beneath layers of later flooring and parquet but revealed again during the recent renovation—has been preserved. In the room adjacent to the northeast corner chamber, the vault is noteworthy; sections of it are covered with unique, hand-troweled plasterwork dating back to the Renaissance. Conservation work also preserved exposed sections of later plasterwork from the 17th century. Remnants of the manor's original defensive design are visible in the corner chambers, which were equipped with narrow loopholes. The walls of the northeast corner chamber are adorned with polychrome paintings created in 1917 by the Pabianice-based artist Jan Szulc.

They depict St. Matthew’s Church, the 1432 visit of King Władysław Jagiełło to Pabianice, Nicolaus Copernicus—who, according to legend, was born in nearby Łaskowice—and the wooden houses on St. John Street. Currently, the ground-floor rooms serve as exhibition spaces. Access to the upper floor is via a staircase leading from the entrance hall; a stone portal with a Renaissance profile has been preserved at the entrance. The staircase terminates on the upper floor of the avant-corps in a small vestibule, known in the 18th century as an "altana" (loggia or sunroom). Renaissance double windows on three sides flooded this room with light; one of them was later bricked up and obscured by a staircase leading to the attic. The entrance to the stairwell from this vestibule is framed by a portal—likely dating to the first half of the 18th century—featuring a prominent, richly profiled entablature. Interestingly, the staircase leading to the upper floor was originally open-air; looking out the window from the "altana," one can see the extension that encloses it, situated between the corner chamber and the avant-corps. The ceilings in the first-floor rooms—originally beamed—were concealed during renovations in 1834–35 by new ceilings featuring profiled wooden cornices; the original beams were only rediscovered during research in 1995 and subsequently restored to their earlier appearance. A few steps lead from the "altana" into a large entrance hall. The spacious room beyond the hall, illuminated by five windows, served as the dining chamber. It retains a fireplace featuring decoration from the 1740s. Recent conservation work revealed 16th-century Renaissance polychrome painting in the room, along with later layers dating from the 18th and 19th centuries. In two rooms within the rear section of the building, notable features include oriel windows that project beyond the façade and overhang the vaulted spaces between the buttresses; these windows are covered by transverse barrel vaults. One of them—situated above the moat—served as the *locus secretus* (latrine) for the canons who resided there. An exhibition recreating the historic interiors is currently being arranged in the upper-floor rooms, while the fireplace hall serves as a prestigious venue for important meetings, civic ceremonies, lectures, concerts, and even weddings.

During the most recent renovation, the attic space was also adapted for functional use, housing workshops and a classroom designed primarily for meetings with children and young people during museum educational sessions. Thanks to renovation and conservation work, the historic building is regaining its former splendor. Research conducted between 1994 and 1996 revealed that a much larger portion of the original historic fabric had survived than previously thought. This made it possible to recover Renaissance ceilings and floors and to restore the polychrome decorations in the fireplace room—partly dating back to the 16th century, with the 18th-century illusionistic polychromy largely reconstructed. These works also yielded interesting archaeological observations in the manor's surroundings; for instance, brick and stone walls were uncovered, as well as a massive wooden structure (possibly a reinforcement for a moat crossing) on ​​the northern side. Inside the building and in excavations outside its walls, thousands of fragments were found—including pottery, stove tiles, glass bottles and windowpanes, and metal and wooden objects—dating from the late Middle Ages (14th century) to modern times. One of the most interesting discoveries is a "treasure" of coins from the 17th and 18th centuries.
