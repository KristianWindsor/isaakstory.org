# History of the Germans in the Łódź area

The history of Germans in the Łódź region begins at the end of the 18th century. By this time, the descendants of earlier waves of immigration in the Middle Ages and the early modern period had already been assimilated. At the end of the 18th century, the local nobility began recruiting German farmers, known as " Hollanders," whose ancestors had lived as settlers in Polish-speaking areas for several generations, establishing new villages from west to east. The oldest settlement charter from the Łódź area dates from 1782. By 1800, over 50 settlements had been founded by German farmers. At the beginning of the 19th century, urban craftsmen followed the farmers and played a key role in the region's industrialization. They mostly migrated to existing towns, but some new towns were also founded. The influx of both groups of settlers continued until about 1840, but even after that, the German minority continued to grow due to a surplus of births. The January Uprising of 1863–64 and the emancipation of the peasants in 1864 worsened conditions for the German settlers in the region, leading to a significant exodus to Volhynia in the second half of the 19th century. Closer contact with their homeland only resumed during the occupation of the First World War. The period between the two world wars was a particularly difficult time for the Germans of Łódź: on the one hand, the newly established Polish state harbored great distrust towards them; on the other, they faced increasing influence and manipulation by the propaganda of the German Reich. Furthermore, the economic situation was dire. The invasion of Poland in September 1939 marked the beginning of a new chapter, but after initial relief following the German occupation of the region, many of the local Germans were shaken and disturbed by the persecution and oppression of Jews and Poles. The end of the Second World War meant the loss of their homeland for a large portion of the Germans of Łódź, and assimilation for those who remained.

In the 1930s, around 180,000 Germans lived in the Łódź area, between the Warta, Bzura and Pilica rivers, of whom about 60,000 to 70,000 lived in Łódź itself. In the 19th century, they were at times the largest population group in the city. [1]

## Middle Ages and early modern period

As part of the German eastward expansion, German immigrants arrived in this region as early as the Middle Ages. [2] In the following centuries, these Germans merged with their Polish surroundings. [3]

During the 16th and 17th centuries, a large number of Protestant German cloth makers, primarily from Silesia, immigrated to Poland as a result of the Counter-Reformation. [4] [5] While most settled in more westerly regions, some also

reached the Łódź area. [6] As part of this wave of immigration, German cloth makers were also settled in separate districts in Brzeziny, [7] Rzgów, and Pabianice, and probably also in Lutomiersk. [8]

In the 18th century, when German farmers immigrated to this region again, the descendants of the early immigrants had already been assimilated.

## 1782 to 1793: Beginning of the rural settlement

In the 1780s, the modern settlement movement of German farmers, which had been advancing eastward from west to east since the 16th century, reached the area of ​what would later become the Łódź industrial region. [9] The German farmers used new techniques to make land usable that had previously been considered unsuitable for agriculture. [10] [11] Settlers of Pomeranian origin came from the

northwest, and settlers of Silesian origin from the southwest. [12] [13] However, in most cases, the parents or grandparents of these regions had already left to settle in Poland, so most of showing the origins of German settlers in the settlers already had experience with land clearing. [14] [15]

The immigration of German farmers was prompted by the local nobility, who wanted to increase their income through the rent paid by the settlers. Typically, the landowner commissioned a recruiter who traveled to the German settlement areas further west, recruiting settlers and bringing them to the settlement area. In return, he usually received the hereditary position of village headman in the newly founded village, which also included rent-free land. The settlers paid a so-called basic fee upon arrival. They received several years of exemption from rent payments for the difficult period of land reclamation and, in return, committed themselves to clearing the land and constructing residential and farm buildings. They also received land from the landowner for a school and a cemetery. [16] As a rule, the settlers lived separately from the native Polish population in their own villages or hamlets. [17]

The settlers, who spoke a Silesian dialect and were called "Hockerlinger" in the local dialect, [15] generally founded scattered settlements, preferring damp, cold-soiled areas. Their villages were also characterized by plank barns or post-and-beam barns built from short planks. The settlers came

mainly from the German villages of the Kalisz region, from the area around Stawiszyn, Grodziec, and Zagórów; others came from the area around Grätz, Santomischel, Koschmin, Rakwitz, Lomnitz, and Pleschen. [14]

The settlers who spoke Pomeranian dialects, also called "Kashubians", [18] [19] [20] founded linear villages characterized by the barn with its distinctive timber frame. They came predominantly from Kuyavia, from the area around Dombie, Babiak, Chodecz, Mogilno, Witkowo, Strelno, as well as from Rogasen, Bromberg, Wongrowitz and Schokken. [14]

When describing the origins of the settlers, it is important to remember that most families had lived in Polish-speaking areas for several generations, and that a certain degree of intermingling and assimilation had already occurred. While this process was slowed by the fact that the Poles were almost exclusively Catholic and serfs, resulting in few mixed marriages, the emancipation of the peasantry progressed rapidly, particularly in the western regions from which the settlers of central Poland came, thus facilitating intermingling. In some cases, usually under pressure from the respective landowner, [21] [22] [23] the German villages had also been Catholicized, which in turn promoted the Polonization of their inhabitants. [24]

The oldest settlements in this region founded by Germans were Ruda, [12] [25] Bugaj [12] [25] (for both see Ruda-Bugaj ) and Wierzbno [25] (today a district of Aleksandrów Łódzki ), which were founded in 1782 by settlers of Silesian origin and initially collectively bore the name Groß-Bruschitz (Polish: Brużyca Wielka ). [15] In 1784, a few kilometers to the north, Słowik [25] and Pustkowa Góra [12] [25] were founded by settlers of Pomeranian descent. Since the Germans were mostly Protestant

and the Poles Catholic, from 1786 onwards the Protestant pastor from Iłów traveled to the area twice a year to ensure pastoral care. [26] During the times when he was not present, baptisms and the like were recorded in the nearest Catholic church.

The following places were founded by 1793: 1782: Ruda-Bugaj, Wierzbno; 1784: Słowik, Pustkowa Góra; 1785: Chechło; 1788: Chojny, Dąbrowa, Gałkówek; 1789: Swędów; 1790: Janów (north of Nowosolna), Janów (south of Nowosolna), Górki; 1791: Rydzyny, Brużyczka; 1792: Żabieniec, Kały, Mileszki.

## 1793 to 1807: Continuation of the settlement under Prussian rule

In 1793, as part of the Second Partition of Poland, the region around Łódź became part of Prussia, specifically South Prussia. Despite the political upheaval, German settlement in the region progressed very rapidly, [9] so that by 1800 there were already over 50 German villages, which had been established on the initiative of the Polish nobility (→ List of formerly German-settled places in the Łódź area ).

Parallel to the expansion of the settlements, the German school system developed: by 1798 there were already nine German elementary schools in the vicinity of Łódź, namely in Słowik, Groß Brużyca (Brużyca Wielka), Klein Brużyca (Bużyczka Mała), Kały, Dąbrowa-Holendry, Swendow (Swędów),

Glombie (Głąbie), Mileszki and Domrasin-Holendry, two of which were vacant. [27] [16] [28] By 1805, five more schools had been added. [27]

In 1801, with the support of the landowner Raphael von Bratuszewski, the first Protestant parish in the region was founded in Ruda-Bugaj, and its first pastor was Friedrich Georg Tuve. [29]

Private colonization was slowed under Prussian rule because, since most of these settlers came from other Prussian provinces, it offered no benefit in terms of population policy. [30] [31] Instead, state-run colonization began, recruiting

settlers from outside Prussia. The first state colonies were established in the region from 1798 onwards, but large-scale colonization did not begin until 1800. [32] [33] The following year, recruitment offices were set up in southern Germany, and the systematic

recruitment of settlers began. [31] The number of settlers increased rapidly between 1801 and 1803, but recruitment had to be stopped in 1804. [34] [35] [36] Since a large proportion of the immigrants came from Swabia, "Szwaby" became the term for Germans in general. [31] [37] [38]

State colonization was carried out with considerable effort: the establishment of a settler's homestead in South Prussia alone cost 1,000 thalers. [39] [40] The settlers and their sons were exempt from military service, they received 3–6 years of tax exemption (sometimes more), depending on the soil quality, clearing payments of 600 Polish guilders for a Magdeburg hide, travel assistance of 15 groschen per mile per family member, as well as farm equipment, livestock, and in some cases even interest-free loans. Furthermore, a house, stable, and barn were built at state expense. [41] This support was probably necessary, because the South Germans, inexperienced in clearing land, depended on the assistance of the local Germans to cultivate their land, whom they could pay for this work thanks to the clearing payments. [42] [43]

Although the recruitment of settlers was still underway, increased emigration and onward migration to Russia occurred as early as 1803 and 1804. [44] The Prussian state tried by all means to prevent the colonists from leaving; the border guards attempted to intercept the fleeing settlers, and those caught faced punishment of running the great rod six times. [45] Nevertheless, many managed to escape across the border into Russia. The destination of this migration was apparently mostly the region of Odessa, between the Dnieper and Dniester rivers. [46]

Prussian colonies in the Łódź region were [41]: Masovian Voivodeship: Amt Laznow: Grömbach (Łaznowska Wola) 1800, [12] Grünberg ( Zielona Góra) 1803, [47] [12] Wilhelmswalde (Borowa) 1800, [12] Friedrichshagen (Augustów) 1803, [12] Neusulzfeld (Nowosolna) 1801, [12] Neuwionczyn (Wiączyń Nowy) around 1800, [25] Oberwionczyn (Wiączyń Górny) 1803, [12] Unterwionczyn (Wiączyń Dolny) 1803 [12]; Tkaczew Office: Friedrichsruhe (Aleksandria), Kleingórne (Małogórne) 1798, Neuwürttemberg (Tkaczewska Góra) around 1800 [25]; Zdiechow office: Schöneich (Stare

Krasnodęby) 1802 [12] and Engelhardt (Aniołów); Kalisch Voivodeship, Pabianice Office: Königsbach (Bukowiec) 1803, [12] Effingshausen (Starowa Góra) 1800 [12] and Hochweiler (Markówka) around 1800. [25]

## 1807 to 1815: Migration to Bessarabia

After Prussia's defeat by Napoleon, the region became part of the newly formed Duchy of Warsaw, a satellite state of France, through the Peace of Tilsit in 1807.

Further turbulent times followed, first the War of the Fifth Coalition in 1809 and then Napoleon's Russian campaign of 1812, initially with troop movements and requisitions, followed by the returning disbanded troops and the subsequent Russian occupation. The first Cossacks appeared in the Łódź area in March 1813. [48]

All these events brought great hardship to the people in the region. South German immigrants were particularly affected, as the privileges granted to them had been steadily reduced since 1807, and when their tax-free years expired, they often had not yet had the opportunity to establish their businesses. Many requested additional tax-free years. [49]

When the Russians arrived in 1813, many German settlers were in desperate circumstances, so the Russian state's recruitment campaign for emigration to Bessarabia fell on fertile ground. [50] Particularly in 1814 and 1815, many families left, so that some villages lost a large part of their population. [51] The Łaznów district [48] and the state colony of Grömbach (Łaznowska Wola) within it were especially hard hit, probably because Bernhard Boneth and Martin Voßler, the leaders of an emigration group of 138 families in 1814, came from there. [52] Most colonists sold their settlements, but many also simply left because they were in debt or because no one wanted to take their properties. [48] [52]

For the recruiters, the whole thing was evidently a lucrative business, which led some to resort to criminal methods: Records from Brużyczka state that the inhabitants were to be forced to emigrate at gunpoint. However, sixteen of the twenty-four families managed to escape and later legally reclaim their ownership of the settlements they claimed to have sold. [53] [54] Similar accounts exist from Krzywiec [55] [56] and from Zamłynie, Annefeld (Annopole), and Zborowskie near Zduńska Wola. [57] [55] However, it is also conceivable (though not in the cases mentioned above) that some

colonists feigned violence, because landowners often reserved the right to forcibly recapture settlers who left without permission. [55]

The migration from central Poland to Bessarabia continued until 1840. [43]

The following villages were almost completely depopulated by migration to Bessarabia: Alt-Schöneich (Stare Krasnodęby), Dąbrówka Górna, Friedrichsruhe (Aleksandria), Engelhardt (Aniołów), Grömbach (Łaznowska Wola), [41] Effingshausen (Starowa Góra) [51] and Kiełbasa. [52] The properties mostly passed into the possession of settlers of Pomeranian origin from the Dombie (Dąbie) area. [41]

## 1815 to 1863: Continued immigration and founding of cities

From 1815 onwards, the Łódź region belonged to Congress Poland, formed at the Congress of Vienna and under the rule of the Russian Tsar. The new borders transformed the area from a largely overlooked hinterland of Prussia into Russia's western outpost. Congress Poland offered prospective cloth makers and weavers the advantage of being protected from the already industrializing economies of the western countries by a customs border, while also opening up the vast market of the Russian Empire. [58] [59] In addition, there were the concessions offered to immigrants by both aristocratic landowners and the state. While rural colonization continued house of the German weavers unabated, the immigration of urban artisans also began, and new towns and even entire city districts were founded for the German immigrants in rapid succession – with varying degrees of success.

The following towns were founded in the Łódź area during this period as a result of the immigration of German cloth makers and weavers: Ozorków (1813, town charter 1816), [60] Aleksandrów Łódzki (1817, town charter 1822), [60] Zduńska Wola (1817/18, town charter 1825), [60] Konstantynów Łódzki (1821), [60] Tomaszów Mazowiecki. [60] The following already existing towns were also settlement sites for German immigrants: Pabianice (since 1796), [60] Brzeziny (since the beginning of the 19th century, 1816 founding of the cloth-making suburb of Lasocin), [61] Zgierz (since 1818) [62] [63] and Łódź (since 1821). [63]

The founding of the towns was followed by the establishment of the Protestant church system. Mostly with the support of the landowners, the following parishes were founded: 1826: Łódź St. Trinitatis, Konstantynów Łódzki, Ozorków; [64] 1827: Pabianice; 1829: Brzeziny, Zduńska Wola; 1830: Tomaszów Mazowiecki; 1838: Nowosolna; 1839: Huta Bardzyńska branch, [65] Poddębice branch. [66]

In the years after 1815, there was also an emigration of newly settled families from South Prussia to Prussia, as some of the families who had immigrated directly from the German Empire could not imagine living under foreign rule. [67] The Łódź area is not documented in this context, [68] although it is possible that there were return emigrants, especially if they did not expect to be allocated land by the Prussian state again.

During the November Uprising of 1830/31, the vast majority of Germans remained passive, harboring reservations about the Polish nobility and the Polish clergy, who were intolerant of Protestants. This attitude was shared by Pastors Herrmann of Brzeziny and Metzner of Łódź, in stark contrast to the majority of Protestant pastors, who supported the uprising. [69] Among the artisans in the towns, opinions were divided. The clothmakers of Zduńska Wola, Konstantynów Łódzki, and Zgierz later received a reward from the Russian government for their actions against the Polish insurgents. [70] The clothmakers in Łódź and Aleksandrów Łódzki, however, formed units with the aim of supporting the insurgents. Since they were foreigners, at least the unit from Aleksandrów Łódzki was sent back to the city to protect the population there from attacks. [71] Citizen militias were also formed in Ozorków, Konstantynów Łódzki, Łódź, Pabianice and Zduńska Wola, which took over the protection of the towns and the surrounding German villages. [72]

During the uprising, hunger and disease decimated the population, and although there were only isolated attacks on the German population, [73] [74] numerous families emigrated to Volhynia and Podolia. [75] Furthermore, the economic stagnation forced many cloth makers to leave the region. Most migrated on to Belarus and Volhynia, where they settled in places such as Białystok, [76] but many also went to Bohemia or returned to Germany. [77] [78] Despite all this, immigration exceeded losses, and the number of Germans in the region continued to rise. [74]

In the newly founded towns around Łódź, the Germans were often dominant, especially since German craftsmen played a key role in the region's industrialization. This particular situation led to an increasing concentration of Polish Germans in the rapidly growing city of Łódź and the surrounding towns. Because Polish farmers were not yet free to migrate, the growing labor demand of industry was primarily met by the non-inheriting children of German farmers, who migrated from the wider surrounding area. [79]

Between 1834 and 1838, settlers were recruited directly from Germany once again, and this time, unlike before, old Polish villages with good clay soil were opened for settlement. [80] Families from Baden were settled in Srebrna and Mikołajewice, whereas in the following places, a total of about 500 families from various Hessian territories were settled: Bechcice, Łobudzice, Babice, Wola Czarnyska, and Przyrownica; in addition, about 170 families of urban settlers were settled, mainly in Konstantynów Łódzki and Łódź. [81] Other villages settled by Hessians were Kurów and Kurówek south of Łask. [82]

In 1845, a four-grade, bilingual "German-Russian Realschule" was founded in Łódź. [83]

Immigration from Hesse ended in 1839, [84] and in the 1840s immigration from the Posen area and other more westerly Polish territories also came to a halt, as America became an increasingly popular destination for emigrants. [85] Since Poles were more frequently settled in the colonies from this time onward than in previous decades, mixed settlements became more common. [86] [87]

With the absence of new settlers from the western regions, the Polish Germans were cut off from new ideas and developments, lived in increasing isolation, and fell into backwardness. [88] [89] In Germany, the Polish Germans were forgotten in the second half of the 19th century. [89]

## 1863 to 1914: Emigration to Volhynia and industrialization

The January Uprising of 1863/64, which was ultimately crushed by the Russians because the still-unfree Polish peasants remained passive, was the turning point in the history of the Polish Germans. While the previous history of this ethnic group had emphasized that their settlement contributed to the development of the country and enabled its industrialization, it now became clear that the advantages granted to the Germans had generated resentment, hatred, and envy among the Polish middle class. [90] However, the vast majority of the German population opposed the uprising, as they considered themselves citizens of the Russian state. The consistory of the Evangelical-Augsburg Church and the vast majority of its pastors also sided with the Tsar. [91]

However, there were also Germans on the Polish side. In the Łódź area, Pastors Karl Gustav Manitius (Łódź, St. Trinitatis Parish, who had to leave the city in 1865) and Eduard Boerner (Zduńska Wola) supported the insurgents. Pastor Biederman in Pabianice is also said to have sympathized with the Polish side. [91] In Tomaszów, a detachment of 60 Germans formed and supported the uprising in battle. [92] When the so-called "Battle of Dobra " took place northeast of Łódź around Shrove Tuesday in

1864, according to oral tradition, there were also some Germans among the insurgents. [93] However, after the battle was lost for the Poles, the German farmers of Neusulzfeld (Nowosolna) handed over a fleeing rebel leader to the Russian authorities [92] (according to another account, it was the Jewish innkeeper of the town). [94]

In this situation, the Germans found themselves caught in the crossfire, and numerous atrocities occurred. In the Evangelical parish of Brzeziny alone, which had 4,246 members in 1866, [95] 14 members lost their lives in the uprising. [96] Deaths are also recorded in the parish of Aleksandrów, [92] and in Świniokierz, the farmer Gottfried Scheffler was hanged by the insurgents under Majewski. [97]

The hostilities experienced by the Polish Germans during the 1863/64 uprising led to a large wave of emigration from all over central Poland to the Lublin region, [98] the Cholm region, Volhynia, and the interior of Russia. [92] Immediately after the uprising, well over 1,000 Germans left the Łódź area. [86] From the parish of Konstantynów alone, over 100 families left. [92] Especially on the fringes of

the Łódź language island, particularly near Ozorków and Brzeziny, numerous colonies were abandoned by their German inhabitants. [99] Emigration to Volhynia was particularly strong in 1865 and 1866, after which it decreased somewhat. However, it continued until about 1892 and only ended after the purchase of land by foreign colonists was prohibited in 1887. [100] Emigration to America also gained importance during this period, especially between 1869 and 1891 and 1910 and 1913, [101] although it played only a minor role in the Łódź area. [102] From the turn of the 20th century, the emigration of families from Birkenfeld (Brzozów), [103] Jankow (now Jankowice ) and Erdmannsweiler (Kochanów) [104] to America is known.

The following villages lost their German inhabitants in whole or in part due to migration to Volhynia, to the cities or overseas: Woźniki, Małczew-Holland or Adamów, Stypin (unidentified), Aniołów, Modlica -Holl., Karkoszki, Sarnie Budy (unidentified, possibly Sanie ), Przyłęk, Karasica, Felicjanów, Erazmów, Głąbie, Laski, Plichtów, Gozdów, [41] Boginia, Janinów, Neu-Skoszewy, [105] Marianów, [106] Janów (north of Nowosolna) and surroundings. [107]

Following the suppression of the uprising, the emancipation of the peasantry was carried out in Congress Poland in 1864. As a result, on the one hand, more and more Polish peasants appeared as free settlers, so that land soon became scarce and expensive. [87] On the other hand, landless Poles also flocked to the cities, where mechanization had progressed to the point where fewer and fewer skilled workers were needed, while at the same time more and more unskilled laborers were required. Germans were now mostly employed as skilled workers supervising production, while the vast majority of the workers were Polish. However, many landless Germans also came to the city seeking employment. [108] [109] The enormous growth of the city of Łódź was further accelerated by the construction of the railway, which opened up the huge Russian market. [110]

The Lodz newspaper (initially under the title Lodz Advertiser ) was founded in 1863 as the city's first newspaper. It was initially published in German and Polish (from 1881 onwards only in German). [111]

The expansion of the school system progressed in the following years, but at the same time, the Russification of the schools began. While the teachers of the cantor's schools had previously generally lacked formal training and mostly pursued a trade as a secondary occupation, [112] formal teacher training now became possible for the first time: In 1866, the Pedagogical Courses were established in Warsaw, offering a three-year training program for Protestant elementary school teachers in central Poland. However, in 1871, the language of instruction there became Russian; only religion and German continued to be taught in German. In 1873, the Pedagogical Courses were incorporated into the Warsaw Protestant Teachers ' Seminary. [113]

Also in 1866, a German Realschule (secondary school) was founded in Łódź, but by 1871 the language of instruction had already become Russian. In 1880, the school was transformed into a trade school with Russian as the language of instruction; German was now only a minor subject. [114]

The “German-Russian Realschule”, which had existed in Łódź since 1845, was closed in 1869 at the behest of the authorities. [83] There were also the German private schools of Ringer

(closed in 1890) and the Rothert girls' gymnasium, founded in 1878. [115] [114]

From 1871, teachers at cantor schools were required to take a Russian language exam, and the following year compulsory Russian lessons were introduced. Pastors were encouraged to assess children's Russian language skills during their school visits. Also beginning in 1870, numerous church-run cantor schools were converted into state-run elementary schools. This measure was generally welcomed by the teachers, as they now received significantly better salaries from the state. However, in many cases, the land belonging to the schools was taken from the churches and placed under state school administration, so that many schools later lost their Protestant, German character. [116] The already well-developed church system was supplemented in 1873 by the founding of the branch church of Kamocin and in 1884 by the parish of St. John in Łódź. [117]

With the growing prosperity in the city of Łódź, a summer resort industry developed, drawing wealthy city dwellers to the surrounding villages. The increasing number of summer visitors also played a role in the decision made in 1912 in Andrzejów to build a church to replace the prayer house that had become too small. [118] Königsbach (Bukowiec) also had summer visitors before the First World War. [119]

When the Russian Revolution spread to Łódź in 1905 and barricade fighting broke out in the city in June of that year, [120] Germans were involved in the conflict on all sides. [121] Many Germans were among the dead. [122] The unrest continued until 1907, involving both a struggle for Polish independence and demands from the working class for better living and working conditions. [123] Initially, the various population groups in Łódź showed solidarity with each other within their respective camps. [123] [124] Later, however, the different groups fought each other – probably also encouraged by the Russian authorities. [125] [126] [127] After the suppression of the uprising, the climate between the Germans, Jews and Poles in Łódź deteriorated significantly and permanently. [126]

However, triggered by the unrest of 1905 to 1907, the government had to make numerous concessions. The forced Russification policy had to be halted, and from 1907 onward, the German language was again permitted for instruction in German elementary schools. The latter, however, remained almost ineffective, as Russian authorities hindered the introduction of German due to a planned universal compulsory education. Furthermore, a mixing of German and Polish pupils had already occurred in the city schools, which also created resistance to the introduction of German. In Łódź, attempts were made to counteract these problems by establishing new German-language cantor schools. [128]

The year 1906 is considered the founding year of the Lodz German Gymnasium, [129] which was initially headed by Braun. In 1908 it was taken over by the German Gymnasium and Realschule Association, which had been founded the previous year. In 1910 it moved into a newly constructed school building. [115]

In 1911, the Protestant teachers' seminary was moved from Warsaw to Łódź. [130]

As early as the end of the 19th century, Russian military officials suspected the German settlers of being “German outposts and spies.” This view spread increasingly in Polish publications before the First World War and soon encompassed wider segments of the population. [131] Thus, the hostility towards Germans that arose with the uprising of 1863/64 continued into the 20th century.

## 1914 to 1918: Battle of Łódź and German occupation

After Germany declared war on Russia on August 1, 1914, many Germans who had by then acquired Russian citizenship were drafted into the Russian army. [132] They mostly served on the Caucasus Front. [133] Those who had not renounced their German citizenship were deported, [132] [134] for example, the ethnic German leader Ludwig Wolff the Elder from Pabianice and his family, who were taken to Russia. [135] The family of the teacher and later school principal and settlement researcher Albert Breyer also had to leave the region and spent several years in Saint Petersburg. [136] Deportations of entire village communities (a total of about 150,000 Germans), such as those carried out in the more easterly regions of Poland from the summer of 1915 onwards, [133] did not occur in the Łódź area, as the region was already occupied by Germany at that time. [137] [138] [133]

The First World War brought profound changes for the Germans of Łódź. Within just a few months, the region became a theater of war: from November 11th to December 5th, 1914, the Battle of Łódź claimed numerous lives, including among the region's German population. [139] [140] [141] There was also significant war damage in many places, especially in Aleksandrów, [139] Konstantynów, [142] [138] Neusulzfeld (Nowosolna), [140] Andrzejów, [118] Königsbach (Bukowiec), [143] Boginia,

Skoszewy, Janinów, Głogowiec, Teolin, Gałkówek, Grünberg ( Zielona Góra), Grömbach (Łaznowska Wola), Wilhelmswalde (Borowa), Albertów, Przylęk Mały and Brzeziny. [141]

The war also led to the rediscovery of the Polish Germans, who had been forgotten within the German Reich. Time and again, German soldiers encountered civilians in the heart of Poland who spoke an archaic form of Low German or Swabian. This revived cultural exchange with their country of origin.

[89]

Until 1918, the region remained under German occupation, officially as the Regency Kingdom of Poland from 1916 onwards. For the population, this meant a time of great hardship, as the Łódź textile industry was cut off from its markets in Russia and the Far East. The city of Łódź, the Evangelical Church, and other organizations established public kitchens and a children's home; children, and sometimes entire families, were relocated to the better-provided surrounding areas. [142] Some emigrated to Germany as laborers needed due to the war effort. [137] Between 1915 and

1918, approximately 70,000 Polish Germans, many of them from the Łódź region, emigrated to Germany; barely half returned later. [142]

During the German occupation, the German school system developed very positively. After the Russians withdrew, some teachers independently reintroduced German as the language of instruction. In September 1915, the German civil administration mandated instruction in German for German children. In Łódź, the number of German schools increased from 23 German-Russian schools with 94 classes in 1913/14 to 44 schools with 186 classes. [144] In the spring of 1917, four schools with 14 classes for German Catholic children were also established. The Łódź German Realschule and the Teachers' Seminary, which had been closed since the beginning of the war, were soon reopened. [145] In 1916, the Luisenlyzeum and the higher boys' school founded by Karl Weigelt were also established; the following year, a girls' school was added. Furthermore, the German school system in Łódź experienced a considerable upswing during the German occupation. [146] Secondary German- language schools were also founded in other cities, such as the Gymnasium in Pabianice in 1916 [147] and the Realgymnasium in Zgierz in 1917. [148]

Since the Łódź region was cut off from its markets in Russia and the Far East, the number of Germans has steadily declined. [149]

## 1918 to 1939: The German minority under pressure

In the interwar period, the German minority in Poland, with the establishment of the Second Polish Republic, transformed from a formerly favored group of immigrants into a minority viewed with suspicion. Alongside the descendants of German colonists, numerous Germans who did not share this history now lived on Polish territory due to Germany's territorial losses after the First World War. Constant tensions arose between the Weimar Republic and Poland over border issues. Propaganda on both sides exploited the Polish Germans, exaggerating their role to an almost gigantic degree. [150] The situation between the two states only eased for a few years after the signing of the ten-year German-Polish Non-Aggression Pact on January 26, 1934.

In the 1930s, the economic situation of the Germans in Łódź was poor due to the Great Depression. In the villages, the non-inheriting descendants could no longer migrate to the cities and earn their living in trade and industry, as they had previously done. As a result, the previously common system of primogeniture with minority rights [151] was often abandoned in favor of a system of partible inheritance, leading to smaller farm sizes and declining prosperity. [51] [152]

Nevertheless, summer tourism was a significant source of income for numerous German villages in the 1930s, especially in the east and west of Łódź, [153] such as Grünberg (Zielona Góra), Königsbach (Bukowiec), Justynów, Rossanow (Rosanów), Żakowice, Galkówek, [154] Kirschberg (Wiśniowa Góra), [155] Kraszew and Hulanka. [156] It was mostly Germans from the city who stayed in the German villages, thus also promoting cultural exchange. [154]

Since around 1925, emigration of Protestants from Poland to Canada has been increasing, [101] examples from the Łódź area are Erdmannsweiler (Kochanów) [157] and Wilhelmswalde (Borowa). [158] Families from Birkenfeld (Brzozów) also emigrated to the United States and Canada after the

First World War. [103]

In the cultural sphere, the dominant theme of the interwar period was the Polish state's restrictive stance toward the German school system, which effectively meant its demise. Although a decree by the Council of Ministers on March 3, 1919, stipulated that German should remain the language of instruction "as soon as this is requested by the majority of parents or their representatives of the children attending the school in question," this and subsequent regulations were interpreted by the authorities in such a way that maintaining German was generally unenforceable. Furthermore, parents were pressured through intimidation and harassment to prevent them from declaring their support for German as the language of instruction. In Königsbach, for example, parents were misled about the true purpose of the vote: they were asked whether they wished their children to now also learn Polish properly and were then asked to vote. The fact that this vote concerned the introduction of Polish as the language of instruction for all subjects was not made clear. Even in the schoolyard, children were now required to speak Polish. [159] Thus, the number of German elementary schools fell from 564 to 294 schools with German, 26 with mixed language instruction, and 53 with Polish, simply as a result of the implementation of the Council of Ministers' decree. Due to numerous other measures, the number of German schools continued to decline rapidly in the following years, so that by 1925 there were only about 225 elementary schools where German was taught for at least two to four hours a week. Of these, however, only 50 to 60 were schools where German was actually the language of instruction. [160] From 1932 onward, teachers at private German schools required a certificate of loyalty from the respective starostei (district), which could be refused without any specific reason. As a result, many schools lost their teachers and had to close. [161] In the 1935/36 school year, there were only eleven schools with German as the language of instruction. [162] In the 1938/39 school year, over 20,000 German children in Poland received no instruction in their mother tongue, and about 14,000 received only one to five hours per week. [162] Some schools also had to close due to economic difficulties, such as the Realschule in Zgierz in 1928. [163]

To replace the German schools, a secret German school committee was formed in Łódź, comprising the pastors Gustav Schedler and Eduard Kneifel, the Sejm deputy August Utta, and Ludwig Wolff the Younger. Its aim was to educate German children in the villages with the help of itinerant teachers. [164] Most of these itinerant teachers were German teachers dismissed by the government. They were

frequently denounced, and quite a few had to answer for their actions in court. [165]

As part of a school reform, all teacher training colleges in Poland were closed, since teacher training was to take place in the future at pedagogical lyceums or at a pedagogical college. Thus, the German teacher training college in Łódź was no longer allowed to admit teacher candidates from 1932

onwards, and it was finally closed in 1936. No new opportunity for the training of teachers for German-language schools was created. [166] [167]

However, there were also positive developments during this period. For the first time, a larger educated class emerged among the Germans. While in the past this class consisted almost exclusively of pastors, now graduates of secondary schools, especially the German Gymnasium in Lodz and the Protestant Teachers' Seminary, were increasingly included. Employees of the press and theater, as well as members of parliament, also belonged to this group. [129]

In the area of ​language, strong trends toward standardization were evident, which were particularly advanced in the later-established settlements east of Łódź. [51] In the villages, the various German dialects were replaced by so - called " Łódź German, " which was a variant of Central German strongly influenced by Lower Silesian, [168] but in some cases probably also directly by a rural High German. [171] This development was different dialect groups. [51] Swabian survived longest in the villages of Königsbach (Bukowiec), [51] [173] Grünberg ( Zielona Góra ) and Neusulzfeld (Nowosolna). [174] In Łódź itself, the "Łódź

German" was increasingly replaced by High German. [175] Increasing Polonization, especially in the cities, was also noticeable. In many families, Polish was already the everyday language, [176] [177] a trend that was further encouraged by the closure of German schools. The number of mixed marriages with Poles also increased significantly in the 20th century. [178] [38]

In the Evangelical-Augsburg Church, the spread of the Polish language among the faithful led to tensions. Julius Bursche, appointed General Superintendent of the Evangelical-Augsburg Church by the Russian authorities in 1904 [179], considered adherence to the German language and culture an anachronism [180] and therefore massively promoted Polonization within his church during his term of office (1904–1939). In doing so, he encountered resistance from the German majority among the faithful and pastors. [181] The conflict intensified from 1918 to 1939 to such an extent that the Evangelical-Augsburg Church was ultimately on the verge of collapse. [182] [183] [184]

At a time when many Protestant congregations celebrated their centenary, numerous new parishes were created by separating from the existing congregations: 1924: Ruda Pabianicka branch, 1928: Grömbach (Łaznowska Wola), 1929: Łódź St. Matthew, 1932: Łódź-Radogoszcz, 1936: Andrzejów (branch since 1925), Bałuty - Żubardź, Königsbach (Bukowiec) branch, Grabieniec branch, [117] Poddębice (branch since 1838). [66]

Shortly after the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, events in the German Reich had a significant impact on the coexistence of the various population groups in Łódź. Some Germans were influenced by Nazi propaganda, which was also reflected in the press. [185] The Young German Party in Poland, founded in Bielsko-Biała in 1921 as the "German National Socialist Association for Poland", was also active in Łódź from 1934 onwards. [186] Local groups were founded in Zgierz, Pabianice and Konstantynów in 1935 [187] and in Tomaszów Mazowiecki in 1936. [186] The German People's Association in Poland, founded on June 1, 1924, which initially focused primarily on "struggle to preserve the German character of the Evangelical-Augsburg Church," increasingly adopted the line of Nazi ideology after Hitler's rise to power, although its Christian orientation was maintained. [188] In

addition, there was also a local branch of the NSDAP's foreign organization in Łódź, [189] but information about its activities is currently lacking. However, only Germans, i.e., holders of the citizenship of the German Reich, were permitted to belong to it. [190]

The protest of the Jews of Łódź against the Nazis' treatment of Jews in the German Reich met with little understanding from the Germans of Łódź. The boycott of goods from Nazi Germany, called for by Jewish associations and joined by many Polish companies, was not directed against the local Germans, [191] but probably still had a negative impact on many businesses owned by Germans in Łódź. For example, the absence of Jewish summer visitors to Kirschberg (Wiśniowa Góra) from 1933 onwards [155] may be related to the boycott. A break in the relationship between the Jews of Łódź and the Germans, who until then had always represented their political interests jointly, occurred after April 9, 1933. A week after the boycott of Jewish businesses in Germany, demonstrations in Łódź against Nazi Germany led to riots against German institutions, resulting in considerable property damage. From this point on, cooperation between German and Jewish organizations ended. [192] The situation likely calmed down somewhat from 1934 onward, in line with general political developments.

In 1938, tensions between Poland and Germany escalated again. In March 1939, Germany invaded the so-called rump Czechia and the Memel Territory, and in April 1939, Germany abrogated the German- Polish Non-Aggression Pact. The rising resentment erupted in many places in attacks against the German minority. For example, in May 1939, serious riots against Germans occurred in the Łódź area, specifically in Tomaszów and the surrounding villages (especially Ludwików, Kumrów (probably Komorów ), and Jakubów ), Konstantynów, Pabianice, and Łódź, during which the police did not intervene. [193] Two people were killed in Kumrów. A general feeling of insecurity spread among the Germans in the region. [194] [195] [196] In addition, there were mass layoffs of Germans from factories and businesses, with most having no prospect of alternative employment. [197] [198] As a result, many Germans saw no other option than to leave their homeland and go to Germany, where reception camps were set up for those fleeing from Poland. [197] Before the start of the war, 70,000 Germans from Poland were living in such camps; the number of those who found accommodation outside the camps is unknown. [199] Many who failed to escape were imprisoned. [197] In this situation of rising tensions, the government compiled lists of leading German figures who were to be arrested in the event of war. [200] [201] Individual people, including some from the Łódź area, were arrested as early as mid-August 1939. [197] [201]

The Hitler-Stalin Pact was signed on August 23, 1939. General mobilization of Poland followed on August 29, 1939. Many ethnic Germans from Poland were also drafted into the military, as they mostly held Polish citizenship. However, in many barracks, the Germans were immediately separated and deported eastward under military guard, without weapons or uniforms. [202]

## 1939 to 1945: In the “Model Gau Wartheland”

The invasion of Poland began on 1 September 1939, when German troops invaded Poland without a prior declaration of war. It ended on 6 October 1939 with the surrender of the last field troops. [203]

In the first days of the war, according to the prepared lists, many Germans were arrested and marched towards Bereza Kartuska. [201] [200] Above all, members of ethnic German organizations and associations, teachers, pastors, publishing directors, and editors were affected by the arrests. [201] But there were also many arrests in more rural areas; for example, around 100 people are said to have been affected in Neusulzfeld (Nowosolna) and the surrounding villages. [204]

The Polish army was unable to halt the German attack and was quickly pushed back towards Warsaw. Behind them, a

small number of ethnic Germans recruited by the Nazi regime carried out acts of sabotage, which would contribute to the myth of the ethnic Germans as Hitler's fifth column. During these attacks, railway stations in Łódź were destroyed on September 2, 1939. [205] From September 5 onwards, retreating Polish army units passed through Łódź. [206] At the same time, many Polish civilians were fleeing, causing congestion on all streets and roads. [200] The situation was extremely dangerous for the ethnic Germans serving in the Polish army, as their comrades and officers frequently suspected them of espionage. Many of them who failed to pass themselves off as Poles were shot. [207] In addition, there were the regular casualties, among them the school principal Albert Breyer, who had made a name for himself in researching the German settlement in Poland. [136] [208]

Łódź was occupied on September 9, 1939. This marked the beginning of the German occupation of Poland, which would last for over five years. The country was divided into the Reichsgau Wartheland, which was to become part of the German Reich, and the General Government. Łódź was not originally part of the Reichsgau Wartheland, but was incorporated into it on November 9, 1939. The border with the General Government now ran southeast of the city, meaning that some of the Germans in the Łódź area lived outside the Wartheland. The occupation policies of the Third Reich aimed at the oppression and disenfranchisement of Poles and the extermination of Jews. For the Germans from Poland, there was little room for neutrality.

On November 25, 1939, German citizenship was granted in principle to all so-called ethnic Germans in the incorporated territories. However, the decree concerning the four categories in Lodz, 1939 of the German ethnic list was not published until March 4, 1941. The first category was to include active Germans and

members of national organizations, the second passive Germans, the third mixed-race individuals, and the fourth unreliable Germans. All holders of the ethnic list were required to prove their Aryan ancestry. However, applying for this status was not optional for ethnic Germans; it was mandatory. Those who failed to comply were to be imprisoned in concentration camps, which in most cases occurred. [209] However, the German ethnic list was not granted to everyone who applied. [210] The classification of ethnic Germans as Germans who first had to prove themselves, as well as the arrogance of many Reich Germans, led to the latter often being pejoratively referred to as "Reich Germans." [211] [212]

After the expropriation of the Poles and Jews [213] [214], it was easy for many Germans, both Reich and ethnic Germans, to enrich themselves and thus become complicit in the regime. [215] [216] [217] On the other hand, if one was willing to support Polish neighbors or acquaintances, one could expect severe punishments. [217] The disenfranchisement of the Polish population led in many cases to attacks on Poles, even by local Germans, [218] whereby, for example, even the killing of a Pole in Justynów had no consequences for the perpetrators at that time. [219]

Under the slogans "Heim ins Reich" (Home to the Reich) and "Lebensraum im Osten" (Living Space in the East), over one million ethnic Germans from Volhynia, the so-called Narew region, the Cholm and Lublin regions, the Baltic states, Bessarabia, Bukovina (Buchenland), Russia ( Black Sea Germans ), and other areas were resettled on expropriated Polish farms in the Reichsgau Wartheland during the war years. [220] Before this could happen, however, many of the resettled families lived for extended periods in camps. One of these was a reception camp in Kirschberg (Wiśniowa Góra) with more than 100 barrack-like wooden houses. [221] The new beginning was not easy for the resettled families: It is recorded that in Wilhelmswalde (Borowa), the resettled people were met with hostility because they resented the fact that their Polish neighbors had been expelled for their sake. [222]

Germans from Łódź appear on both the side of the Nazi perpetrators and the side of the victims during the German occupation. Among those who identified with the Nazi regime were the following ethnic Germans from the Łódź area, who, presumably because of their role in preparing for the war, were promoted to high SS ranks effective November 13, 1939: Ludwig Wolff Obersturmbannführer (Lieutenant Colonel ), Eugen Nippe as Hauptsturmführer (Captain), and Heinrich Boltz as Obersturmführer (Senior Stormtrooper ). Ludwig Wolff soon became the first district leader of the Nazi Party in Łódź, and Eugen Nippe became leader of the self-defense force. [223] In Tomaszów, Georg Boettig became known as the greatest tormentor of the Jewish and Polish

population from among the local population. [224]

However, if native Germans refused to integrate into the Nazi system, they could quickly become victims. Several examples of this are known: The Völker couple from Wilhelmswalde (Borowo), who refused to accept the German Volksliste (people's list), were arrested and murdered in prison. The lawyer Richard Vogel from Łódź, his wife, and his two daughters, who refused to accept the German Volksliste, were all interned in Auschwitz concentration camp. [225] The Felker couple from Königsbach, Bible Students, were sent to a concentration camp because he refused military service and died there. [226] [227]

## After 1945: Loss of homeland

Following the Red Army 's entry into Łódź, with all its immediate consequences, all German men between the ages of 17 and 50 were arrested and subsequently deported to forced labor in the interior of the Soviet Union, beginning in February 1945. This also affected many Polish men who were classified as "capable of Germanization" in category 3 of the Volksliste (People's List), simply because they were considered German. [228]

Unlike the Germans in regions that had previously belonged to the German Reich (especially East Prussia, Western Pomerania, and Silesia), the Germans who had lived as a minority in Poland before 1939 were not expelled but interned and forced to perform hard labor. [229] All persons who had declared themselves to be of German nationality during the occupation were deprived of all public and civil rights, and their property was confiscated. [230] Germans received neither złoty nor ration cards. [231] The Germans from the Łódź area were mostly interned in labor camps in Tomaszów, Sikawa near Łódź, and Warsaw. Conditions in the camps were harsh; many died from deprivation and mistreatment. On January 7, 1946, a transport of elderly and sick people left the camp in Sikawa. After a four-day journey, 2,400 people arrived in Brandenburg an der Havel; many had died en route. [232]

It was not until 1950 that the sanctions against the Germans who remained in the country were lifted. [233] In 1951, they were declared Polish citizens. [234] However, a large number of Germans had

found their way to one of the two German states, with or without official permission. In the Federal Republic of Germany, however, until the Federal Expellees Act came into force, many Germans from Poland had difficulties being recognized as German citizens, since they had held Polish citizenship before 1939. [235] The Germans from Łódź were scattered in all directions, and many emigrated overseas in the following years. Only a few families from Königsbach (Bukowiec) settled together in Großgörschen near Leipzig. [236] [237] [238] It is not known how many Germans remained in the Łódź area. However, there must have been a considerable number, because of the 17 parishes with four branches of the Diocese of Łódź of the Evangelical-Augsburg Church in 1939, all but one of which had German as their liturgical language, [239] eight still exist today, namely Aleksandrów Lódzki, Łódź, Ozorków, Pabianice, Poddębice, Tomaszów Mazowiecki, Zduńska Wola and Zgierz. [240]

In West Germany, various organizations looked after the interests of the Germans of Łódź: the Landsmannschaft Weichsel-Warthe (founded May 6, 1949), the Relief Committee of Evangelical Lutheran Germans from Poland, and the "Homeland Association of Germans from the Łódź Industrial Region" (existed from 1964 to 2005). [241] After the political changes in Poland in 1989/90, a German cultural association was founded in the city. [242]
