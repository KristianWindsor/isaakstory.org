# Steppe by Steppe: German-Russian History and Immigration

*A presentation by Sarah Bailey Roberts, hosted by the Fayetteville Public Library.*

> **Source:** YouTube — ["Steppe by Steppe" / Virtual Series: German Russian Genealogy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DWBjxS8vG_w), Fayetteville Public Library (Nov 10, 2021).
>
> A cleaned, readable transcript of the talk, polished and formatted for reference, with garbled proper names and German/Russian vocabulary corrected against historical sources (e.g. *Oranienbaum*, *Saratov*, *Rothammel*, *Volhynia*, *runza*, *Grebbel*, *belina*). Section headings reproduce the video's chapter markers. Wording is otherwise the speaker's own; minor filler and transcription artifacts have been trimmed for clarity.
>
> **Scope for the Isaak Story:** this is the wide-angle overview — who the Germans from Russia were and the full arc from Catherine's manifestos through the 1870s emigration, WWI, the famine, and the Stalin-era deportations. It is Volga-centric, with the Black Sea Germans (the Isaak line's branch) covered as a secondary thread. It contains no Bessarabia- or Kulm-specific detail, so treat it as orienting background rather than a source for the family's particular path; the manifesto privileges, the 1871 loss of those privileges, the Homestead Act pull to the Dakotas, and the later persecution all apply broadly to the Black Sea Germans too.

## Introduction

My name is Sarah Bailey Roberts. I'm really pleased to be here today. First, I want to thank Fayetteville Public Library — especially Amy — for helping me host this series. I'm really looking forward to it. And second, I want to thank you all for being here and learning with me today.

So, a little bit more about myself and how I got into German-Russian history. I just moved here from Lincoln, Nebraska, this year; I'd been living in Lincoln for six years. I got my master's in library and information science while I was there, and my first library job was as the research librarian at the American Historical Society of Germans from Russia. I still work there part-time, working on special projects, working with really cool texts and resources with a really great group of people.

So I've not only worked with a lot of texts — which has been a phenomenal experience — and learned German-Russian history and genealogy that way, but I've also learned it through getting to know the very engaged and thriving community of German-Russian people that still exists today.

My presentation is titled *Steppe by Steppe*, and we're going to be highlighting German-Russian history and immigration. I really think this presentation will serve as a good foundation as we jump into German-Russian genealogy and resources — it'll help us understand what types of records are even available as you see these people move from country to country.

So keep in mind throughout this presentation: focus on how immigration was such an important part of German-Russian history. From one steppe region to another — when they lived in Germany and Russia, when they immigrated to the United States under the Homestead Act, when they went to Canada, Brazil, Argentina — these people really were a people of movement and opportunity.

## Steppe Ecosystem

So this photo you see here is of a steppe ecosystem — S-T-E-P-P-E. What is a steppe ecosystem, if you've never heard of it? Maybe you've been in one before and never known it. It's a dry, high-elevation prairie or grassland. Here in the United States, you can find a steppe ecosystem in eastern Colorado and far western Nebraska. And this kind of ecosystem made up the land where Germans settled in Russia, here in the United States, and in the prairie provinces of Canada.

If you've ever been to the Great Plains — and if you haven't, you should go, because it's lovely — this photo looks like the Great Plains. You have the rolling hills, the short-grass prairie, the big sky. But this photo is not the U.S. or any North American great plain. This is a Russian steppe ecosystem, specifically a village called Rothammel. So we have this German — well, this Russian — steppe here.

## Nebraska

And then we have a Nebraska photo on your right, of a Nebraska steppe region. The German-Russians really found the prairies and rolling hills of North America similar to those of their home in Russia, and I think this really attracted them to the area when they moved here. If you think about it, the agriculture, the economic experiences, and the climate in Russia really prepared them for a successful life when they moved to this area.

So who are the Germans from Russia? I think the name can be really puzzling, especially if you've never heard of them before. To put it very simply: they were Germans who moved to Russia, lived there for around 200 years, and then immigrated out of Russia to North and South America, as well as a few other places. On the screen here, you'll see various German-Russian families — you can see their traditional garb, what the women are doing, the families. These are actual photos from the American Historical Society of Germans from Russia, which has a fabulous photo archive.

## Catherine the Great

So our story really starts with Catherine the Great. She was also Princess Sophia, but we know her as Catherine the Great. She started reigning in 1762 in Russia. She was a German princess. She became Empress of Russia in 1762 by marrying Peter III, who was Peter the Great's grandson. She married him — you probably already know this history — quickly organized a coup, overthrew her husband, and boom, she's in charge of Russia now. It was that easy, right?

So she successfully overthrew him, and under Catherine's reign she really wanted to revitalize Russia. She wanted to grow the economy, and part of the way she wanted to do this was to issue a manifesto inviting migration to Russia — specifically to the lower Volga region. (We'll show you a map a little bit later on.) Her hopes were really to bring progress and industry to this Russian frontier.

## Catherine's Second Manifesto

Her first manifesto, which Catherine wrote in 1762, invited foreigners to come settle in Russia, but it really yielded few results — mostly because it was too general, not really specific about what benefits she was offering.

So in 1763 — take two — Catherine writes this new manifesto, offering more benefits and privileges, again aiming to turn the lower Volga region into productive farmland, not the "wild land" that Russia saw it as. We know there were nomadic cultures living in this land; many of them were displaced as the new settlements came in. Many had lived there since the 1500s — I feel like we need to definitely point out that there were communities there before. Catherine just didn't want them there.

So if you don't read old German script — if you do, you can read this slide to yourself — let me just tell you a little about what the manifesto promised: free land, freedom of religion, freedom from taxation, local self-government, freedom to settle anywhere in Russia, and freedom to practice any trade. You did not have to serve in the Russian military. And most importantly, these benefits and privileges could be passed on to the Germans' descendants — it was going to last *forever*. This is key to remember: Catherine said this could last forever.

And she was extremely successful with this manifesto. She sent Russian representatives out to recruit, and it attracted the attention of not only Germans but also the French, the Swedish, and some others who were interested in coming and settling. In fact, many of those who were attracted to the Volga region were Germans from South Germany and southwest Germany who had already immigrated to Denmark — by invitation of the Danish king, who had created an agricultural reform act. But when they got to Denmark, they realized it was just a big swamp and they didn't actually want to live there. So they immigrated again, to Russia, to this lower Volga region.

## War Impact

So what prompted them to come — other than that some were living in a swamp — and take advantage of the manifesto? Well, the Seven Years' War had just ended, and the Germans really felt a great impact of war on their families, their country, and their communities. Mixed with economic hardships, the promise of the manifesto — especially not having to serve in the Russian military — was a really attractive feature of moving. But it has to be said that each family had their own reason for moving, and we're not going to know all of them; these are just some of the probable factors that helped influence those decisions.

## Large Families

So those who did come over often came with large families. This photo here is actually of a kind of small family — it wasn't uncommon for German-Russians to have ten kids. Many of them were skilled as blacksmiths, shoemakers, doctors, lawyers, clerks, and, definitely, some farmers.

## Trades

But the Germans soon discovered, when they arrived in Russia, that while the manifesto said they could settle anywhere and practice any trade, what Catherine really meant was: you have to settle in the lower Volga region, and you need to be a farmer. So a lot of those trades they'd been practicing didn't apply. You can imagine a doctor suddenly forced into being a farmer, without any experience in agriculture — that was extremely challenging, extremely overwhelming.

## Immigrants

So the first wave of immigrants came between the years 1764 and 1772. It's estimated around 30,000 Germans came to the lower Volga region, and about a hundred of these little German settlements popped up around the lower Volga area.

Just a quick recap of how they got there: it took about nine days — sometimes a little longer — to go through the Baltic Sea to get to St. Petersburg. They stopped at a city called Oranienbaum, where they took an oath of allegiance to the Russian crown and were given some supplies. It's even said that Catherine herself would sometimes come out on the balcony and welcome the Germans in their own native language — because, after all, she was a German princess. These colonists who arrived at this city were recorded; a list was compiled. This is a fabulous resource that we'll talk about next week when we get into what types of records are available for genealogy.

So the lower Volga region — I'm not sure if you can see my mouse, but the lower Volga region is this area here. We have Saratov, a city close to the lower Volga region. These are the routes they would have taken to get there: St. Petersburg around here, the Baltic Sea. Again, these are the points of origin, migration routes, and areas of settlement. We'll talk a little more about the Black Sea region later, but here is the lower Volga region that Catherine initially said, "Hey, I want to settle here — how can we do this?"

## Wagon Train

So they stayed at Oranienbaum for several weeks — sometimes several months — before making the long trip down to the Volga region. And it was long. It was over wagon train, over winter. Picture in your mind: it's very similar to those wagon trains crossing the American West. Catherine did pay Russian families to host some of these German families traveling south — housing them, feeding them — so they had a little help. But you can imagine: they get to Russia thinking they can go anywhere and do anything, but they actually have to take this huge trip down south across a steppe region, across the prairie and plains.

## Volga

So when they arrived along the Volga, I'm sure it was really quite beautiful — you have this arid steppe ecosystem, these grassy slopes toward the Volga River. Here are some photos of the Volga River near Samara. But this is a prairie, and building houses and other structures is difficult because there's no lumber here. So they had to build sod houses, really improvising, figuring out the land, because it was so foreign to them. They had to wait for lumber to be shipped down the Volga River from Saratov or St. Petersburg to begin building their settlements.

And I want to point out: when you think of these German settlements, maybe you picture quaint small towns with a couple of buildings. But these villages grew in population to 40,000 — like Norka and Huck, for instance, in the Volga, which were really large, thriving communities. Here's an example of one of the communities after they'd built it up over time. There was a lot going on. Even though it's kind of out in the middle of nowhere, it really was hustle and bustle. So once they were provided with the tools necessary, they created a thriving region in the Volga. They built barns, houses, stores — and, importantly, they built their churches.

## Religion

Their churches were always centrally located in each of the villages. The German-Russian people were of all faiths, and religion was extremely important to them — not only because they were very devout, but because their settlement locations were determined by their religious affiliation. So you would have all-Catholic villages, all-Mennonite villages, all-Protestant villages. They really were building their communities and tying them together by their faith, by what they believed.

We'll look at more pictures of churches later, but this type of architecture is from their homeland — they replicated a lot of that as they moved to Russia. So you'll see steeples and columns on the churches they built, as we look at photos down the line in the presentation.

## Culture

So the German-Russian people didn't really assimilate to Russian culture. They thrived in their German communities. They practiced their own customs, ate their own food, kind of had their own way of life. Schools and churches were taught in their native German. They maintained these customs throughout the entire time they were living in Russia.

## Agriculture

Soon after they got settled, they started farming — which is what Catherine wanted them to do, right? She wanted them to make progress, make industry, start farming. So they began to farm the Volga region. I think people are always surprised when I say they used camels as livestock. This was an arid ecosystem. When you think of Russia, maybe you think of Siberia — cold and forested — but this was further south, a little more arid. They had camels as livestock, using them to pick fruit from the orchards (sitting on top of them, like this gentleman is doing, going along picking fruit) and as transportation to pull wagons.

## Camels

There are many accounts — primary-source documents — which I find quite funny, saying that the camels were grumpy and mean and spat on you all the time. So they're extremely strong, extremely reliable creatures, but they had a horrible disposition, and this is well documented.

## Wheat

They also farmed wheat, which was one of the primary crops of the German-Russian farms. In this picture you see men, women, and children — they all helped; it was a family affair. The men certainly weren't out in the fields working by themselves. They also farmed rye, grain, millet, and hemp. They truly were making Catherine proud.

## Farms

So in the Volga region specifically, these farms were located outside the settlement wall. The German-Russians would live in their settlements and travel out to their farms during the day, coming back inside at night. This differs from the Black Sea Germans, who lived on their farms — and we'll talk about that a little later.

## Alexander I

So, moving forward in time: Catherine's great-grandson, Alexander I, started reigning in 1801. He further expanded the boundaries of Russia, reissuing the original manifesto his great-grandmother had created and inviting more Germans to come.

It must be said that prior to Alexander there was a continuous influx of Germans coming into this region. You had the Mennonites immigrating from West Prussia; you had Poland giving land to Russia, creating Volhynia. So Germans were continuously coming in. But because of the reissuing of the manifesto, and because of the new territory obtained by Russia around the Black Sea region, there was a greater burst in immigration. Around 3,000 families immigrated to the Black Sea region between the years 1803 and 1810.

## Black Sea Migration

This is the same migration route we saw before, but now we have the Black Sea region here — modern-day Ukraine. We have Odessa here for reference, and all these red dots are areas where the German-Russians settled. You can see they took many different paths to get here. I'll linger on that — over here we have Lübeck, around where Bremen is, just to orient yourself on this map.

So after immigration to the Black Sea, the German immigrants and their families grew and peaked to around two million total Germans living in the Russian Empire, owning almost 24 million acres of land. They came in and started buying land from the Russians very quickly. This, for example, is a Black Sea village called Alexanderhilf. You can maybe see here that the house and barn are under one single roof — again, this differs from the Volga, because the Black Sea houses are actually on the farms themselves. They didn't have to travel outside the city. And similar to the Volga, wheat was a primary crop.

## Turkey Red Wheat

Specifically, a Turkey Red wheat. This was the type of wheat farmed by the Black Sea Mennonites. It was not only important to their life in Russia, but as we see these Germans immigrating to the U.S., this type of wheat was really well suited to grow in Kansas and became a really important part of the Kansas economy. We'll get to that a little later.

## Alexander II

So, unfortunately, the good times did not last long. We have Alexander II here; he took power in 1855. And in 1871, Alexander basically says: Catherine the Great issued this manifesto, it was great while it lasted, but when she said it would last forever, I think what she really meant was it would only last 100 years — so your time's up.

He's in power now, and he rescinds all those privileges and benefits the Germans had. Now they were subject to serving in the military, they had to subscribe to Russian Orthodoxy, and they had to assimilate to Russian culture. So rather than face Russification, the German-Russians really began a mass immigration out of Russia at this point.

## World War I

So immigration was further fueled by the murder of Tsar Nicholas II — I think all of us probably know the story of Anastasia and Rasputin; Anastasia was Nicholas's daughter — which catalyzed the Bolshevik Revolution when the Russian Empire fell, and was further fueled by World War I. There was just a lot of political unrest growing.

It must be said that those German-Russians who did not have the opportunity to exit Russia at this time were banished, exiled, or killed. They were subject to a famine that claimed many lives — up to, I think, a third of the Volga Germans died in a famine that happened in the 1920s. It gained the attention of the U.S., which provided aid in the form of the Volga Relief Society. The Germans were really discriminated against, despite having called Russia home for 200 years, and it was all because they were Germans practicing their very old German customs in the Russian Empire. That was really why.

## Homestead Act

So they're immigrating out, and we have a couple of different areas they're immigrating to. I'll go through them all, starting with the Homestead Act, which was going on at the same time as the immigration out of Russia. When word of the Homestead Act reached Russia, a few men from various villages met and sent delegates to America to scope out the land and see what it was all about. They came back to Russia and said, "Yeah, let's move." This resulted in the first several hundred German-Russians immigrating to the United States during the 1870s.

This is one of my very favorite advertisements — of Kansas, by one of the railroads — saying it's the best in the U.S., basically a tropical prairie-woodland adventure that everyone should go to. So this is the type of advertising they were getting as they tried to determine whether they wanted to go to the Americas to resettle and get out of Russia.

## Canada

In Canada, in 1880, the Volga Germans were settling on the prairie provinces — Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan. Even some German-Russians who had originally gone to the United States then immigrated to Canada, because they heard the U.S. might get involved in World War I. So it's kind of like a double migration.

In Argentina — one of the places a lot of Germans went — the president signed an immigration and colonization act that provided a lot of support to Europeans who wanted to come. A lot of Volga Germans took advantage of this opportunity; like they did in America, they sent delegates to Argentina to scope it out. Primarily Catholic villages went to Argentina and Brazil. But there are several instances I've read where Argentina just had better farmland, so there's a higher density of German-Russians in Argentina than in Brazil. Some German-Russians went back to Germany. And again, those who didn't leave — those who couldn't leave — were exiled or killed.

I think a lot of German-Russians who left didn't realize this migration was permanent. Some of them thought they would go back to Russia, that they would be reunited with the family they left behind. But in a lot of cases, that just wasn't what happened at all.

## Immigration

So immigration often meant the males would go first. They would scope things out, find work and a home, and act as a sponsor for their family to come over — providing that solid foundation for their family once they arrived.

## Immigration to North & South America

Most of the time, German families would be placed in steerage — it's a little bit cheaper, where the cargo is stored — during immigration. And specifically regarding immigration to North and South America: it's not super well documented why the German-Russians decided to go to Argentina. It could have been that the sea voyage was a cheaper fare. I've read in several instances that there were less rigorous medical controls along the border — so maybe if you had an infection or were a little sick, the U.S. wouldn't take you, but Argentina or Brazil would let you through. Or maybe it was a more favorable environment for different religious groups — say, if you were Catholic.

## Great Plains Region

So in the United States specifically — I'm going to focus on the U.S. since that's where we're living — the German-Russians primarily settled in the Great Plains region. They definitely settled other places, but primarily the Great Plains; not too much in the South, where we are, though perhaps some of you are from the Great Plains and are German-Russians yourselves. So right here in the red box we have these Great Plains regions: the Dakotas, Nebraska, Kansas, Oklahoma, parts of Texas, parts of Wyoming, and Colorado.

## Prairies of North America

In total, around 300,000 German-Russians immigrated to the U.S. during the first and second waves of immigration — up until around the 1920s or so — with most of them settling in North Dakota. So we have this map here, the prairies of North America (or the U.S., I should say). It pinpoints different towns that were favorable as German-Russians settled, and as we go further south there are fewer German-Russians in these states. North Dakota originally received around 23,000 German-Russians; South Dakota, 10,000; Nebraska, 9,000; Kansas and Colorado, 8,000 or so.

The 1920 census would probably be one of the first censuses to include first- and second-generation German-Russians. You'll see a pattern: as German-Russians settled in the U.S., it was similar to how they'd settled in Russia. A lot of Black Sea Germans settled in North and South Dakota, a lot of Mennonites settled in Kansas, and a lot of Volga Germans settled around the Lincoln, Nebraska, area. We'll get into this a little more next week, but it's worth mentioning here.

## Life on the Steppe

So Volga Germans really had been prepared for life on the steppe, as we mentioned. This Homestead Act in particular differed from their life in Russia in that it required the Volga Germans specifically to live on their own land, instead of living in the very tight, close-knit communities they were used to. You can imagine how isolating that was. This isn't specific to the German-Russians — we know the Homestead Act was a very isolating experience for everyone, having to live on these vast acres of land. But what they did do, similarly, was remain somewhat isolated from the other residents. They continued to preserve their language and their traditions for decades, and they really didn't assimilate into American culture until many decades down the road.

## Houses

They continued to buy up a lot of farmland, just like they did in Russia, and passed it along to their children. They built houses. Large families lived in these houses — it wasn't uncommon to find children, parents, grandparents, and maybe some aunts, uncles, and cousins all living in one house, finding any place they could to sleep. Again, we'll dive into this a bit more next week as we get into the censuses.

## Baptismal Certificates

So within the walls of the house you would find baptismal certificates and confirmation certificates — again, a testament to how important religion was to the German-Russian people. It's not just a Sunday activity; it's really an integral part of their life and the community they were creating for themselves.

## Churches

Churches and houses were patterned after those in the homeland in Russia, which were in turn patterned after the homeland in Germany. You can see here the churches have a very similar architectural style. The bottom church is one of the churches in Balzer, Russia, and the top church is a church in Lincoln, Nebraska, that is still standing today. They look very similar — they have the tall steeples, the columns, these whitewashed wooden churches.

You saw the map earlier — I want to point out Ellis County, which is in Kansas. As you're driving through that short-grass prairie, there are still these "cathedrals on the plains," as they call them. You can see the steeples rise over the short-grass prairie as you drive, and it's stunning, because they really are big, beautiful cathedrals that they put a lot of time, effort, and money into, because it was so important to them and their community.

## Employment

So for employment in the U.S., German-Russians worked in the sugar beet fields — in Scottsbluff, Nebraska, and northern Colorado around the Greeley area. The Volga German immigrants were extremely instrumental in developing and growing the sugar beet industry. The Great Western Sugar Company and the American Sugar Beet Company often paid for passage on the different railroads — like the Union Pacific or the Burlington — to take German-Russians out west to work in the fields. They would take whole families out west. During farming season, they'd have to live in tents or shacks; if they were lucky, they could sleep in one-bedroom rooms, all while working in the field. The American Historical Society of Germans from Russia has a ton of Great Western Sugar Company photos that are just fantastic — there's one of a man here with his child — really striking images of people working in this industry.

## Statistics

A little statistics for you: many started off working as sugar beet workers. In the 1920s, 50% of these Germans were working as laborers — topping the beets, working long hours in the fields. Ten years later, in 1930, only 15% were laborers and a full 85% were owning and farming their own fields. They rose to the top very quickly, again very similar to how they rose to the top in Russia. They're extremely hard workers; they know how to work the prairie, they know how to work hard. Plus they'd been immigrating from country to country, trying to figure out how to live, and with that comes a really great work ethic and the ability to power through hard times. This is one of my favorite photos, because it shows a mountain of beets behind some hardworking men. These beets often weighed 10 pounds — they were not small. It was a really hard job.

## Child Labor

And, believe it or not, children often worked in the beet fields — totally not uncommon. In this photo you see kids as young as four or five. They would leave for the beet fields before school ended and return after school had started, and often had to repeat grades because of it. There have been many papers published about child labor in the early days of beet farming. This really demanded long hours, low wages, and back-breaking work — for the men, women, and children who were all involved.

## Wheat Farming

So, unlike most other farmers in the Great Plains — we've said this before — German-Russians were already well experienced at prairie-style agriculture after living in Russia. Wheat farming was a main source of income, particularly in Kansas, when the Mennonites brought over that Turkey Red wheat I was talking about. Again, Turkey Red wheat was so well adapted to grow in the Great Plains that it became really important to the Kansas economy up until the 1940s — so this is very recent.

## Railroad Work

Aside from working in agriculture, a lot of men worked on the railroads — the Union Pacific, the Kansas Pacific, the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe. It was another popular job, and there were a lot of perks. It was a pretty enticing job: it offered free housing, free sleeping cars out west. The railroads granted land for schools and churches, and supplied some farmers with seeds. So they worked there and got something in return, certainly.

## Dutch Hop

So while the German-Russians weren't working in the fields doing back-breaking work, they certainly knew how to have a good time. The Dutch Hop dance is a real favorite of a lot of German-Russians. It was music they brought to the United States, part of their culture and tradition — held weekly in houses and dance halls. They still take place to this day; I've been to Dutch Hop dances, and they're a hoot. They feature accordions, keyboards, and definitely the hammered dulcimer, and they're a lot of fun. They were a constant source of entertainment, for sure.

## Food

And of course, I couldn't do this presentation without talking about food, which is very popular too. In the top right here we have a runza — if you've ever been to the Midwest, you may have seen one. (I think Amy is nodding her head — maybe she has.) These are stuffed bread rolls of cabbage, ground beef, and onions. These are homemade runzas, but Runza is also a trademarked fast-food restaurant — so these were originally German-Russian runzas.

You also have kuchen, which is a really popular dish — but there's a little war between the Black Sea and the Volga Germans over what the *real* kuchen is. Volga Germans have the coffee-cake kuchen; Black Sea Germans have the custard kuchen. If it were up to me, they're both extremely delicious, so I think everybody wins this game. You'll also find Grebbel, which is fried dough, or belina, which are pancakes.

I just learned while preparing this presentation that many traditional German-Russian recipes don't list tomatoes — because tomatoes hadn't really been adapted into their foodways when they were living in Russia. So I thought that was kind of interesting to share.

As I said, the German-Russians were a people of movement. They traveled from country to country carrying on their hard work, their ethics, their customs, and their cultures. But their story isn't over by any means. The community of German-Russians is alive; they are still working on preserving their heritage and sharing it with future generations — their children and their grandchildren. The third part of this series will focus more on those communities trying to preserve German-Russian history, how to get involved in them, and how they're discovering and disseminating German-Russian information. There are many places doing this, and they're all definitely worth looking into if you want to continue expanding your knowledge about the German-Russian people.

## Reading Resources

So here are just some additional reading resources I thought I'd add. I'm going to pause on this slide so you can write them down if you want. All of them do a really good job covering the history of the German-Russians — of course, I only have 30 or 40 minutes, so I've certainly left some things out, but these are really good texts to help fill in the gaps.

*From Catherine to Khrushchev* is an extremely popular book; it's the one I read when I first got started learning about Germans from Russia, and it did a really good job of creating a good foundation for me.

*The Czar's Germans* — I have a particular liking for this one, because Hattie Plum Williams was a student at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln. She did her thesis and dissertation on German-Russians in the Lincoln area. She graduated with a PhD in 1917 — a woman graduating with a PhD in 1917 — and did a local census of Lincoln, Nebraska, German-Russians, and wrote this book based on her thesis and dissertation.

And just a few more books — these are more geared toward U.S. settlements. There's certainly more on the North and South America stories, but since we're focusing on the U.S. right now, this one is more about the Black Sea Germans. And then there's a pretty interesting book by Richard Sallet about the German-Russian settlements in the U.S.

## Conclusion

So that's kind of the end of my presentation. Thank you all for being here. I hope you learned something new, and I'm happy to take any questions — you can put them in the chat box if you have any.

### Persecution and Deportation

**Host:** "Were the pogroms that pushed the German-Russians out as violent as those experienced by German Jews? Did the Russian military do the massacres, or did they incite civilians to fight the Germans?"

**Sarah:** Yeah, it was pretty horrific. There are several publications that HSGR — the American Historical Society of Germans from Russia — has published that are memoirs from a lot of people who have firsthand or secondhand accounts of what went on as they were forcibly removed from their homes. And it's pretty grim. A lot of them had to dig their own graves; they'd be made to stand in front of the graves they dug, and then they'd be shot.

During the famine, they were having to peel bark off the trees and boil it to eat, because there was nothing else. They'd be sent to gulags in Kazakhstan — and a lot of those gulags in Kazakhstan actually weren't shut down until later, until after the concentration camps were shut down. So, the German-Russian history, we say, is kind of a grim one. They were really discriminated against, and forcibly removed from their lands multiple times. There were mass deportations in the 1940s because of Stalin — he just kicked them right out, their villages were destroyed, completely wiped out.

It's truly amazing to me that the records we have continue to exist, because I can't believe they survived — they just tried to eliminate them completely. So, you know, I don't want to compare, but that discrimination across the board was horrible.

A lot of the German-Russians, when they came to the U.S. or immigrated out of Russia, would not talk about their life in Russia — I think it was too painful for them. So some of the children or grandchildren of those first-generation U.S. citizens maybe didn't know the German language, because their parents didn't teach it to them, or didn't know some customs, because their parents just didn't pass them down. So, yeah, I think it was pretty painful.

### Letters from Hell

**Sarah:** Letters from Hell — I see Kevin there holding up *Letters from Hell*. So let me tell you a little about this. We have this famine going on in the 1920s, and the Volga Germans are suffering terribly — as I said, peeling bark off trees and boiling it to eat. Some of them had family in the States, so they were corresponding back and forth. People in the U.S. were messaging their families along the Volga River, saying, "Hey, what do you need?" And these people are saying, "We need money — send it." Well, they could send money, but Russia was opening all the envelopes and taking out the money before it even got to the families who needed it.

There are these letters that were written back and forth between families — we call them the Letters from Hell — and they document what these people were going through. HSGR (I'll do a little plug here) just published the first volume of the Letters from Hell. It's incredibly sad, but also incredibly interesting. This is actually my favorite part of German-Russian history — the famine, and these letters — because, though they're painful to read, they are so real. You get to know these people on such an intimate level, because they are baring their souls through these letters, just trying to reach out for help as best they can. The people writing them are just anyone from the community.

These letters were published in newspapers to get broader awareness of what was going on along the Volga River. So it wasn't just the families reading them — communities who maybe had no relation to the Germans from Russia were reading these letters and saying, "Wait, what's going on over there?" That prompted the Volga Relief Society, which sent aid to help the Volga Germans.
