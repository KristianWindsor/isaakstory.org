## What this extract contains

Eighteen documents in chronological order. Thirteen of them (Nos. 102–139) are also in the Life in Bessarabia extract; the five post-1878 documents (Nos. 149, 150, 151, 152, 160) appear only here, since that extract stops at 1878:

**Push factors and the exit process (1849–1878)**

- **No. 102 (1849), No. 104 (1852), No. 112 (1856)** — the shifting rules on colonists' departure abroad, tightened during the 1848–49 European revolutions and relaxed afterward.
- **No. 123 (1861)** — the treasury-debt levy raised to 10 rubles per family for the German colonists of the Bessarabian region specifically, the highest bracket outside Kherson and the Molochna.
- **No. 124 (1865)** — colonists who resettle between governorates without ministerial permission lose colonist status and its privileges forever.
- **No. 127 (1866)** — the first governorates' colonies transferred to the general institutions for peasant affairs; the southern colonies, Bessarabia included, are explicitly left for later, and that "later" is 1871.
- **No. 128 (1868)** — passport issuance devolved to the district and village boards, simplifying the paperwork of leaving a colony.
- **No. 129 (1871)** — the central act: colonist status abolished, Bessarabia named in the title, former colonists reclassified as "settler-proprietors" within the peasant estate. Two articles matter most here. Art. 9, para. b opens a **ten-year window** (i.e. to 1881) in which a settler may renounce Russian subjecthood and leave Russia without surrendering any part of his accumulated capital to the treasury — the concession the Isaaks used in 1878, and which expired three years later. Art. 5, para. a, note preserves the old exemption from recruit duty only "until the issuance of a general law on military service" — the warning shot that became the 1874 conscription statute. Art. 4, para. i requires all village and volost record-keeping to be in Russian, a language almost no colonist then read.
- **No. 130 (1874)** — renunciation of Russian subjecthood tied to the new conscription statute.
- **No. 131 (1874)** — the exit deadline: intent to emigrate must be declared before 1 September of one's conscription year, and those who declare and then fail to leave are conscripted without drawing lots, as evaders.
- **No. 139 (1878)** — conscription exemption extended to foreign settlers who had entered on the terms of the 1861 private-lands decree.

**The colonies and the people who stayed (1866–1897)**

- **No. 126 (1866)** — a District Committee of the Lutheran auxiliary fund established at Tarutino, the largest Bessarabian colony and seat of the region's oldest parish (1815), so that the 28,000 Bessarabian colonists' contributions would build churches and schools in Bessarabia instead of going across the Dniester to Odessa.
- **No. 137 (1877)** — after the Guardianship Committee's dissolution, Lutheran church property in the former colonies of Bessarabia and the southern governorates passes to elected parish church councils, and parishioners gain the right to call their own preachers.
- **No. 149 (1890)** — the Senate finally rules, eleven years late, that "settler-proprietors" are entitled to the same protection from ruinous debt-collection as other peasants.
- **No. 150 (1890)** — all Lutheran church schools outside the Baltic governorates subordinated to the Ministry of Public Education.
- **No. 151 (1891)** — consistory presidents appointed by the emperor on the Minister of Internal Affairs' recommendation.
- **No. 152 (1891)** — Lutheran vital records to be kept in Russian from 1 January 1892.
- **No. 160 (1897)** — Russian introduced "as far as possible, gradually" as the language of instruction in former-colonist schools, with the native language and religious instruction preserved.

Documents on the emigrants' arrival in America are not present, and cannot be: the collection is Russian state legislation and stops at the border.

## Historical background from the compiler's Introduction (paraphrase)

This is a collection of **Russian imperial state acts**; it has no jurisdiction over, and contains no documents about, Dakota Territory or the United States. What it does contain — and what is reproduced below — is the Russian side of the emigration story, in two parts.

The first is the machinery of departure: the rules for leaving Russia, renouncing Russian subjecthood, and the conscription obligations that former colonists (people in the Isaak family's exact legal position after 1871) had to satisfy or evade before they could go. The compiler's Introduction frames 1871–1874 as the hinge. The abolition of colonist status in 1871 (doc. No. 129) and the extension of general military conscription to former colonists in 1874 broke a promise that Catherine II and every emperor since had made to the colonists and their descendants in perpetuity; the compiler is blunt that this "profoundly undermined the trust of Russian Germans in the supreme authority" and produced "a sharp rise in emigration sentiment" (endnote to doc. No. 130). Those two acts, together with a decade of preceding measures — the levy increase of 1861 that fell specifically on the Bessarabian colonists (doc. No. 123), the 1865 penalty stripping colonist status from anyone who moved between governorates without permission (doc. No. 124), and the transfer of the colonies to ordinary peasant administration begun in 1866 (doc. No. 127) — are the push factors behind the 1878 departure for Dakota Territory.

The second is what the same legislation meant for the majority of Bessarabian Lutherans, who did not leave. For them the years after 1871 were a slow absorption into the ordinary Russian peasant estate and, from about 1890, into a deliberate program of Russification. The Guardianship Committee that had administered the southern colonies since 1818 was wound up in 1877, and with it the special church arrangements the colonist parishes had enjoyed (doc. No. 137). Lutheran church schools passed to the Ministry of Public Education in 1890 (doc. No. 150); consistory presidents became imperial appointees in 1891 (doc. No. 151); parish registers — the birth, marriage, and death records of Kulm and Sofiental — had to be kept in Russian from 1 January 1892 (doc. No. 152); and instruction in the village schools was to shift to Russian from 1897 (doc. No. 160). Doc. No. 149 shows the practical cost of the 1871 settlement in a single case: courts denying "settler-proprietors" even the protections granted to former serfs, and selling a man's last horse.

The compiler presents this whole arc as the beginning of the process that eventually culminated in the much harsher WWI-era "liquidation legislation" of Nicholas II — which is out of scope for this file since it postdates 1900 and is excluded from all three files per the extraction criteria.

This is a paraphrase, not a verbatim reproduction of the copyrighted Introduction essay; the full text is in the full version listed above.

## No. 102. DECISION OF THE STATE COUNCIL ON THE CONDITIONS FOR COLONISTS' DEPARTURE ABROAD, CONFIRMED BY NICHOLAS I (1849)

23051. — February 28. Highest-confirmed opinion of the State Council, published April 25. — On the release abroad of foreigners who had entered the estate of rural inhabitants under the designation of colonists.

The State Council, in the Department of Economy and in General Assembly, having examined the most humble report of the General Assembly of the first three Departments and the Heraldry of the Governing Senate on the question: whether, with regard to colonists who have declared a wish to return abroad, the permission of the Governing Senate should be sought — took into account that, with regard to foreigners who had entered the estate of rural inhabitants under the name of colonists, there exist special rules contained in the Code of Statutes on the Good Order of Towns and Villages (Digest of Laws, Vol. 12). By force of these rules, they are granted free departure abroad altogether, subject only to certain conditions concerning the one-time payment of taxes and debts owed by them and the disposition of lands belonging to them in ownership (Arts. 151, 462, 542 and 543); but those rules contain no provision that permission of the Governing Senate must be sought for colonists to relinquish Russian subjecthood, as is prescribed in the Laws on Estates (Art. 1406) with regard to foreigners in general, that is, those who have entered other social estates of the state; it is stated only that in the case of departure from the state (without distinction between temporary absence and departure forever), colonists are obliged to furnish themselves with passports, through communications of their authorities, from those places and persons to whom, on the basis of the Statute on Passports and Fugitives, the issuance of such passports is assigned as a duty (Art. 461). This enactment has always been understood in this sense in practical application, for, according to the certification of the Minister of State Domains, the Guardianship Committee for Foreign Settlers of the Southern Region of Russia, established as early as 1818, has always released colonists back abroad at its own discretion. In consequence of this, the State Council, in agreement with the conclusion of the General Assembly of the Governing Senate, recognizing for its part that, given the definiteness of the rules and conditions by which colonists may be discharged for return departure abroad, the seeking of special permission from the higher government for this would constitute a mere formality, without any substantial benefit to the matter, and would serve only to needlessly constrain the colonists, resolved in its opinion: 1) To confirm the Senate's report, and, in consequence thereof, to leave the aforementioned Committee with the right it has enjoyed until now, and further to supplement Art. 1406 of the Laws on Estates (Digest of Laws, Vol. 9) with the following note: "the release abroad of foreigners who have entered the estate of rural inhabitants under the designation of colonists is carried out in observance of the rules established for that purpose in the Statute on Colonies." <...>

Resolution. His Imperial Majesty was pleased to confirm the opinion rendered in the General Assembly of the State Council on the question of whether, with regard to colonists who have declared a wish to return abroad, the permission of the Governing Senate should be sought, and commanded that it be carried out.

*PSZRI. Coll. 2. Vol. XXIV. P. 121.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 102.

The question of whether the departure of colonists abroad required the permission of the Senate arose at that time in connection with the revolutions of 1848–1849 in a number of European countries, including Germany and Austria. Under these conditions the Russian authorities sought to eliminate any possibility of revolutionary ideas—let alone their bearers—penetrating the country. The present act confirmed the exception to the rules on departure that existed for colonists, in contrast to foreign immigrants of other estates: the Board of Trustees for Foreign Settlers of the Southern Region of Russia could, as before, grant permission to leave at its own discretion.

This decision applied only to colonists departing abroad permanently. However, as is evident from doc. No. 112, on May 24, 1849, measures were also adopted with respect to the temporary departure of colonists: in order to restrict it "until calm and order are restored in Europe," it was prescribed that "the Highest permission" be sought each time for such absences. This question appeared far more fundamental to the Russian authorities of the time, since the likelihood of undesirable ideas penetrating the country was much greater in the case of temporary absences.

## No. 104. DECISION OF THE COMMITTEE OF MINISTERS ON THE DEPARTURE OF COLONISTS ABROAD, CONFIRMED BY NICHOLAS I (1852)

26070. — March 18. Highest-confirmed statute of the Committee of Ministers. — On the discharge of colonists abroad.

A memorandum of the Minister of State Domains, of February 26, was heard, on permitting twenty-one Bulgarian colonists to travel to Mount Athos, and one female colonist to Jerusalem, for veneration of the Holy places, and on discharging colonists abroad henceforth without seeking special Highest permission for this.

The Committee resolved: to confirm this submission.

The Sovereign Emperor was Highly pleased to consent to the discharge of the said colonists, whereupon there followed a command in His Imperial Majesty's own hand: For departure to Germany, always seek My permission.

*PSZRI. Coll. 2. Vol. XXVII. P. 158.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 104.

By the time this act was adopted, the European revolutionary storms of 1848—1849 had long since subsided, and the authorities considered it possible to lift the restrictions then in force on the temporary travel of colonists abroad (see doc. No. 112). However, Nicholas I did not approve a complete lifting of the restrictions — an extremely rare case of a Russian emperor expressing his own particular opinion on a serious matter concerning the German colonists. This shows the importance Nicholas attached to this problem, despite the fact that temporary trips abroad by colonists were a rather infrequent occurrence.

## No. 112. DECISION OF THE COMMITTEE OF MINISTERS ON THE PROCEDURE FOR COLONISTS' DEPARTURE ABROAD, APPROVED BY ALEXANDER II (1856)

30380. — April 10. Highest-approved regulation of the Committee of Ministers. — On the procedure for granting foreign colonists leave to travel abroad.

The memorandum of the Minister of State Domains, of March 24, on permitting the colonist Pahl to be absent for one year abroad, was heard.

The Committee resolved: to grant the said colonist leave to travel abroad, in accordance with the report.

In discussing both this and other similar cases, the Committee has repeatedly noted that colonists' leaves of absence abroad are presently excepted from the procedure established by article 461 of the Statutes on Colonies of Foreigners, Digest of Laws, Vol. XII (1842 edition). This exception was made in 1849, when, owing to the political unrest disturbing Western Europe, the Minister of State Domains, with a view to possibly reducing colonists' absences abroad, petitioned the Committee of Ministers that, until the restoration of calm and order in Europe, Highest permission be sought each time, through the established procedure, for all such absences. This report, by resolution of the Committee, was honored with the Highest approval of His Imperial Majesty the late Emperor Nicholas Pavlovich of blessed memory on May 24, 1849.

Thereafter, in 1852, Adjutant General Count Kiselev, finding that the influence of the causes which had prompted the Government to adopt this measure had more or less already been removed, considered it possible, with a view to reducing correspondence and preventing delay in granting colonists leave to travel abroad, to return to the procedure previously in force for matters of this kind. The Committee of Ministers resolved to approve this report; but on the journal concerning this matter, the Emperor Nicholas Pavlovich, now with God, deigned to write in his own hand, on March 18, 1852 (26070): "For leave to Germany, always seek My permission."

Now the Committee, having examined all the details of this matter, found that, with the change in circumstances that had called for special precautions regarding the granting of leave to colonists to travel abroad, and with the restoration of peace and calm in Europe, there is no inconvenience whatsoever in returning again to the procedure for granting foreign colonists leave to travel abroad that existed prior to 1849, and accordingly resolved to seek His Imperial Majesty's Most Gracious permission for this.

To this resolution of the Committee there followed His Imperial Majesty's command, written in his own hand: "Agreed."

*PSZRI. Coll. 2. Vol. XXXI. Pp. 209-210.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 112.

The document finally abolished the restrictions on the temporary travel of colonists abroad that had been introduced in 1849 and partially retained in 1852 at the insistence of Nicholas I (see doc. No. 104). By this point these restrictions had become an obvious anachronism, all the more so since the new emperor, unlike his "late" father, sought to pursue a liberal policy.

## No. 123. RESOLUTION OF THE COMMITTEE OF MINISTERS ON RAISING LEVIES ON THE GERMAN COLONISTS OF NEW RUSSIA AND BESSARABIA, CONFIRMED BY ALEXANDER II (1861)

37062. — May 30. Most Highly confirmed resolution of the Committee of Ministers, announced to the Senate by the Minister of State Domains. — On increasing the levy on the German colonists of the New Russian governorates and the Bessarabian region.

The Sovereign Emperor, upon the submission of the Minister of State Domains and the resolution of the Committee of Ministers, on the 30th day of May of this year, was Most Highly pleased to command: that the colonists of the three New Russian governorates and the Bessarabian region, beginning with this year 1861, be assessed, in place of the levy now paid, a levy for the repayment of the treasury debt for settlement, in the following amounts:

1. German colonists of the Bessarabian region, 10 rubles per family.

2. German colonists of Yekaterinoslav Governorate: those of Josephstal and Rybalskoye, 2 rubles 85 kopecks, except two families of the latter, now paying 4 rubles 28 kopecks, who shall pay 5 rubles, and the colonists of Yamburg, 2 rubles 30 kopecks per family.

3. German colonists of Kherson Governorate: a) of the Liebental and Berezan districts, 15 rubles per family; b) of the Kuchurgan and Glückstal districts and the separate colony of Hoffnungstal, 10 rubles; and c) of the Swedish district, 3 rubles.

4. German colonists of Taurida Governorate: a) of the Neusatz and Zürichtal districts, 3 rubles, except the colony of Hertsenberg, which for the time being remains at the former rate of 1 ruble 42¾ kopecks per family, and b) 382 families of colonists of the Molochna district, from 10 to 20 rubles per family.

*PSZRI. Coll. 2. Vol. XXXVI, Part 1. P. 800.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 123.

The increase in dues under this document was intended to speed up the completion of the repayment of the treasury debt by the colonists of southern Russia.

Hoffnungstal (Tsebrikovo) — a colony of Hoffnungstal volost, Tiraspol district, located northeast of Tiraspol, founded in 1819, 1,145 inhabitants (1859).

Herzenberg (Kizilnik) — a colony of Zürichtal district (later Salyn volost, Feodosia district), located north of Feodosia, founded in 1804, 39 inhabitants (1858).

## No. 124. DECISION OF THE STATE COUNCIL ON SANCTIONS AGAINST COLONISTS WHO RESETTLED WITHOUT PERMISSION FROM ONE GOVERNORATE TO ANOTHER, CONFIRMED BY ALEXANDER II (1865)

41919. — March 14. Most Highly confirmed opinion of the State Council, published on 26 April. — On depriving colonists who resettled without permission from one governorate to another of the rights granted to colonist status.

The State Council, in the Department of State Economy and in General Session, having examined the submission of the Minister of State Domains on supplementing Article 435 of the Statute on Colonies, resolved: to confirm the present submission and, in consequence thereof, to supplement Article 435 of the Statute on Colonies (Digest of Laws, Vol. XII, Part 11, ed. 1857) with a note of the following content: "Colonists who resettle without authorization and without the permission of the Minister of State Domains from one governorate to another are excluded by the chief local administration from colonist status and, forever losing the rights and privileges granted to that status, are enrolled among other taxable estates on general grounds."

Resolution. His Imperial Majesty was Most Highly pleased to confirm the opinion rendered in the General Session of the State Council on supplementing Article 435 of the Statute on Colonies, and commanded its execution.

*PSZRI. Coll. 2. Vol. XL, Part 1. P. 315.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 124.

By introducing strict sanctions, the authorities sought to curb the unauthorized resettlement of colonists from one governorate to another. Amid an increasingly acute shortage of land, especially in the Volga region, with its rapidly growing colonist population and archaic system of per-capita land use, this process had taken on wide proportions. The document had no appreciable effect: only a few years later, in 1871, colonist status was abolished (see doc. No. 129), and with it disappeared the main obstacle to the widespread resettlement of former colonists across the territory of Russia.

## No. 127. DECISION ON SUBORDINATING THE COLONIES OF A NUMBER OF GOVERNORATES TO THE GENERAL INSTITUTIONS FOR PEASANT AFFAIRS, CONFIRMED BY ALEXANDER II (1866)

44000. — December 17. Highest-confirmed resolution of the Main Committee on the organization of the rural estate, communicated to the Senate by the Minister of State Domains. — On the transfer of the colonies of foreign settlers to the jurisdiction of the general institutions for peasant affairs.

The Sovereign Emperor, upon the report of the Minister of State Domains and the finding of the Main Committee on the organization of the rural estate, on 17 December 1866, was Highest pleased to command:

1. The colonies of foreign settlers, which are under the jurisdiction of the Chambers of State Domains in the governorates of Voronezh, Novgorod, Samara, St. Petersburg, and Chernigov, shall henceforth be transferred to the jurisdiction of the general provincial and district institutions, as well as the local institutions for peasant affairs.

2. Until such time as the question of the organization of public administration in the colonies, as well as of the courts, is resolved in the established manner, the currently existing rules on the internal public administration of the colonies (Code of Laws, Vol. XII, Part II, Statute on Colonies) shall remain in force, while the authority belonging to the Chambers of State Domains, with respect to colonies passing out of their jurisdiction, for the adjudication of litigation among colonists (Arts. 6, 38 clause 7, 485 and 488 of the Statute on Colonies) shall be transferred to the local Provincial Presences for Peasant Affairs.

*PSZRI. Coll. 2. Vol. XLI. Sec. 2. P. 408.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 127.

This act began the process of transferring the colonies to the jurisdiction of the general administrative bodies, completed in 1871 (see doc. No. 129). In Samara governorate it affected the colonists who had settled in Samara district since the late 1850s (see doc. No. 118). Now only the remaining colonies of the Volga region, the colonies of southern Russia, Transcaucasia, and Livonia remained under the jurisdiction of special administrative bodies.

### No. 129.

This document is among the most important state acts concerning Russian Germans. It determined the future fate of hundreds of thousands of former German colonists for decades to come. The Regulations of 1871 belong to a series of reform laws of the reign of Alexander II intended to modernize the entire system of agrarian relations, a process begun by the Peasant Reform of 1861.

Except for Sarepta, which is covered by a proviso in art. 1 of the Regulations, the document also did not apply to the Transcaucasian and Livonian colonies, although they had been established on state lands. Special acts were subsequently adopted for all these groups of colonists (see docs. No. 136, 161, 162).

The document was drafted over a number of years by a representative commission that included such an expert on colonist affairs as A.A. Klaus, a high-ranking official of the Ministry of State Domains and himself descended from the colonists of southern Russia, author of the book *Our Colonies: Experiments and Materials on the History and Statistics of Foreign Colonization in Russia* (St. Petersburg, 1869). After the commission presented the results of its work to this ministry, the Regulations were discussed and revised for several more years in the higher state bodies. See: Fleischhauer I. Op. cit. Pp. 281–284.

As often happens, such a lengthy process of preparation and coordination did not benefit the document. It is marked by neither clarity nor consistency. Even the new concept it introduces, "peasant-proprietors (former colonists)," is quite ambiguous, which had significant consequences later on (see doc. No. 149). Proceeding from the well-meaning intention to bring colonists as close as possible in legal status to Russian peasants of other estates, while at the same time preserving, as far as possible, the tried-and-tested system of organization and self-government of the colonies (insofar as it is even appropriate to speak of self-government under the extremely strict regulation prescribed by the Colony Statute of 1857), the authorities ultimately arrived at a number of highly questionable decisions.

Thus, the Regulations provide that all record-keeping at village and volost assemblies of the former colonies and in the corresponding volost courts be conducted only in Russian (art. 4, para. i), a language which at that time the overwhelming majority of German colonists did not know (in large part through the fault of the authorities themselves). The document states that the existing norms for performing recruit duty (from which all colonists settled on state lands, except for the Volga Mennonites, had been exempted) would be preserved only "until the issuance of a general law on military service" (art. 5, para. a, note), making it clear that with the adoption of this law a number of the most important conditions, guaranteed "in perpetuity," on which the colonists had at one time agreed to settle in Russia, would be abolished. The Regulations state that the transformation and organization of the village and volost administrations of the settlers would be handled by various officials, without any participation whatsoever by the former colonists themselves (art. 10). The document requires communities of settlers who have surplus land compared to the "established normal allotment" to admit into their midst those with insufficient land (art. 16), even though, for example, in the Volga colonies with their system of per-capita land use, the concept of a "normal allotment" had long since lost any real meaning. And to top it all off, while declaring the preservation of the former colonists' existing personal privileges (art. 2), the Regulations at the same time unambiguously show them the door, offering anyone who wishes to do so the right, within 10 years, to renounce Russian citizenship and leave Russia on favorable terms — without paying into the treasury a portion of the capital they had accumulated, as had previously been required of colonists (art. 9, para. b).

The German colonists themselves could not fail to notice such provisions, although, of course, few of them studied and absorbed from beginning to end this voluminous document, written in dry bureaucratic language. It is not surprising that in the very same Volga colonies the Regulations were met with considerable wariness. The matter evidently lay not only in a whole series of points in the document that provoked at the very least bewilderment, but also in the fact that unerring popular instinct told the colonists: it was not merely certain rules of their life that were changing, but that fundamental changes were coming in the very attitude of the authorities toward Russian Germans, or even toward Germans as such. In principle this conclusion was obvious, especially since the issuance of the Regulations practically coincided in time with the unification of Germany and the proclamation of the German Empire — events that were received in Russia, to put it mildly, without enthusiasm.

### No. 130.

After the Regulations of 1871 (doc. No. 129), the "general law on military service" promised in them (art. 5, para. a, note) was not long in coming — the Statute on Military Service of January 1, 1874. The Regulations themselves may be regarded as a rather clumsy embodiment of fairly sound ideas. The Statute, in all likelihood, deserves a more critical assessment. The point is not that the German colonists in principle rejected performing military service on an equal footing with other Russian subjects. Only the Mennonites (for religious reasons) were its categorical opponents, and even then not all of them. However, Alexander II and his government had no right to forget that previous Russian emperors, beginning with Catherine II, had solemnly promised all colonists entering Russia, as well as their descendants, exemption from military service for all time. The Statute of 1874 struck down these highest assurances at a single stroke, and no intelligible explanations, let alone apologies, followed from the authorities.

This high-handed action profoundly undermined the trust of Russian Germans in the supreme authority, which over the eight decades since Catherine II had never once deceived them and which they had therefore long been accustomed to trust unconditionally. This distrust naturally extended not only to the issue of military service. If the government had carelessly violated the laws that existed with respect to colonists in this sphere, then who could guarantee that it would not do the same in any other matter? The result, which could easily have been foreseen had anyone wished to, was a sharp rise in emigration sentiment — above all among the Mennonites, but partly also among other groups of Russian Germans, including not only colonists.

This phenomenon is reflected in the present act, which appeared almost immediately after the adoption of the Statute of 1874. Judging by the surnames listed in the act, the persons in question are not colonists but Baltic German nobles. They had wished to renounce Russian citizenship as early as 1873, that is, under the influence not of the Statute on Military Service itself but, evidently, already of the announcement of its forthcoming adoption, contained in the Regulations of 1871. The authorities, as the document shows, used the Statute to counteract the emigration sentiment that it itself had provoked, but this could not, of course, reverse the general trend that had emerged.

## No. 149. DECISION OF THE SENATE ON THE PROCEDURE FOR COLLECTING PRIVATE DEBTS FROM FORMER COLONISTS (1890)

329. On the application of note 3 to article 24 of the General Statute on Peasants Emerging from Serfdom to peasant-proprietor settlers (former colonists).

The Governing Senate, in the general assembly of the 1st and cassation departments, on 22 January 1890, having heard the proposal of the Chief Procurator, based on the order of the Minister of Justice No. 601, on the printing in the Collection of Statutes and Orders of the Government of the decision of the Governing Senate rendered by the general assembly of the 1st and cassation departments on 28 May 1879 concerning the procedure for collecting private debts from peasant-proprietors, resolved: that the decision of the said general assembly of the Governing Senate, of 28 May 1879, concerning the procedure for collecting private debts from peasant-proprietors, be printed in the Collection of Statutes and Orders of the Government, and that notice thereof, together with a copy of the said decision, be sent to the office of the Senate Printing House.

28 May 1879. By decree of His Imperial Majesty, the Governing Senate heard: the report of the Governor of Kherson, forwarded with the notice of the chancellery of the 1st department of the Governing Senate, concerning a dispute that had arisen between the peasant institutions and the Odessa district justice-of-the-peace assembly regarding the application to peasant-proprietor settlers (former colonists) of the statutory provisions and of the Senate's decree of 11 January 1878, No. 1,106, concerning peasants in the collection of private debts from them.

The Governor of Kherson, in his report of 12 December 1878, No. 3,477, submitted for the resolution of the 1st department of the Governing Senate a copy of the journal of the Kherson Provincial Presence for Peasant Affairs of 23 November 1878, from which it appears that, on the question that had arisen concerning the satisfaction of private debts of temporarily obligated peasants, the general assembly of the cassation departments of the Governing Senate, at its session of 15 December 1869, recognized that, according to the 1869 continuation of the Code of Laws, issued already after the Judicial Statutes of 1864, the rules set forth in Vol. XII, Part 2, of the Statute on Order in State Villages, articles 384-407, to which reference is made, and in the General Statute on Peasants, are not shown as repealed or supplemented, and that, therefore, the said rules concerning the procedure for the execution of court decisions relating to monetary claims against peasants must be considered to have retained their force even after the issuance of the Judicial Statutes of 1864, in which the repeal of those rules is not expressly stated. Since, according to information received in the Ministries of Internal Affairs and of Justice, it appeared that, in the course of collecting private debts from peasants, the most essential property in their households was often sold, there having been repeated instances of the sale, to cover such claims, of literally all of a peasant's property, the Minister of Justice, in agreement with the Ministers of Internal Affairs and of Finance, communicated by circular proposals the above-stated clarification of the general assembly of the cassation departments of the Governing Senate to the chairmen of the assemblies of justices of the peace and the chairmen of the district courts, and requested them to instruct the court bailiffs attached to the assemblies and courts entrusted to them to be guided, in the aforesaid case, by note 3 to article 24 of the General Statute on Peasants and articles 384-407 of Vol. XII, Part 2, of the Statute on Order in State Villages. Notwithstanding this clarification, difficulties were constantly encountered in practice in the precise understanding and application of the cited articles, owing to the fact that the execution of court decisions on the collection of debts from peasants, under the Judicial Statutes of 20 November 1864, is entrusted to court bailiffs, who in these cases are guided exclusively by the relevant articles of the Judicial Statutes. The Governing Senate, as appears from its decree of the 1st department of 11 January 1878, No. 1,106, in order to remove such difficulties and misunderstandings in those cases where the execution of a court decision on the collection of a debt from individual peasants, bypassing the village authorities, is entrusted to a court bailiff, found it necessary to clarify: a) that the court bailiff, in the event of non-payment by a peasant-debtor of the sum awarded against him by a court decision, must first, in accordance with articles 384 and 385 of the Statute on Order in State Villages, set a term within which the defendant is obliged to pay the debt; b) that, upon expiration of the term set, in the event of non-payment of the debt by the defendant, the bailiff may proceed to inventory his property, but only in the presence of the village elder and the volost elder, in order to determine what part of the debtor peasant's property may be sold to satisfy the plaintiff without disrupting the peasant household; and c) that, out of the sum obtained through the sale of the property, the court bailiff, according to information communicated to him by the relevant volost administration, is obliged to satisfy in full the arrears recorded against the peasant-debtor in respect of taxes, communal dues, and redemption payments or landlord quit-rents, and to apply only the remaining sum to the satisfaction of the creditor and the recovery of court costs. Meanwhile, the court bailiff of the Odessa district assembly of justices of the peace, Leiman, on 16 March 1878 inventoried the movable property of the Freudental peasant-proprietor settlers Peter and Heinrich Rener in satisfaction of private debts, without observing the conditions specified in the aforementioned decree of the Governing Senate, explaining that that decree applies only to peasants, but not to settlers; and another court bailiff of the same justice-of-the-peace assembly, Braikevich, in 1877 inventoried the property of certain peasant-proprietor settlers of the village of Kuban and scheduled it for sale on 20 April 1878, and although the volost elder informed the court bailiff that the inventoried property constituted the last necessity in the household, the court bailiff nevertheless sold this property; for example, he sold at auction the last and only horse of the settler Afanasy Meikhov for 15 rubles, and out of the proceeds paid 4 rubles 66 kopecks toward Meikhov's arrears of state and zemstvo obligations, retaining the remaining money for travel expenses (progony). Recognizing that the Governing Senate's decree of 11 January 1878, No. 1,106, and the statutory provisions cited therein, apply to all peasants, including settlers (former colonists), the chairman of the Odessa district Presence for Peasant Affairs, pursuant to reports from the Freudental volost administration and the Kuban volost elder, communicated the above-described actions of the court bailiffs to the Odessa district assembly of justices of the peace. In response, the assembly of justices of the peace communicated that the settlers Reiner could themselves petition on their own behalf, and that the assembly did not consider itself entitled to extend the effect of the cited decree of the Governing Senate to peasant-proprietor settlers, since it applies exclusively to peasants. On 27 September 1878 the court bailiff of the same assembly of justices of the peace, Leiman, inventoried the property of the settler of the village of Kandel, Georg Rieger, and scheduled it for public sale on 4 October in payment of a debt to the meshchanin Leiba Itskovich, and in doing so the bailiff invited neither the village elder nor the volost elder, whereas, as the Odessa Presence for Peasant Affairs reported, on the basis of information furnished by the Seltz volost administration, the sale of the inventoried property would bring about the complete ruin of the settler Rieger. In view of the foregoing circumstances, the Kherson Provincial Presence for Peasant Affairs and the chairman of that Presence, the Governor of Kherson, applied to the Odessa district assembly of justices of the peace, requesting it to direct that the court bailiffs subordinate to it, in enforcing claims against peasant-proprietor settlers (former colonists), be guided by the Governing Senate's decree of 11 January 1878, No. 1,106, which applies to them as well, inasmuch as, according to the precise meaning of the statutory provisions of articles 1 and 2 of the Rules on the Arrangement of Peasant-Proprietor Settlers (Former Colonists), Code of Laws on Rural Estates, 1876 edition, and of article 700, with the note thereto, of Vol. IX of the Code of Laws on Estates, 1876 edition, peasant-proprietor settlers are the same as peasants. In response, the Odessa district assembly of justices of the peace informed that it adhered to its previous opinion that the Governing Senate's decree of 11 January 1878, No. 1,106, does not extend to peasant-proprietor settlers, since the procedure set forth therein, concerning claims by private persons, applies only to peasants. Taking into consideration: first, that under the law of 4 June 1871 (Rules on the Arrangement of Peasant-Proprietor Settlers [Former Colonists], 1876 edition), the provisions of 19 February 1861 concerning peasants have been extended to former colonists; second, that, under the same law (art. 2), former colonists have entered into the composition of rural inhabitants and have been assigned "to the category of peasant-proprietors"; third, that, on the basis of the same law, former colonists have the same rural and volost administration as peasants (art. 3), and also perform the same communal obligations and zemstvo dues as peasants of all designations, and likewise pay quit-rent tax for the land allotments received under title deeds, on the same bases as state peasants (art. 5); and, finally, fourth, that in the laws on estates (Vol. IX, art. 700 and the note thereto, 1876 edition), former colonists are shown as assigned to the number of rural inhabitants — the Provincial Presence found that former colonists, transferred to the jurisdiction of the general provincial and district, as well as local, institutions for peasant affairs, are peasants as of 4 June 1871, and that, therefore, it is beyond doubt that the statutory provisions cited in the Governing Senate's decree of 11 January 1878, No. 1,106, and the decree of the Senate itself, extend to them as they do to peasants, even though the designation "peasant-proprietor settlers" is not used therein. But since the Odessa district assembly of justices of the peace holds an entirely contrary opinion, the result of which is the sale of the last property necessary in the households of peasant-proprietor settlers (former colonists), thereby ruining the peasants and rendering them potentially insolvent payers of state and zemstvo dues, the Provincial Presence for Peasant Affairs resolved: to submit the foregoing for the resolution of the Governing Senate through the 1st department. The present case was transmitted, together with the notice of the chancellery of the 1st department, to the like general assembly of the cassation departments.

Having heard the opinion of the acting deputy chief procurator, the Governing Senate, in a joint session of the 1st and cassation departments, turning to consider the question raised by the Kherson Provincial Board for Peasant Affairs as to whether the decree of the 1st Department of the Governing Senate of 11 January 1878, No. 1,106, extends to settler-proprietors (former colonists), finds that settler-proprietors (former colonists), by the exact force of articles 700, 731, and 748 of Vol. IX with the notes thereto, and article 2 of the rules on their organization, set forth in No. XV of the statute on the rural estate (special appendix to Vol. IX, 1876 edition), have entered into the composition of rural inhabitants and been assigned to the category of peasants, whereby, as stated in this latter article, they retained, on the grounds indicated in the subsequent articles, also the personal privileges which they had hitherto enjoyed. From the literal sense of the said provisions it is evident that settler-proprietors (former colonists) have been granted not merely the name of peasant-proprietors, but that, besides the personal rights and privileges established in the special rules concerning settler-proprietors and those which they had hitherto enjoyed, they must also enjoy those rights, personal and pertaining to status, which are common to all rural inhabitants assigned to the category of peasant-proprietors, and which do not contradict the special provisions concerning settler-proprietors. Such a right is unquestionably the right granted to peasants in note 3 to article 24 of the General Statute on Peasants Emerging from Serfdom, establishing the rights of peasants with respect to claims, complaints, petitions, and litigation, and consisting in the following: "monetary exactions from peasants are carried out in accordance with the rules established in articles 384–407 of the Statute on the Good Order of State Villages, and that, in the sale of peasants' property to satisfy an exaction, out of the proceeds, arrears on state and seigneurial dues, as well as on communal levies, are satisfied first and in full, and only thereafter other exactions in proportion to the claims." Basing itself precisely on this note and on other provisions of the General Statute, as well as of the Statute on Redemption and the Local Statute of the Great Russian governorates relating to the procedure for collecting arrears on state taxes, communal levies, redemption payments, and seigneurial quitrents, the Governing Senate, through the 1st Department, by decree of 11 January 1878, No. 1,106, printed in article 62

No. 16 of the Collection of Statutes and Orders of the Government, resolved the question raised, among others, of the procedure by which the inventory and sale of the property of individual peasants should be carried out to satisfy private debts adjudged by court decisions, and then, in furtherance of the aforementioned laws, issued the rule set forth above in the journal of the Kherson Provincial Board for Peasant Affairs, for the guidance of court bailiffs, in the case where the execution of court decisions on the recovery of debts from individual peasants is entrusted to them apart from the village authorities. In consequence of this, and taking into account: 1) that, on the basis of the aforementioned rules on the organization of settler-proprietors, 1876 edition, former colonists have the same village and volost administration as peasants (art. 4), and likewise perform the same communal duties and zemstvo levies as peasants, and pay for the land allotments received under title deeds, on the same grounds as state peasants, the quitrent tax (arts. 5 and 18); 2) that the proper fulfillment by settlers of the taxes and levies, zemstvo and communal duties due from them, is likewise secured by the procedure indicated in articles 187–191 of the General Statute on Peasants and the supplementary provisions thereto (art. 5); 3) that the principal motivating cause for the issuance of the rules mentioned in the Senate decree of 11 January 1878 was the necessity of extreme caution in the sale of peasants' property to satisfy private debts, in order to safeguard the economic life of the peasant estate and its financial and economic interests, and 4) that the same necessity exists with respect to settler-proprietors (former colonists), who, as stated above, form part of that same peasant estate — the Governing Senate resolves: to recognize that the decree of the Governing Senate of 11 January 1878 applies also to settler-proprietors; and to send decrees to this effect to the Kherson Governor and to the Odessa District Justice of the Peace Congress.

### No. 149.

The document clearly shows that the very vague concept of "settler-proprietors" ("poseliane-sobstvenniki"), introduced in 1871, allowed the local authorities to deny former colonists even the rights granted to former serfs. Of course, in this case the Odessa uezd congress of justices of the peace acted in a plainly biased manner, in the interests of local men of wealth to whom the settler-proprietors were in debt, but it was the authors of the Rules of 1871 themselves who created the possibility for such actions, by failing to provide adequately for the protection of former colonists' rights. It is also notable that the Senate did not hand down a final ruling on so straightforward a legal question for more than 10 years.

## No. 150. DECREE OF ALEXANDER III ON THE SUBORDINATION OF LUTHERAN SCHOOLS TO THE MINISTRY OF EDUCATION (1890)

7211. — November 22. Imperial command, announced by the Minister of Internal Affairs (Collection of Statutes 1890, December 23, art. 1184). — On the subordination of Protestant schools in the districts of the St. Petersburg and Moscow Lutheran Consistories to the Ministry of Public Education.

The Sovereign Emperor, upon the most loyal report of the Minister of Internal Affairs, on the 22nd day of November 1890, was Highest pleased to command: that all Lutheran church schools without exception in the districts of the Moscow and St. Petersburg Evangelical Lutheran Consistories, located in the governorates indicated in points 1 and 5 of article 434 of the Digest of Laws, Vol. XI, Part I, per the 1886 Supplement, together with all property belonging to these schools, be subordinated to the department of the Ministry of Public Education, on the same general basis as other primary schools under the administration of that Ministry, and with the proviso that, under the new order of administration, the right of the Protestant clergy to oversee the religious education of youth in the said establishments be preserved.

*PSZRI. Coll. 3. Vol. X. Sect. 1. Pp. 742–743.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 150.

This act applied to all Lutheran church schools outside the Baltic governorates. In doing so, the authorities took the first major step toward the nationalization (étatization) of the parish schools of the Russian Germans.

The adoption of this measure was accompanied by the introduction, in the schools, of the post of Russian-language teacher, paid as a rule by the zemstvo. As a result, friction arose between the state administration and the rural communities, which up to then had themselves paid the teachers and thus retained full control over the school and its teaching staff. See: Bobyleva S.I. The Education System of the German Colonies in the Early 20th Century and the "Liquidation Laws" (Based on Materials from the Southern Governorates of Russia) // Germans of Russia and the USSR: 1901–1941. Moscow, 2000. P. 101.

1057. On the keeping of vital records of the Lutheran confession in the Russian language.

His Imperial Majesty was Highest pleased to confirm the opinion rendered in the General Assembly of the State Council on the keeping of vital records of the Lutheran confession in the Russian language, and commanded that it be carried into effect.

Signed: for the Chairman of the State Council, State Secretary

D. Selsky.

OPINION OF THE STATE COUNCIL. 3 June 1891.

Extracted from the journals of the Department of Laws of 23 March and of the General Assembly of 20 May 1891.

The State Council, in the Department of Laws and in the General Assembly, having considered the ruling of the First General Assembly of the Governing Senate on the clarification of the language in which Lutheran preachers must keep vital records, as well as issue extracts therefrom, resolved in its opinion:

I. In amendment and supplement of the pertinent articles of the Digest of Laws on Estates (Digest of Laws, Vol. IX), the Statutes on the Spiritual Affairs of Foreign Confessions (Digest of Laws, Vol. XI, Part I), and the Digest of Local Statutes of the Baltic Governorates, to enact the following rules:

2) With respect to extracts issued, at the request of the authorities or upon petitions of private persons, from those vital records which prior to the issuance of the present law were not kept in the Russian language, evangelical Lutheran preachers are obliged to attach a translation of such extracts into Russian, certified by the preacher's signature and the affixing of the church seal.

The authentic opinion is signed in the journals by the Chairmen and Members.

*SURP. 1891. 2nd half. No. 104. Pp. 2740–2741.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 152.

This decision entirely disregarded the well-known fact that the parishioners of Lutheran churches, especially in rural areas, still had a poor command of Russian — unlike the pastors, who, under Art. 271 of the Statutes on the Religious Affairs of Foreign Confessions (doc. No. 114), were required to know it. With the translation of the church registers into Russian, parishioners were in practice deprived of any means of getting errors, frequently made in vital-records entries, corrected. Under these circumstances it would of course have been more sensible to keep the registers in two languages, but such a natural measure ran counter to the firm course toward the Russification of every aspect of the German population's life, adopted by the Russian authorities under Alexander III.

## No. 160. DECISION OF THE STATE COUNCIL ON TEACHING IN THE RUSSIAN LANGUAGE IN COLONIST SCHOOLS, CONFIRMED BY NICHOLAS II (1897)

463. On the introduction, in the schools of settler-proprietors (former foreign colonists), of instruction in the Russian language.

His Imperial Majesty was Highest pleased to confirm the opinion rendered in the General Assembly of the State Council on the introduction, in the schools of settler-proprietors (former foreign colonists), of instruction in the Russian language, and commanded that it be carried into effect.

Signed: Chairman of the State Council, MIKHAIL.

OPINION OF THE STATE COUNCIL. 24 February 1897.

Extracted from the journals of the Joint Departments of Laws, State Economy, and Civil and Ecclesiastical Affairs of 28 December 1896 and of the General Assembly of 10 February 1897.

The State Council, in the Joint Departments of Laws, State Economy, and Civil and Ecclesiastical Affairs and in the General Assembly, having considered the submission of the Minister of Public Education on granting to the Ministry entrusted to him the right to introduce Russian as the language of instruction in schools established for settler-proprietors (former foreign colonists), resolved in its opinion:

To grant to the Minister of Public Education the authority, as far as possible, gradually to introduce in the schools of settler-proprietors (former foreign colonists) instruction in the Russian language, provided, however, that the pupils' native language and the Law of God of their confession be taught in their native language, with the number of lessons necessary for the proper mastery of these subjects.

The authentic opinion is signed in the journals by the Chairmen and Members.

*SURP. 1897. 1st half. No. 32. P. 1002.*

**Compiler's endnote (Примечание):**

### No. 160.

This act, too, may be considered typical of Nicholas II. The authorities of course knew that the former colonist schools were plainly unprepared for the transition to instruction in Russian. The colonies had only about a dozen central schools that trained teachers to teach in that language, and a significant proportion of such schools were found in the Mennonite colonies. Besides this, colonist teachers for the Russian schools were trained only at Russian teachers' seminaries, and even there in minuscule numbers. Moreover, many graduates of these institutions preferred not to work in schools at all, taking positions instead as clerks, minor officials, and the like. Nor were the pupils themselves prepared for Russian-language instruction, since outside school they communicated, as a rule, in their native language. On the other hand, the voices of nationalist circles, indignant over the "separateness of the German colonies from Russia," were growing ever louder in the country. So Nicholas made a Solomonic decision: instruction in Russian was to be introduced, but "as far as possible, gradually." At the same time the emperor reassured German religious circles, giving them his word that the number of hours allotted to teaching the Law of God in German would be sufficient "for the proper mastery" of the subject.

Alexander III had acted far more decisively in such cases. Thus, in the southwestern governorates, a system for training teachers for the German schools had been practically nonexistent at all, yet this did not in the least prevent the emperor from introducing compulsory instruction in Russian there fully 10 years before the present act, in 1887 (see: Kostiuk M., Neutatz D. Op. cit. P. 398).
