> **Note.** Memoir by Allegra (Isaak) McBirney — the Isaak (paternal) line. *"My grandfather"* = **David Isaak**; *"my father"* = **Albert Isaak**; *"his mother"* = **Hannah K. F. Hetzler**; *"my mother"* = **Eva Roesch**.

## This is a photo of my Grandfather Isaak's general store in Eureka, South Dakota around 1890.

In the photo my grandfather is on the far left toward the front. (He looks exactly like my dad!) You can imagine how many needs were met by the array of merchandise carried in that store! (as typified by the horse-collar, iron stoves, baby-buggy with a vertical parasol attached, stacks of drawers of hardware, etc., etc.!)

Here was a store—in the middle of a vast prairie--that met the needs of countless German immigrants who were "homesteading" there, i.e. receiving land from the U.S. government in exchange for settling on it and farming it under a long-term commitment.

My father was a very little boy when this photo was taken. He spoke both English and German. His mother was a quiet, artistic lady. She was the daughter of an immigrant farmer who <u>volunteered</u> to fight in the Civil War, because of his deep desire to preserve unity in his new country which he loved so much. In one of the war's many serious battles he was shot--but the New Testament carried in his shirt pocket stopped the bullet and saved his life! Understandably, after the war he became a preacher rather than a farmer!--and with heart-felt gratitude to the Lord who had spared his life, he founded several evangelical churches in which on a circuit-riding basis he preached God's Word.

This was the spiritual heritage of my dad's mother...who nurtured him in the Scriptures, and also gave him a love for art and music. (He studied classical music, and played the violin and also the clarinet very well.)

Work-wise, my father as a teenager helped in the family's general store; and also as a teenager taught school! This early start came about because there <u>were</u> no other teachers...and the many German immigrants in the area needed to learn English, as well as other subjects. (In teaching English he would begin by bringing to the classroom a box full of all kinds of everyday items—screws, a toothbrush, towel, hammer, ink, kettle, button, a shovel, an egg, etc.—and then teach the class the English word for each item...and little by little add verbs and phrases...)

These years were spent learning business skills from his father, and artistic skills from his mother; but his life was abruptly changed when he was suddenly called into the military to serve in World War I. (I still have a loving letter which his mother sent to him in Europe, urging him to read Psalm 91 and cling to the Lord.) Because he was an excellent sharp-shooter, he served as a machine-gunner—against the Germans.... Fighting against men of his own German background was very difficult for him. (Once when he was on guard duty at his American camp, a spy sneaked in, apparently to set off explosives. My dad discovered him, and had been ordered to kill spies on sight—but when the man began begging in <u>German</u> to be spared, my dad just couldn't bring himself to kill someone who before the war had been not an enemy, but a "brother." And so he broke orders, and simply commanded the man sharply—in German—to get out of the camp as fast as he could, and never return.)

(My dad had an undeniably tender heart...as evidenced here. I discovered his tenderness myself as a little girl many times...for instance, the day that my canary died... My big, silent daddy <u>cried</u> with me that day in my heartbreak....)

During a severe battle in World War I my dad was seriously hurt by poison gas in an enemy attack. And while in the army hospital he received word that his father had died of a heart attack.

So, when the war finally ended in 1918 he returned home to the heavy responsibility of managing the store, and caring for his widowed mother and his sister.

He met my mother, who worked in a bank in Eureka, and they were married the following year...on November 11 (originally called Armistice Day, now Veterans' Day) the commemoration of the end of the war. (My dad remained strongly patriotic, deeply in love with his country—standing straight and tall, and always saluting in the presence of the flag, even in his elder years...)

In preparation for marriage he had built an apartment above the old General Store, and enlarged the store to accommodate additional types of merchandise--clothing, shoes, household linens, and stationery items—and I grew up in that apartment over the store.

A dear middle-aged German immigrant woman who moved to our town cared for me there during the day while my mother worked downstairs in the store. And I can remember that although I was only a pre-schooler, I helped teach that dear woman English!—and we laughed often over her struggle with pronunciations! And in exchange for my helping teach her English, she taught me at the age of 5 to sew! Oh, how we chatted together, day after day—in our funny mixture of languages!--while she mended my dresses, or washed my hair, or served me goat's milk and homemade bread.

And she'd hover over me like an angel during my life-threatening sessions with whooping cough and a serious blood infection.

But when I was 7 all of a sudden--without giving me notice--she left my life, and moved far, far away to marry a German widower. And with her departure--my world crashed. My grief was so overwhelming that no one could comfort me. Her leaving seemed somehow worse than death because she was still alive, but we were not together...and never would be again....

Our family's move to Iowa when I was 8 helped a little...but only a little. There my parents started a new business—not a general store, but a very, <u>very</u> up-to-the-moment women's clothing store—and that began a new era in our lives. My dad—with a strong artistic sense (and always wanting to tread new ground and do things that others hadn't thought of...) (a trait which I seem to have inherited...) hired a top-ranking professional architect to design and build a store that would surpass in class anything that even New York City could offer! And I really believe it did! It was state-of-the-art: a completely impressive futuristic combination of elegant chrome and glass fixtures, flattering rose-tinted mirrors, lighted shadow-boxes, imported woods, rich fabrics—and a totally unconventional design. (As I recall, upon completion it was even featured in a national magazine.)

And with the creation of the APPAREL ART store came other changes. For instance, we lived in a real house for the first time--a mile from the store...not on top of it.

Our customers were demanding.
They didn't wear muddy boots.

And nobody spoke German....

<br>

I was a clerk there as a teenager, and also designed the window displays and put together the outfits for the mannequins (and on the side wrote music and was church organist.)

My parents worked <u>very</u> hard, literally day and night. And surely part of their motivation was to provide for my going to a university, which had never been possible for them. My choice was Stanford, where I could major in the love of my life: <u>writing</u>.

(My dad never had the opportunity to go beyond 3rd grade, actually, simply because there <u>were</u> no more grades than that when he was a little boy. The rest of his schooling was learned by living and attempting.)

The APPAREL ART store brought our family a new way of life—but it left behind decades of memories of a general store that had a major part in all of our lives....

I wish we could have shown the beautiful Apparel Art store to my dad's father...

or do I?

Very likely his general store of 1890--
with its kerosene lamps hanging from the ceiling, and with its parasols and iron stoves and kegs of nails arriving on the weekly train from Minneapolis--
was even more exciting to him.

Yes, it's a very long way back to that old general store...
Lots of transactions have taken place between then and now--
not just financial ones, but life-transactions....
and spiritual transactions,
including the one I experienced when I transferred my trust from
"religiousness" to Christ Himself...
exchanging my self-effort and sin for His sin-payment and Righteousness....
and getting a whole new life in the trade!
(I can hardly wait to tell my great-grandfather all about that!—
the one whose New Testament in his pocket kept him from getting killed in the Civil War!
He will understand about that "New Life"—
and about my urgency to tell others about Jesus,
because he had that urgency, too....)

Yes, it's a very long way back to that General Store of the 1890's,
and even to the updated one of the 1920's and 30's when I was a little girl....

And, no, I wouldn't want to go back....
...and yet, at times, in my thoughts I peek in on my mother and daddy working hard in that store;
and
I sneak up to that little apartment above it,
and replay chatting with my immigrant angel in a mixture of English and German!----
while I struggle to thread my first needle,
and wonder what will happen tomorrow....
