# The Budschak around 1860

*Erwin Heer*

While studying the literature for the "*Eichendorf Chronicle*", Senior Teacher Hugo Häfner came across a very informative nature description of the Budschak [also Budjak] around 1860. He was so kind as to send me an excerpt of it, insofar as it concerns the nature of the Budschak. For that, I thank him most sincerely at this point.

This nature description of the Budschak is found in the book "Materials on the History and Statistics of the Church and School System for the Evangelical Lutheran Communities in Russia (Collected and Published on behalf of the Central Committee of the Support Fund for Evangelical Lutheran Communities in Russia) by E.H. Busch, Russian Imperial Collegiate Councilor. St. Petersburg, 1862, Commission Publisher G. Haessel in Leipzig; on commission by Dörffling & Franke."

From pages 134 to 137, the following excerpts regarding the description of the country of Bessarabia, namely the Budschak, the southern Bessarabian steppe, are found:

> "In the valleys, there is lush vegetation even when the plains and the hills have long lost their green. When everything is in splendor and bloom, there is no more beautiful sight than that offered by the Bessarabian Steppe. There one sees near the colonies lovely vineyards with their dark green; further away, the fields worked with German diligence, pleasing to look at, and then the magnificent meadows with their hollyhocks (Stockrosen), wildflowers, and fragrant herbs (Kräutern) . . .
>
> <!-- source_page: 2 -->
> There is a complete absence of forest in southern Bessarabia. This is also considered the main reason for the persistent summer drought. In the German colonies, small forest plantations are found everywhere; they are tended to and cared for, and yet the trees die out by the dozens. The tree that thrives best with some care is the lovely American acacia (Akazie) with white, fragrant blossoms. It is therefore very common here; it adorns the front yards of houses, the courtyards, and gardens; it also makes up the principle supply of the artificially created forests . . .
>
> But then it also depends on being restrained in the enjoyment of the wonderful fruits. One can calmly enjoy the watermelon (Arbuse) and praise God for the refreshment he grants through this fruit, especially in the hottest time. But one must be more careful with the melon. Watermelons and melons grown in large gardens, the so-called "Baschtans", are exceptionally cheap. About 120 kinds of the finest varieties are paid for with 1 ruble . . .
>
> Overall, there is no shortage of all kinds of fruits, such as peaches (Pfirsichen), apricots (Aprikosen), quinces (Quitten), and so forth, not to mention the juicy apples (Äpfel) and pears (Birnen), of which Bessarabia is rich. On the other hand, there are few or no berries (Beeren); however, they are completely compensated for by the magnificent grapes (Trauben), which are present in large quantities and in the most diverse varieties. On average, every German farmer has 3,000 vines, which yield a fair quantity of wine and also provide the opportunity to consume something similar to grapes. In this, they are certainly not lazy; what can be eaten is eaten, and every year, around the end of September, when the grape harvest or the so-called "Herbst" takes place, they undergo a complete grape cure . . .
>
> Yet there are also, here as everywhere, things that are less pleasant. This includes the countless snakes (Schlangen), lizards (Eidechsen), and toads (Kröten) that are found everywhere in the fields, in the garden, in the vineyard, in the yard, in the cellar, and sometimes even in the house and rooms. The snakes found here are not poisonous, but their bite can still cause dangerous incidents, and their appearance is always terrifying, even to the one who, in a certain sense, has become accustomed to them over time.
>
> Often the large migratory locusts (Heuschrecken) also sweep through the country and eat everything that pleases them; hailstorms often cause great damage; yes, from time to time earthquakes are also felt, which, however, bring more fear than destruction in their consequences.
>
> There are almost no wild animals; in the man-high weeds (Unkraute), which cover the slopes of the hills and are called *Burian*, where small steppe wolves occasionally dwell, which cause some damage among the flocks of sheep; however, they are killed in large numbers through regularly recurring driven hunts. On the other hand, the steppe swarms with the small, eligant [sic] ground squirrel mice (Zieselmäusen), called hares (Erdhasen), which cheerfully go about their business in the sunshine. Not infrequently, one also encounters mighty vultures (Geier), which feast on a dead animal.
>
> <!-- source_page: 3 -->
> As passing guests, one sometimes sees thousands of pelicans (Pelikanen) on the riverbanks in May, who here engage in fishing without invitation. Their skin is tanned and provides material for waterproof garments.
>
> Bustards (Trappen) wander in large flocks grazing on the steppe; lapwings (Kiebitze) gather by the thousands along the way; above all, however, it is the stork (Storch) that fills the villages with its leisurely clattering and returns to its native nest every year. Often 2 or 3 pairs nest on one roof.
>
> There is no lack of well-being. Whoever has a heart for it will find something here in the country that pleasantly touches them. That is why, on the whole, Germans feel very comfortable here."

So far concerning the esteemed author of this nature description of the Budschak, the "Russian Imperial Collegiate Councilor" E. H. Busch. His descriptions reveal great intelligence, a solid knowledge for that time, including in the field of natural science—furthermore, a clear, concise style. All respect for such observation of nature! At the same time, the description of the Budschak was probably made only incidentally, as the heading clearly indicates! (After all, it is primarily about church and school matters).

And now to the individual questions, which practically invite comparison, namely a comparison with the Resettlement year of 1940. That is 80 years later, an 80-year difference! In these 80 years, a lot has changed, especially in the field of zoology. But the original steppe has also undergone significant changes during this time and has been transformed into a cultural steppe.

## a) The Plant World around 1860 and around 1940:

Wild-growing plants are only generally slightly touched on, such as the "hollyhocks, field flowers, and aromatic herbs". — In contrast, the author deals with cultivated plants in detail: The watermelons and melons are mentioned first, with the note that one can enjoy watermelons calmly, but should be cautious with melons. In general, both fruits were enjoyed in our region, with watermelons, however, being consumed to a much greater extent than melons. The author rightly points out: "One can enjoy the watermelon calmly and praise God for the refreshment it provides, especially granted in the hottest time through this fruit." Peaches, apricots, and quinces are particularly emphasized, not to mention apples and pears. Among them, the apricot was probably the most precious fruit. There were few or no berries back then; well, around 1940 it was probably not much different. We did not need them anyway, especially since grapes—as already in 1860—were also abundantly available in the finest varieties in 1940.

At that time, small forest plantations in the German colonies were tended and cared for. Otherwise, the steppe was completely treeless. The most suitable tree for the steppe was the hardy, undemanding acacia tree; it was also the most common. — It was the same in 1940—after 80 years! Only one tree was widely promoted and sometimes planted on a larger scale around 1940: the walnut tree (Nußbaum); partly because of its valuable wood, partly because it bore abundant fruit in old age. And the author does not mention it for 1860.

## b) The Wildlife around 1860 and around 1940:

So far concerning the vegetation of the Budschak around 1860. Let us now turn to the animal world: The author begins with the reptiles (Kriechtieren), the snakes and lizards, and continues with the amphibians—the toads. In doing so, he speaks of things that are "less pleasant". These animals were then present in huge numbers, both in the fields, gardens, and vineyards, as well as in the yard and cellar, yes, "sometimes even in the house and rooms". To the credit of the author, it must be said that he clearly emphasizes: '"The snakes found here are indeed not poisonous…" [sic] Surely, among the harmless grass snakes there were also "biting" species; however, these animals were by no means dangerous. But at that time, the reptiles must have lived in almost unimaginable quantities. I have already reported extensively on reptiles and amphibians (Literature 2).

Locusts still "often roamed the land and devoured everything" around 1860. By 1940, locusts were only rare visitors to the Budschak. I still remember the last major invasion in 1929-1920 [sic] in Teplitz. With the end of the 19th century, the large mass invasions slowly decreased and almost completely stopped in the 20th century.

There were already few wild game mammals (Säugetiere) around 1860. The author probably meant by "wild animals" the larger game mammals such as the wolf, fox (Fuchs), and others. (The Saiga antelope had already long been extinct). About the wolf, he writes word for word: "In the waist-high weeds that cover the hill slopes and are called Burian here, the small steppe wolves sometimes dwell . . ." In my opinion, this is probably the most important piece of information in this report. Accordingly, around 1860, a small species of wolf still lived in the Budschak, which no longer existed by 1940. Of course, there were still wolves in the Budschak around 1940—certainly even today, that is, around 1970!—but that is a different species of wolf; perhaps it migrated from the Moldavian Carpathians across the Pruth [river] into the Budschak. Be that as it may, the small steppe wolf that was still found in Bessarabia around 1860 must have been exterminated in the 19th century. The author also mentions that these small steppe wolves were "killed in large numbers through regularly recurring driven hunts". They were already heavily pursued at that time, which may have led to the extinction of this wolf species. My view was reinforced by the information of the Odessa zoologist A. A. Brauner; he writes word for word: "The steppe wolves have also been exterminated. Certainly, they had to be destroyed, but the extermination could proceed in parallel with the research. It is possible that the steppe wolf was similar to the greyhound-like (*windhundartigen*) Transcaspian wolf, whose necessary study the researcher Jeittels had already pointed out to the Russians in 1877." (Lit. 1). Too bad that science was not advanced enough at the time! Otherwise, we would know today exactly what the "little steppe wolf" looked like back then and whether it indeed belonged to a special subspecies.

The author mentions—as could not be expected otherwise!—a single rodent: the "Erdhasen"! Even in 1940, it was probably not much rarer than in 1860! Also in 1940, certain steppes of the Budschak "proliferated" with "ground hares", as I have repeatedly observed. (Lit. 3). The author overlooks the European hare (Feldhasen), although it provided good roast hare in the autumn.

If we now turn to the birds, the author first attracts attention to the "Mighty Vultures". Here we are dealing with both the Griffon Vulture (Gänsegeier) and the Cinereous Vulture (Kuttengeier), <!-- source_page: 5 --> which had already had to be referred to as rarities in 1940. Of course, in 1860 both species were not uncommon, with the Griffon Vulture still being a quite frequent scavenger in the southern Bessarabian steppe, which could not yet be considered rare by around 1910. Until then, there were still large pastures with equally large herds of cattle and correspondingly much carrion. (Lit. 11).

It is also quite possible that even the very rare, smaller vulture at that time could be found in the steppe as a visitor to animal carcasses. (Lit. 11). — I was particularly surprised by the report on the masses of migrating pelicans: "As migrating guests, thousands of pelicans can sometimes be seen on the riverbanks in May . . ." That is entirely true; because in the south-western neighboring Danube Delta, an ornithologist estimated the population of the Dalmatian pelican (Krauskopfpelikans) in 1867 and 1868 alone at several million; the rosy pelican (Rosepelikan) was also not much rarer at that time. It is also revealing that their skin was tanned and the material was used for waterproof clothing. (See in detail in Lit. 4.) — Now, how was it around 1940? At that time, the pelican as a migratory bird was a very rare sight; I recorded and published the last reports in the Budschak, as far as they became known to me (Lit. 4).

The bustards roamed the steppe in large flocks (Herden) around 1860. Here, the author means the great bustard (*Otis tarda*), because he speaks of flocks! At that time, the great bustard was certainly still a more frequent breeding bird. But by 1940, its population had shrunk significantly, so that vigorous protection for the preservation of this proud, typical steppe bird became necessary. (See detailed information in Lit. 5!).

The lapwing is mentioned. Of course, back then lapwings were very common; indeed, even in 1940 they were still not a rarity, rather still frequently present—but not, as around 1860—"by the thousands along the path," but only to be observed in the hundreds.

I consider the stork-note, along with the wolf-note, to be the most important. Around 1860, friend Adebar [the bearer of good luck] was quite frequent and a popular village bird—"which filled the areas with pleasant clattering." It was similar in 1940, only that the white stork was then rather in decline, which I have justified in detail (Lit. 6 and Lit. 7). That around 1860 the stork "often nested in 2 and 3 pairs on a roof", I find to be a brilliant—albeit belated—confirmation of our statements. In Lit. 7, namely, I wrote word for word: "Nest numbers on each roof. As a rule, one nest, not infrequently two; and according to I. Radke (Brienne) and G. Scheller (Beresina), there used to be as many as three nests on a roof, with the latter noting that two were on the gable sides and one nest was on the chimney . . ." Around 1860, there were only thatched roofs, not yet tile roofs, which presumably appeared at the earliest in 1906, that is, at the beginning of the 20th century. The stork is, so to speak, an "old-fashioned" bird, for which the old-fashioned thatched roofs were more appealing than the modern, smooth tile roofs. So, around 1860, there were still many roofs with 2 or 3 nests. The stork also was probably more common back then than around 1940.

One notices that the author seemed to be more knowledgeable in the field of zoology, especially ornithology, than in botany. He names the animals individually (steppe wolf, European hares, pelicans, bustards, lapwings, white stork); regarding plants, he speaks generally of "strawflowers, field flowers, rooted herbs". However, this does not detract from the whole in <!-- source_page: 6 --> any way, especially since in our case the animal world is of more interest than the plant world. We can only be grateful to the author for leaving us so much about the nature of the Budschak.

## c) General:

Here, the primary interest lies in the names of animals and plants. The estimated author mentions the "Arbuse", the "Baschtan", the "Burian" from the plant world; the "Erdhasen" or the "Zieselmaus" and the "Steppenwolf" from the animal world. (See Lit. 8 and 9!). — From this, we can see that as early as around 1860, these names had already entered the vocabulary of the German colonists in the Budschak (and throughout the entire Black Sea region). However, I am certainly not mistaken in claiming: These names were already incorporated into the vocabulary of the colonists during the colonization from 1814 to 1830. The moment they saw and got to know the plants and animals that were unknown to them, and therefore new, the need to give them a name was also present. In this way and only in this way—and right at the beginning of the colonization—did the new animal and plant names enter the vocabulary of the German colonists on the Black Sea.

Finally, one more name should be mentioned: "The farmer" (*Der Bauernwirth*). With us, the farmer was simply called "*Wirt*", because upon settlement he had received an estate of land—a whole 60 *Deßjatinen*. The local hired workers addressed their farmer as "Lord Farmer" (*Herr Wirt*) (= "*gospodin chosjein*"). So the term "*Wirt*" for a farmer (= colonist) originates from the time of settlement and has nothing at all to do with the actual host who was the owner of an inn.

Furthermore, the author mentions hailstorms and earthquakes. These two natural events also afflicted our colonists up to 1940 in the same way that the author reports them for 1860: "Hailstorms often cause great damage, and once in a while, earth tremors are also felt, which, however, bring more fear than destruction." This is exactly how I know hailstorms and earthquakes from my life in the Budschak up to 1940. Here, everything remained the same—unlike in the animal and plant world, where humans could intervene and change things at will.

Finally, one more thought: The author writes at the end: "...Therefore it is also the case that the Germans feel very comfortable here overall." — This has to do with the prosperity of the German colonists around 1860. Let us compare 1840 with 1940 [sic]: Around 1840, the German colonists of Bessarabia had not yet recovered from settlement; they had to pay the state their debts from the settlement period, so at that time they were still poorer than, for example, the neighboring Bulgarians. (See Lit. 10!). Twenty years later, around 1860, their situation was already much better: "Therefore it is also the case that the Germans feel very comfortable here overall." — By around 1860, they had fully recovered from their debts to the state and had completely settled in their new homeland—so to speak, acclimatized. — However, they probably experienced the most beautiful times at the end of the 19th century. Ferdinand Wagner writes about it word for word: "In the 1880s, there were consistently good harvests. This decade is probably the most blessed time in the life of the Germans in Bessarabia." (Lit. 12). If we also add that at that time there were neither revolutionary years nor wars—which in the 20th century occurred in abundance: 1905 and 1917 two revolutions, 1914 and 1939 two world wars!—then one also comes to the conclusion that the 1880s were in every respect the most blessed time in the life of the Germans in Bessarabia.
