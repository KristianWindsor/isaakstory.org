## Chapter Seventeen

# The Children's Hour

Eureka, South Dakota — 1919 to 1928

---

The Statue of Liberty was growing.

Albert stood at the rail of the USS *Pueblo* with his pack at his feet and his hat in his hand. Seven months ago he had watched her shrink. Now she was coming up out of the January harbor at the speed of a ship coming home, and the buildings of lower Manhattan were rising up behind her — the same tall buildings his father had seen at seventeen from the deck of the *Mosel* in 1878.

The buildings were taller now. They were taller every year.

Around him the men on the deck were shouting and crying and holding their hats over their hearts. Albert was not shouting. His lungs were not good enough to shout. He watched the buildings come up and thought about his father at seventeen standing in this harbor without the statue in front of it, and about the winter evening in 1897 when David had said *it was very tall, the city. I did not know a country could have buildings like that.*

He had crossed the Atlantic twice now, and his father was dead.

---

The Eureka spur train came in at three o'clock on a cold blue afternoon at the end of February. Albert stepped down onto the platform with his pack on his shoulder.

The main street had not changed. The Schmidt brothers' bakery still had the sign in the window, painted wood now: PLEASE SPEAK ENGLISH IN THIS STORE. He walked the four blocks with his coat buttoned to the throat. His lungs made the small wet cough every thirty steps.

Hanna was at the top of the wooden stairs.

She had been waiting there since morning. When she saw him at the corner she did not call out and she did not move. She watched him climb. He was slower than he had been when he left. She put both her hands on his face and looked at him for a long time.

"You came home."

"I came home, *Mutter*."

She pulled him inside.

The kitchen smelled of coffee and of bread and of the small close smell of a room that had been kept warm for two days. The stove was the same stove. The table was the same table. At the head of the table was David's chair, still pulled in at the same angle, empty.

Albert stopped in the doorway.

"I did not move it," Hanna said.

"No."

"I sit at my end in the morning and I look at it. That is how I have my coffee."

She poured him a cup. He sat down in his old place. He did not look at the chair at the head of the table because he was going to have to learn to look at it gradually, and this was not the afternoon for it. The coffee was bitter and hot and the same coffee his mother had made his whole life.

After a while Hanna said: "He said your name at the end. Twice. He said to tell you he was not afraid."

Albert held the cup with both hands.

"Anything else."

"He said he loved you. He said it in German. It was the last thing he said."

Albert looked down into the coffee. Outside the window the prairie wind was blowing the way it had always blown. The small ticking of the wood stove cooling at the end of its morning fire was the only sound in the kitchen.

The door at the bottom of the stairs opened. Boots on the wooden steps. Ed came up into the kitchen with his apron on and his sleeves rolled up and stopped in the doorway.

"Albert."

"Edmund."

Ed crossed the kitchen in three steps and pulled him up out of the chair, and the hug was the hug of a brother who had stayed and a brother who had gone, and it lasted a long time. When Ed let go his eyes moved to Albert's chest and then back to his face.

"Your lungs."

"Not good."

"They'll get better."

"Maybe."

Ed sat down at his old place. Hanna poured him a cup. The three of them drank the coffee while the afternoon lengthened toward evening, and the fourth chair at the head of the table stayed where it was.

---

The Rexall smelled of peppermint and of alcohol and of the small dry smell of paper from the prescription pad on the counter. Hank was behind the register.

"Albert."

"Hank."

"You came in."

"I came in."

Hank stepped out from behind the counter and looked at his cousin with the small careful look he had been giving him since February. Then he smiled, because Hank could not help smiling, and he clapped Albert on the shoulder and steered him toward the two wooden chairs by the window where the men of Eureka sat to drink the free coffee Hank had been giving away since 1915.

"Sleigh ride Saturday night. The old crowd. Walter and Edwin and Otto, and some of the girls, and the new girl from the bank. You should come."

"I do not have a partner."

Hank looked at him.

"Go ask the new girl."

"I do not know the new girl."

"Her name is Eva Roesch. She works at the second window. She has been out with us twice already and she does not talk much and she does not flinch. Go ask her."

"Hank."

"Albert. Go ask her. Today. Now. The bank closes at four."

Albert looked at the clock above the prescription counter. It was quarter to four. He looked at Hank. Hank was Hank — the round friendly face, the cornet-player's eyes, the small certainty of a man who had been pushing his cousins toward things their whole lives. Albert put his coffee cup down on the window sill.

"All right."

"Good boy. Wear your hat."

---

The bank smelled of ink and of cold marble and of the faint cigar smoke from the manager's office in the back. Two windows open. An old farmer at the first, counting bills into a pile. Albert took his hat off and got into line at the second.

He had not been in the bank since 1917. He had not asked a woman anything in two and a half years. His heart was doing a thing it had not done since the Argonne.

She was small. She came up to his collarbone. Dark hair pinned up in a small neat knot under the lamplight of the teller cage, grey-blue eyes, a white blouse with a dark collar, hands folded on the marble counter in the still practiced way of a woman who had been counting money all afternoon.

"Good afternoon, sir."

"Miss Roesch."

She looked up at him. Her face did not change.

"Mr. Isaak."

"You know me."

"I have been hearing about you for a year. Mr. Isaak, what can I do for you at the bank."

"Hank sent me. Hank Isaak. He said there is a sleigh ride on Saturday night and that the old crowd is going and that you are going, and he said —" Albert stopped. He had not rehearsed the rest of it. "He said I should ask if you would go with me."

The farmer at the first window was still counting bills. The bank clock on the wall behind her said three-fifty-two. Eva looked at Albert across the marble counter for the length of one long breath.

"Yes, Mr. Isaak."

"Yes?"

"I will go with you. Pick me up at the boarding house on Cedar Street at six. It is the white one with the blue shutters." She slid a deposit slip across the counter toward him without looking down at it, and her mouth did the smallest thing at one corner — not a smile, but the ghost of one. "Was there anything else for the bank today, Mr. Isaak."

"No, Miss Roesch."

"Saturday at six."

"Saturday at six."

He put his hat back on and walked out into the cold afternoon with his hands in his coat pockets and the first not-bad feeling he had had in fifteen months sitting somewhere just behind his sternum.

---

The sleighs left the church at six-thirty. There were three of them, with bells on the harness and lanterns swinging from the front rails and buffalo robes piled high in the beds. The moon was nearly full and the snow was the dry powder snow that creaks under the runners.

Hank put them in the second sleigh. Right-hand side. Buffalo robe over their laps.

For the first mile neither of them said anything. The bells of the harness and the creak of the runners and the long slow breathing of the horses were the only sounds. Eva's shoulder was warm against his through three layers of wool. Her knitted hat had a small white pompom that trembled in the wind, and her face in the lantern light was turned to the road ahead.

Albert coughed. The small wet cough. He could not stop it.

Eva did not turn her head.

"Mr. Isaak."

"Yes."

"Hank told me about the violin."

Albert looked at her in the lantern light.

"Hank talks too much."

"Hank loves you. I would like to hear it sometime."

The wind came across the prairie from the south and the moon made the snow blue. The second sleigh went up the road behind the first at a slow trot, and Eva was quiet for a long time under the robe, and Albert was quiet beside her, and the only sound was the bells.

On the way back she fell asleep on his shoulder.

He held very still. The moon had moved halfway across the sky and the horses had slowed and the other girls in the other sleighs had started singing a hymn he half-remembered from church, and Eva's dark hair was soft against his jaw under the edge of the knitted hat, and Albert looked up at the stars and understood that this was the woman.

When they reached the church she lifted her head and rubbed her eyes and looked at him.

"Mr. Isaak."

"Yes."

"I had a nice time."

He walked her to the white boarding house with the blue shutters on Cedar Street. At the door he held out his hand to say goodnight. She shook it the way she had shaken hands at the bank. Then she went inside.

He asked her to marry him on the first of August.

She said yes before he had finished the question.

---

They were married on the eleventh of November, 1919. The first anniversary of the armistice. Albert chose the date and did not explain why.

Eva wore a dark blue wool dress with a high lace collar and small pearl buttons her grandmother had sewn on in the boarding house over two months. Her veil had been her grandmother's. The church was full.

Albert wore his Sunday suit. The small Bible from St. Louis was in the inside pocket of his coat.

Hanna sat in the front pew on the groom's side in the same dark blue dress she had worn to David's funeral. Beside her on her right hand was an empty space about the width of a man's shoulders. Hanna had insisted. For the whole of the ceremony her right hand rested on the polished wood of the bench in that empty space.

In the middle of the vows Albert glanced across. Her hand was on the wood. She was looking at him. The look between them was the look of two people who had been carrying David between them for two years. Then Albert turned back to Eva and said *I will*, and the ceremony continued.

Hank played the cornet for the recessional. *Nun danket alle Gott.* Now thank we all our God. He had been telling Albert he was going to play the cornet at his wedding since the morning of the fifth of June, 1917, and two and a half years later, in the choir loft of the Lutheran church in Eureka, he kept it. His eyes were wet by the end of the hymn.

---

They lived at Hanna's house that first winter. Eva took Josie's old room and made it theirs, and in the evenings she helped Hanna with the supper dishes, and in the mornings she went down to the store to help Ed on the floor while Albert did the ledgers in the back office. The store was already doing better than it had done before the war. The wheat prices were climbing. Men came in with money in their pockets and wanted things they had never wanted before.

By spring Albert had started the expansion.

He bought the lot next door from the widow Mueller for eight hundred dollars and hired two carpenters from Aberdeen and knocked out the east wall of the store. The new half went up in six weeks. When it was finished the store was twice as wide and had new shelves that ran from floor to ceiling, and Albert stocked the new shelves with the things he had seen selling in the Aberdeen department stores — ready-made dresses from a catalog house in Chicago, men's shoes in cardboard boxes, linens and towels in neat stacks, bottles of ink and fountain pens and writing paper in small glass cases. The sign on the front of the building was repainted. ISAAKS VARIETY STORE, it said now, in dark green letters with gold trim.

Above the expanded store, on the second floor, Albert built the apartment.

He built it for Eva. Three rooms — a kitchen, a small sitting room, a bedroom — with electric light from the town line Eureka had put in the year before and a new linoleum floor in the kitchen in a pattern of blue and white squares and a small porcelain stove by the window where Eva set the pot of red geraniums. They moved in on the first of September, 1920. Eva unpacked her clothes into the new wardrobe and hung the geraniums in the window and stood in the kitchen looking at her blue and white linoleum, and Albert came up the inside stairs from the store that evening with a new cash register on his back because the old cash register had finally given up and the new cash register was electric and rang a bell when the drawer opened, and Eva laughed for the first time in Albert's hearing in their own apartment above their own store, and that was the beginning.

---

Gottlieb died in Tripp on the twentieth of January, 1921. Eighty-six years old. Albert went to the funeral with Eva. They lowered the old man into the frozen ground beside Louisa.

Four months later, on the eighth of May, Alice Estelle Isaak was born in the apartment above the store with the doctor from Aberdeen and Hanna and Eva's grandmother in the bedroom with her. She was a loud red baby with a great quantity of dark hair. Hanna held her first granddaughter and cried for the husband who had not lived to hold her.

The Twenties came to Eureka the way they came to the rest of the country. Cars appeared on the main street. The first was the doctor's Model T. The second was the new one Otto Wenzel bought to show off at church. By 1924 there were twenty cars in Eureka and the men at the grain elevator were talking about paving the main street. The radio reached the Isaak apartment in 1925. The candy jars in the store were full. The ready-made dresses from Chicago sold out in two weeks every time Albert restocked them. Eva ran the counter in the mornings with Alice on her hip, and customers who came in for thread stayed to buy a hat and a pair of stockings and a tin of talcum powder and a small framed print of a field of poppies, and the till rang and rang and Albert in the back office kept the ledgers and watched the numbers climb.

On winter evenings, when the store was closed and Alice was asleep, Albert went into the small back bedroom of the apartment and took out the violin and played for half an hour with the door closed. Eva knew. Eva did not go in. The violin was for his father, and his father was in that room when Albert played, and Eva left him to the playing and closed her own book in the sitting room and listened through the wall.

Edith Allegra was born in a hospital in Aberdeen on the second of July, 1927, at ten-past-four in the morning, in a delivery room with three electric lamps and a new white-enameled metal bassinet.

She had blonde hair. Fine and golden and the color of wheat in August. Albert looked down at her in the bassinet and thought of the poem he had read at nine in the Eureka schoolhouse — *grave Alice, and laughing Allegra, and Edith with golden hair* — and he turned to Eva in the hospital bed and said *both of them*.

Eva said *both*.

Edith Allegra Isaak.

The doctor told them there would not be a third. The second pregnancy had taken too much. Albert had nodded at the doctor and had not argued because Eva was alive and Eva was the thing that mattered, and on the drive back to Eureka the next week with Edith in a wicker basket in the back seat Albert had not mentioned the poem or the third daughter that was not going to come, and Eva had looked over at him at the wheel and had known he was not mentioning it and had loved him for the not-mentioning.

Tante Frieda came a month later. A young woman from a German-Russian family south of town, twenty-three, plain-faced and patient, the kind of woman children imprinted on. Eva hired her because the store was busy and Hanna was sixty-five and the apartment above the store needed someone to watch Edith while Eva worked the counter. Tante Frieda spoke German to the girls. Alice tolerated her. Edith adored her. The first word Edith said after *Mama* was *Tante*.

---

The summer Edith was a year old, they drove south to the Roesch farm.

Albert drove the Model T with both hands on the wheel and the wheat going past on either side of the road — high and gold and almost ready for harvest, the flat blue August sky above it the cloudless sky that only the Dakotas have in August. Alice in the front seat between her parents. Edith in the wicker basket in the back. The drive took most of the morning.

Opa Roesch was in the yard when they pulled up. He had a lamb in his arms.

It was a black-faced lamb, two weeks old, the runt of a late ewe. The mother had rejected it and Opa Roesch had been bottle-feeding it since the morning of its birth and the lamb had imprinted on him and cried when he set it down, and so he had been carrying it around the farm in the crook of his left arm for two weeks like a baby. The lamb looked up at him with wet black eyes.

Oma Roesch came out of the house still in her apron. She went straight to the basket in the back seat without stopping to greet Albert and said *gib sie mir* — give her to me — and Eva lifted Edith out and Oma Roesch held her against her shoulder and put her face into Edith's blonde hair and breathed in.

Opa Roesch came over with the lamb. He stood beside his wife in the yard with the lamb in the crook of his arm, and the four of them — grandfather, grandmother, granddaughter, lamb — stood in the August noon in a small accidental tableau. Edith on Oma Roesch's shoulder. The lamb looking up at Opa Roesch. The grandparents close together because they had been close together for forty years.

Albert took the picture in his head.

Lunch was bratwurst and *Knoephla* soup and dark bread, and the lamb fell asleep on Opa Roesch's lap at the table and snored faintly, and Alice tried to feed it a piece of bread, and Eva watched her older daughter laughing at the lamb and her younger daughter being held by her grandmother and her husband looking better than she had seen him look in months, and Eva thought, very privately: *this is the good day.*

They drove back to Eureka in the late afternoon. The girls slept in the back. The radio played a tinny song about a girl in a red dress. The car smelled faintly of the lamb.

---

A few weeks later, on a warm summer evening, the Isaaks sat on the porch of the apartment above the store.

Eva was on the bench with Edith on her hip. Edith's small fat fingers were winding into the loose hair at the back of Eva's neck and tugging gently. Alice was on the top step in her white summer dress with the blue flowers, her bare feet flat on the second step, drawing a map in the dust of the top step with a long stick. Albert was in the rocking chair with his shoes off.

The light was going. Out across the side yard toward the open country the wheat was turning from gold to copper to a deep red to a pale grey-rose. A meadowlark was singing four notes somewhere in the wheat. The small breeze off the prairie was warm and dry and smelled of the harvest that was still two weeks off.

Eva looked over at him.

"Albert."

"Yes."

"This is a very good evening."

"Yes."

"I would like to remember it."

"So would I."

He held out his hand. She put her free hand into his. Alice drew her map. Edith pulled her mother's hair. The meadowlark finished its four notes and began them again. The wheat went from red to grey-rose. The sun touched the rim of the prairie and began to go down behind it.

Albert closed his eyes in the rocking chair to feel the warmth of the last of the light on his face. Eva watched him close his eyes and did not say anything.

The porch in the long evening light was the answer to every shell hole and every canvas tent and every silence in every field hospital, and Albert was home.

He did not know what was coming. Nobody on that porch knew what was coming.

The meadowlark sang its four notes one more time. The light was very long and very flat and very gold, and then the light was gone.
