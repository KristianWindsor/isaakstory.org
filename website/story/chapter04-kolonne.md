## Chapter Four

# Kolonne

Pabianice, Duchy of Warsaw — April 1815

---

The plow went first.

The Isaaks had stayed behind the previous spring for little Anna Maria, too small then to risk on the road. Now she could walk.

Father had sharpened the plow the autumn before out of habit, the way a man does a thing so many times it lives in his hands instead of his head. The iron stayed bright through winter, unused. When the miller came up the track in April with his cart and his small eyes and his knowledge of exactly what an Isaak needed to be debt-free, Father walked him out to the barn and named a price. The miller named another. Father looked at the worn handles and the blade he had driven through Polish clay for just shy of two decades, said yes, and turned away before the miller had finished counting.

Karl saw his father come out of the barn alone. Father walked to the fence and stood looking at the far fields. Then he slapped both hands on the rail, pushed off, and went inside.

At the kitchen table Karl counted the coins and set aside the sum still owed. Father watched without correcting him. What remained would go toward horses and the journey.

The farm came apart over three weeks. The spinning wheel and the loom went to the neighbor's wife down the road. The brass candlesticks and Mother's locket came out from behind the chimney stone and went to market wrapped in a rag. Michael was twenty-one that spring; he wrapped the tools they were keeping in oiled cloth and fitted them beneath the wagon's floorboards, like a man packing the bones of something that would need to stand again.

The milk cow went to the same neighbor. Karl had called her Marta in his own head since he was small, though no one else had bothered to name her. He stood at the gate while she was led away. Once she stopped to crop the grass, and for a moment he thought she would turn back.

Her price helped pay for two shaggy horses from the Lask market. They needed a team to pull the wagon; the Warsaw hospitals had never sent back the old one.

In the kitchen, Mother packed the things they were taking. The wooden bowls. The new iron pot—the old one had left in a deserter's sack. The good knife. Dorothea, eleven and serious, received each object with both hands, one eye on little Maria, waddling toward every open crate.

Little Maria reached into the flour sack. Dorothea caught her wrist too late. Mother took the small white hand, shook the flour back into the sack, and folded the top shut.

On the second-to-last evening, Karl walked alone up the rise at the eastern edge of the rye field. The ground lay open and grey. He had worked this field beside his father and brothers, watched armies from its edge, crossed it in weather that made the house seem very far away. Now he stood with nothing to do in it.

He was not sure there was anything to say to a field you were leaving, a field that had never belonged to you and would not miss you. He stayed until the light changed. Then he went back down.

The last evening, a neighbor came to the gate—a Pole, a grey-bearded farmer who had lived at the edge of their world for as long as Karl could remember, rarely exchanging more than a nod at the road. He bought nothing. He didn't ask to. He took Father's hand in both of his and held it, and said something in Polish, low and serious. Father listened with his chin down. Then the neighbor walked back the way he had come.

Karl had been too far away to hear. He asked what the man had said.

Father was quiet for a moment. "He said, God go with you, Isaak. May you find good land." He went back to the fire.

---

They loaded the wagon the next morning.

Michael had planned the load since February and ran it like a sergeant, checking the weight on each axle with both hands. The seed grain went under the wagon seat—linen sacks of rye, barley, flax, a little wheat. Enough for the first sowing. Michael counted the sacks twice.

The three books went in together, and Karl watched his mother pack them. Inside the Bible lay an old letter in her mother's hand, nearly parted at the folds. She eased its protruding edge between the pages before laying the book flat in the straw. The Catechism beside it. Then the Gesangbuch, the old Lutheran songbook, its cover worn soft as skin from years of Sunday hands. She held it a moment before she put it in. Her face didn't change but the way she held it did, something in the wrists. The three books lay in the dark of the crate together, and she closed the lid.

Christian appeared at the wagon's gate with Hexe in a wicker basket. The one-eyed grey barn cat had expressed her opinion of the basket loudly all morning, but now sat in watchful, offended silence, with the posture of a minor empress in exile. Ernst pressed his face against the weave and whispered something to her. She looked into the middle distance and did not respond.

"She'll be fine," Christian told Ernst, with the absolute confidence of a boy who understood animals the way other boys understood nothing.

Ernst accepted this and moved away, then came back a minute later.

The wagon was ready by mid-morning. Everything that was going was on it. Everything that was staying—the cradle, the loom, the churn, the bedframe—stood in the yard in the thin April light, already belonging to someone else.

---

The crossroads east of Pabianice was already full—a dozen families, wagons, animals, noise and smell. A baby cried somewhere in the forward wagons. Two men were arguing about the order of march. A dog ran between the wheels and was shouted at from three directions.

These were the ones left behind—families who had needed one more harvest, one more season to clear debts, or the courage to leave. The Leischners were among them. Their debts had kept them another year too.

Among them were the Strümpskes. Karl knew the father by sight from the church pews. His daughter Elisabeth, sixteen that April, was holding both Samuel's hands while he climbed the wheel. He jumped for the wagon bed and pulled her against the side. She laughed, then rubbed her wrist when he had turned away. Two long chestnut braids hung down her back, one already coming loose. Michael called Karl to their wagon. He had to call twice.

Mr. Leischner sat his horse near the head of the column. The families had entrusted him with their papers and the dealings at the border posts. The Kronpass—the Crown Passport for the whole column—rode inside his coat, folded in oiled leather. He touched the breast of it the way other men touched a ring.

Mr. Roloff, out of Parlin, whom the families had chosen to lead the column, came past on a dark horse, stopping to ask whether each family was ready. At their wagon he leaned down and offered Father his hand.

Mr. Flegel climbed onto his wagon seat, opened his Bible, and the column went quiet. He was not ordained, but the families had asked him to lead their prayers. He read from Genesis—the call, the going out, the promise—and then the Gesangbücher came out and the column sang. Quietly at first, then louder, the familiar words carrying across the fields in the thin morning.

Then Mother climbed onto the wagon seat, folded the wool blanket double beneath her, and straightened her spine. She did not look back toward Pabianice.

Michael went to the lead horse's head; Father, Karl, and Johann took their places beside the wheels; Christian settled in the wagon bed with Dorothea and Ernst. The call passed down the column, and the wagons began to move.

Karl walked beside the wheel. At the first bend he looked back, but the wagons behind them already hid the road to the farm.

---

The road was alive in May.

The column made ten miles on a good day—half that when spring rain turned the roads to a clinging clay that swallowed wheels. Then the men went in knee-deep, shoulders to the spokes, Wilhelm Leischner among them, eleven and fiercely proud, until the wagon came clear and they stood muddy to the waist, grinning. They were moving. The mud was what it cost.

They rose before light. Father took off his cap and led the morning prayers while the children stood blinking beside the wagon. Then came the hitching, the creak of wheels, bread and cheese eaten walking, a long noon halt beside whatever water they could find. Before they ate, they folded their hands again. Ernst always reached for the bread on "forever," and Mother always caught his wrist before "Amen." On the wagon seat she rode with little Maria bound to her chest in a woolen shawl, nursing beneath the wool while she mended.

At the noon halts Ernst disappeared into the tall grass with seven-year-old Samuel Strümpske, a fast alliance of dirt and mischief. At one such halt, Karl went down a muddy creek bank to fill the water buckets and found Elisabeth there before him.

She knelt at the water's edge, holding a heavy wooden pail beneath the surface. She was small and slender. One braid hung over her shoulder, almost touching the water. When she pushed it back, Karl saw her face clearly for the first time—the pale cheeks, the small straight nose, the frown as she tried to free the pail from the sucking mud. He set his buckets down and forgot to fill them.

She stood and pulled. One boot slipped. Water leapt over the rim, splashed across her linen apron, and poured down her skirt. The brown wool darkened almost to black. She gasped at the cold, then stood staring at the water dripping from her hem.

"Let me," Karl said, taking the iron handle before she could object, and hauled the pail onto the dry grass.

"I had it," she said, though she was breathing hard.

"I know," Karl said.

She looked at him then. A fleck of mud had landed on her cheek. He nearly told her, but she was smiling, a little uncertainly, and he smiled back without saying anything. She took the handle in both hands and started up the bank. At the top she glanced back. Karl bent over his own bucket so quickly that he knocked it against a stone.

Johann, who was leading a horse down to drink, passed by and raised an eyebrow.

"It was a heavy bucket," Karl said, before his brother could speak.

Johann smiled at the water and said nothing.

The Isaak and Leischner wagons traveled nose-to-tail. Wilhelm spent much of each day walking beside Karl, scavenging the roadside. One afternoon he held out a piece of white quartz, smooth as a tooth.

"Because I have two of them," Wilhelm said.

Karl took it. It was warm from the boy's hand. He slipped it into his pocket without breaking stride, remembering the useless things he had once brought to Michael.

Farther east, onion domes appeared among the church spires. The words Christian brought back from the markets began to sound less like the Polish Karl knew.

---

In the third week, a child died.

A toddler from the wagon of a family named Brandt, a boy of not quite two. A throat sickness: a fever, a cough that closed on itself, two days that the family fought with everything they had and lost. The column halted. The men dug beside the road while the Brandts washed the boy. A fly settled at the corner of his mouth. His mother brushed it away and drew the cloth over his face. The cross was wood, the name cut with a knife.

Mr. Flegel read the burial prayers, rough-voiced but steady. Then they sang Wenn wir in höchsten Nöten sein—in our greatest hour of need—and the voices went out across the fields until there was only the wind.

Karl watched the child's mother, standing at the graveside with her husband's hand in both of hers, her head up, looking at the cross. When the men began to fill the grave, she let go of him and crouched beside it. Her husband waited.

Dorothea had made a quiet friend of the dead child's older sister. She stayed beside her through the burial, and when Mother called her back to their wagon, she shook her head.

That night Mother held Ernst while he slept, sitting beside the fire past the time she would ordinarily have gone to her blankets.

The column moved on in the morning. In less than a minute the grave was behind them and the road ahead was the same road. At the first halt, Karl went to their wagon and laid a hand on little Maria's back. She was asleep against Mother, warm through the shawl.

That evening the men still talked about sixty dessiatines. But they spoke more quietly, for shorter stretches, and left more room between sentences.

---

They reached the Russian border at Uscilug, where the road crossed the Bug, late in May.

The border post was a low wooden building held by two young guards in green coats, surprising Karl with their ordinariness, their capacity for yawning.

The column halted. Mr. Leischner dismounted with deliberate gravity, drew the Kronpass from its case, unfolded it, and handed it across the table with both hands. The officer took it with one and did not look up.

The official moved family by family, calling each head of household forward, entering names into his ledger without once looking at the face attached to the name. Heads tallied. Ages recorded. Occupations: farmer, farmer, farmer.

"Isaak," Father said when their turn came, and spelled it without being asked, having learned that his name required spelling.

The officer wrote it. At Karl's name he paused for his age. "Eighteen," Karl said. The pen went on.

When the ledger closed, the clerk slid coins across the table with one finger—the first installment of the feeding money, reckoned at five kopecks a soul for each day of Russian road. Father passed them to Karl to count. Karl checked the sum, nodded, and handed them back.

Four Cossacks, mounted soldiers in the Tsar's service, watched from a field to the north, their lances upright, perfectly still.

At the post the column was assigned a conductor—a Russian official with a leather satchel and a tired horse, to ride with them the rest of the way, naming the roads and the halts. The families kept their places in the column. The road, from here, was the empire's.

When they moved on, the conductor chose the road. Karl looked back once to make sure the Leischner wagon had followed.

That evening Father divided the coins into small piles on a scrap of cloth: food, fodder, something to keep for trouble. He counted the last pile again before folding it away.

---

There were more rivers. On the wooden bridges Karl could feel the planks shake under the team. At the shallow crossings he walked beside the wagon with his trousers rolled above his knees, feeling for stones with his feet.

The land was broader here, browner, braided with rivers and marshes that breathed in the summer heat. The mosquitoes came up from the low ground at evening in clouds so thick they moved like weather. The children were welted from wrist to ear. Christian bought a length of thin cloth from a Jewish merchant and draped it over his hat, tucking the ends into his collar. He wore it for a week while his brothers made remarks. Then Johann asked to borrow it.

The column bought buckwheat porridge from Ruthenian farmwives, dried fish from river merchants, and goat cheese that tasted sharp and smoky. Christian did the bargaining, fifteen and more ear than judgment, in a mixture of the road's languages that made the market women laugh and give him extra.

"What did you say to her?" Dorothea asked when Christian came back with his arms full.

"I said I had many brothers and a very poor father."

"That's not entirely false," Karl said.

One night a summer squall broke over the camp. Karl was out in the driving rain, hauling on the rope of a horse that kept throwing up its head. A loose edge of wagon canvas snapped beside them. Elisabeth came through the rain and caught his sleeve.

"Give him room," she said, barely heard above the rain. "Away from the wagon."

Karl eased the rope and moved with the horse instead of pulling against it. Elisabeth gathered the flapping canvas. A gust pulled her forward; she braced a foot against the wheel and held on while he led the horse clear.

By the time the squall passed, the horse had stopped trembling. Karl stood with the wet rope loose in his hand. Elisabeth came to stand beside him, rain dripping from her braids.

"I thought he'd get away," Karl said.

"So did I."

She tucked her shaking hands beneath her arms and tried to laugh. Karl stayed beside her. After a little while she looked up at him and managed a smile, and he smiled back. Then Mr. Strümpske called her name. She went toward his voice, and Karl led the horse back to its tether.

A few days later, a wheel broke at a creek crossing—a sudden crack, the wagon tipping sideways, and Michael shouting to stop the team. He and the column's wheelwright worked through the morning. The other wagons went on ahead.

Karl stood beside the tilted wagon and watched the last canvas top disappear around the bend. He could still hear the hammers behind him. The road was empty.

They caught up only at dusk, pulling into the camp circle as the fires were being lit. Wilhelm came running to meet them. Beyond him, someone was calling for a missing pot, a baby was crying, and the horses were cropping the grass between the wagons. Karl had not known how much he would need to hear it all again.

By the end of June, Mother was ill.

It began as a headache she mentioned once, then stopped mentioning. Then a shivering that didn't match the temperature, a cold no wool addressed. The others called it marsh fever. Katherina Leischner climbed into the wagon at the halts with a wet cloth and spoke to her in a low, steady voice.

Mother lay on a doubled blanket, her white linen cap damp at the edges, her breathing shallow. Sweat soured her shift, and her teeth chattered hard enough for Karl to hear them from beside the wheel. She was soon too weak to nurse. Dorothea fed little Maria bread softened in goat's milk, but the child kept reaching for Mother. Mother's face in fever was changed, all the daily arrangement of it gone, reduced to the bones beneath the skin.

Michael brought broth at every halt, holding the bowl close and refusing to pull it away until she swallowed. Dorothea sat beside Mother when little Maria would let her. Karl took over the water buckets, walking ahead whenever they stopped to find where they could be filled. Once he caught himself counting the days the Brandt boy had been ill.

Wilhelm came after him with the second bucket.

"Go back," Karl said. "Your father will want you."

"He sent me."

Karl let him take the lighter pail on the way back. At the next halt, he waited for him.

One evening, with the fires low and the column sleeping, Karl looked up to see his father sitting in the grass beside the wagon—lowered down without sound, a heaviness in his body that was not tiredness. He reached up and laid his hand on Mother's forehead through the side of the wagon. His hand—calloused, missing the tip of its ring finger, a hand that had broken ground and split wood—lay there without moving.

Karl watched. He had seen his father carry his mother's work when she was occupied. He had seen him reach past her to close a shutter against rain. He had never seen him touch her face. He began the Lord's Prayer under his breath. At "Thy will be done," he stopped, swallowed, and made himself go on.

After a time Mother opened her eyes—pale blue in the firelight, the blue of very cold winter sky, and despite the fever clear and thoroughly her own.

"How far," she said.

Father's voice came out softer than Karl had ever heard it. "Weeks yet."

"Then drive faster."

Father lifted her hand from the blanket and held it in both of his.

Two mornings later, she could hold the cup herself. Michael stayed beside her until she had finished it. She still needed help to sit up, but when little Maria reached for her, she held out her arms.

---

By late July they were among the long hills of Podolia. Then the Dniester appeared without warning.

The column came around a low hillside and there it was—wide and brown and fast, a broad muscular rush unlike any water Karl had crossed before. The far bank was low and indistinct with heat haze. Between them and it, the ferry landing was crowded with wagons, their wheels sunk deep in the churned mud.

They waited in the mud three days. Flat-bottomed ferries went and came back, a wagon or two at a time, drawn across on a thick rope. The line moved slowly in the heat while the mosquitoes worked the margins and the children complained and were ignored. Nearby, the wreck of an earlier wagon lay on the river bottom—a wheel and part of an axle breaking the surface, grey as driftwood. Nobody pointed it out. Nobody had to.

At the front of the queue, Karl studied the ferry. Its low rail was smooth from other men's hands; along the edge, water showed through gaps in the planking. Mud and horse dung had been trodden into the wood, giving off a sour smell in the heat. A ferryman kicked a wedge beneath a wagon wheel and beckoned them on.

The horses would not go. Michael went to the nearest horse's head and spoke with his face close to its ear, saying something Karl couldn't hear. The animal's breathing slowed. It put one hoof on the planking, then the other. Karl felt the boards bend beneath his boots as the team came aboard.

Mother sat in the wagon bed with little Maria bound to her chest, one hand braced on the wagon side. Karl stood beside the wheel with Ernst close against him.

The ferry pushed off. The current took it sideways at once and the rope went taut and they were in it—brown water racing past the planks, the far bank seeming to draw no closer. An iron ring knocked against the timber. Beneath Karl's feet the river slapped and sucked at the hull; he could feel each blow through the soles of his boots. The horses stood with their legs spread, blowing hard. Everything they owned was on this little patch of wood.

Halfway across, the ferry shuddered and tilted. Ernst lurched against Karl's legs; Karl caught him and pulled him close. Cold water splashed over the edge and soaked his boot. He could hear Mother praying behind him, very quickly. His own mouth had gone dry. He held Ernst until the boards settled beneath them and the water drained back through the gaps.

The rope held. Slowly, the far bank came nearer.

They rolled down the ramp onto Bessarabian ground. Mother finished her prayer. Karl let go of Ernst and stood a moment with both feet planted in the mud.

---

The trees grew scarce.

For days they had passed fewer woods, then only scattered clumps in hollows and along streams. Between them the road crossed long bare ridges. There were villages, but hours could pass without a roof in sight.

Karl climbed onto the wagon seat to see farther. Beyond the next rise lay another, and beyond that another, their outlines fading into the pale sky. The waist-high grass moved in long slow pulses, the way deep water moves. It hissed. The wind came out of the east smelling of dust and distance.

From up here he could see the wagons ahead and behind, the column moving through the grass like a row of stitches across the face of something enormous.

Karl climbed back down. At home he had measured distances by the woods and rooflines. Here he could walk an hour toward a rise and scarcely seem closer.

After a while Father came up beside him and they walked in silence.

"I had not pictured it like this," Father said.

Karl looked at him. Father kept his eyes on the grass ahead. They walked several more paces.

"Neither had I," Karl said.

Father shifted the coil of rope on his shoulder and kept walking. Karl stayed beside him.

That evening, while Karl was brushing down a horse, the canvas of the Leischner wagon turned pink. He looked up. The sun had dropped behind the ridge, but a broad band of orange still burned there, bright as the mouth of a forge. Above it, a violet glow spread high into the sky, crossed by long dark streaks. He stood with the brush resting against the horse's neck. By the time the first stars showed, he could scarcely see the animal beside him, and still the western sky glowed.

---

In August, the conductor rode ahead with two horsemen come out from the colony. The word came back: close now. The column came up a long rise, and Karl saw the settlement.

A summer on the road had given him time to imagine it—timber houses, the skeleton of a church, a Polish village on foreign soil. He looked past the low roofs for the rest of it.

The Kogälnik valley lay broad below them, green along the river. The settlement stood higher, on a long slope. A single street of staggering width ran through the grass, wider than any market square Karl had stood in and half an hour's walk end to end. Dark patches of low oak growth broke the bare hillside beyond.

Along both edges of that great street stood the huts—low shelters of poles and woven branches, roofed with grass and cut sod. Some were sunk into the slope, their doorways scarcely higher than a wagon wheel. Wagons stood beside them in the long staked yards. Cook-fire smoke rose among them. Between the yards, gaps had been left open in the grass—measured, empty, waiting.

Karl began to recognize faces among the huts: a man who had sat behind them in chapel, a woman who used to bring eggs to market. Behind them, strips of steppe had been broken open, the sod turned in ropes of black against the yellow. A man lifted bundles of tall, dry weeds onto a stack with a wooden fork. A woman pulled out a handful and fed it beneath her cooking pot. The stalks caught with a sharp crackle, sending up a bitter thread of smoke. Rows of dung cakes lay drying nearby. At the edge of the settlement stood a stack of pale new timber. A few house frames had begun to rise.

Mr. Hille came out of a hut and called Father's name. For a moment Father stared at him; then they were gripping hands and talking at once. Father looked past him at the low doorway.

"You spent the winter here?"

"In Moldavian villages. We had a corner of somebody's kitchen. They brought us onto this ground in spring."

Father bent to look inside. Hille put a hand on his shoulder, then turned to fetch a pail for Johann.

"Drinking water's up the street, at the spring. Come, I'll show you."

The officials were three: a junior officer, a surveyor, and a translator whose German Karl could follow if he listened closely. They called the colony Paulsberg. Karl repeated it under his breath, trying to fix the name to the bare ground before him.

The surveyor walked the family to one of the empty gaps between two smoking huts. He pointed out the pegs marking the front corners of the ground set aside for their house and garden, then led them a short way up the long plot behind it. Most of their sixty dessiatines lay in fields beyond the village, the translator explained. The surveyor swung his riding crop toward the higher ground.

Back at the wagons, the translator called the heads of household together for the oath of allegiance to Alexander. Mr. Hille had returned with Johann. He stood beside Father as the men gathered.

"Will they give us the settlement money now?" Karl asked him.

Hille glanced toward the table. "Don't reckon on a purse of silver. Some who came last year wouldn't swear until they had it. And the cattle they'd been promised."

Karl remembered the agent lowering the paper before he spoke of silver.

"What happened?"

"They were threatened with being sent out of Russia. First they'd have to pay back the aid they'd been given to settle."

Karl looked at their wagon. They had sold nearly everything else to get here. Father said nothing.

Hille lowered his voice. "This summer it was the stores at Elisabeth colony. Rotten flour, money held back. The men demanded the keys. Pastor Schnabel stood with them."

"They had a pastor?" Father asked.

"Word came that soldiers beat some of the men. Took Schnabel to Kishinev. He's in prison."

At the table the officer shifted his papers. The translator called again, and Hille stepped back.

Father went forward with Mr. Strümpske and the others. Karl watched him listen to the words, his right hand still at his side. Beyond him, Dorothea was trying to lift little Maria back into the wagon. The child had begun to cry. Mother reached down to take her.

Then Father raised his hand. Karl could hear his voice among the others.

When it was over, Father waited at the table and asked how soon their share of the building timber could be issued. The translator pointed toward the stack and began explaining. Father listened closely.

Karl looked at the pegs. Their wagon filled only a small part of the space between them. Tomorrow they would have to begin making a shelter here.

The Strümpske wagon stood a little farther down the street. Elisabeth looked back as she helped Samuel down, and Karl raised a hand. She waved before turning to the unloading.

Mother handed the fussing toddler to Dorothea and climbed down from the wagon by herself, still slow after the fever. Michael moved toward her; she put up one hand. He stopped.

She walked from the wagon into the grass. Three steps, four—her skirt pushing through the dry stalks with a sound like paper—until she stood in the untouched steppe. Then she knelt and pushed the dry grass apart. She put her right hand flat on the ground, palm down, fingers spread, the way a woman tests a child's forehead for fever. She pressed her hand into the dry sod and held it there.

The wind moved through the grass around her in long slow waves.

Karl watched his mother. None of them spoke—even Ernst, who had been asking questions since before the Dniester, was silent. She stayed like that a long time.

When she stood, there was dark earth on her palm. She looked at it and did not wipe it off. She walked back to the wagon, and Father put out his hand to help her up. She took it.
