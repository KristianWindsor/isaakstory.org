## Chapter Three

# The Tsar's Manifesto

Pabianice, Duchy of Warsaw — January 1814

---

The Duchy of Warsaw was dead. From the winter snows, a Russian-controlled Polish state was rising. Word reached the village that Napoleon’s grand army was crumbling—driven from Germany as the Tsar's forces marched on France—leaving the world to be carved up once again by men in distant rooms.

For the German colonists, the changing of the guard felt like a tightening noose. 

Karl was seventeen. Johann was fifteen. Michael had just turned twenty. Father’s hair had gone entirely grey at the temples, the exact color of the iron-grey January sky. 

On Sunday morning, the household bundled into their Sunday best. Karl and Johann wore Michael's outgrown winter coats, the stiff woolen cuffs riding uncomfortably high on their freezing, plow-calloused wrists. Mother fastened her thick woolen shawl, pinning it tightly against the biting frost, and took up her hymnal. As they walked the frozen, snow-crusted ruts of the road, the icy, bone-chilling morning air only magnified the suffocating weight hanging over the family. 

They walked in silence. There was no bell to call them over the snowdrifts. 

The low square church tower stood dark and empty. The bronze bell had been requisitioned by advancing French armies two summers ago to be melted down for cannon, and the Polish Catholic authorities, eager to remind the German settlers of their place in this new regime, had firmly refused to grant a permit for a new one. The Lutherans were expected to gather in the quiet of the winter.

Inside, the chill seeped through the floorboards. The men sat on the right and the women on the left in the old way. The wooden benches were dark with sixty years of use, but the church was half-empty, hollowed out like a rotten tooth. Everywhere Karl looked, there were glaring gaps in the congregation—entire pews left vacant, marking the neighbors, friends, and whole families lost to the typhus epidemic the armies had left in their wake. 

Pastor Hartmann, looking older than his years and widowed by that same fever, stood at the altar. His breath plumed in the freezing air. He opened the heavy pulpit Bible to the Book of Genesis, looked out over his shivering congregation, and read a single verse.

*"Now the Lord had said unto Abram, Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee."*

He closed the book and stepped aside.

A stranger stood up from the front pew and climbed the creaking steps to the pulpit. He wore the thick, forest-green greatcoat of a Russian imperial agent, his brass buttons gleaming like cold coins in the dim light. 

When he spoke, it was in perfect, heavily accented German. He unrolled a heavy sheet of crown paper bearing the black, two-headed eagle stamp of Tsar Alexander I. 

"By the Imperial Manifesto of His Majesty," the agent began, his voice ringing off the timber walls, "signed the twenty-ninth of November, eighteen-thirteen... 'We, Alexander the First, by the Grace of God, Emperor and Autocrat of all the Russias, do hereby open the southern crown lands to you.'"

He spoke of a vast, untouched steppe on the Black Sea. A region the Tsar's men called *Bessarabien*. 

The agent read the terms slowly, letting each one drop into the silent, freezing church like a stone into a still pond. 

First, the land. Sixty *dessiatines*—roughly one hundred and sixty acres—granted free and clear to any colonist family willing to work it. A quiet, feverish murmur rippled through the men's side of the aisle. One hundred and sixty acres was a staggering, almost magical number. A man could work his entire life in Prussia or Poland and never own a quarter of that. It was the size of a nobleman's estate. 

Second, the Crown’s provision. "To ease the hardship of the road," the agent bellowed, his voice echoing over the rapt congregation, "the Tsar will provision your wagon trains with a travel stipend of silver. Upon your arrival, the Crown will front the cost of your survival—plows, seed grain, and livestock—as an interest-free advance. You will not be asked to repay a single ruble until ten years have passed."

Third, ten years of total exemption from all taxes and duties. For a village that had been bled dry by French foraging parties and Polish tax collectors for a decade, the promise of keeping the entirety of their harvest felt like a beautifully impossible dream. 

Fourth, freedom of religion. They could build churches, cast bronze bells, and call their own pastors in perpetuity. 

In the third pew, Father sat perfectly still, his large, scarred hands resting heavily on his knees. He had heard this song before. Twenty years ago, the Prussian King had offered these exact same promises to convince young German families to settle this very soil when it was newly conquered Polish territory. Father and Mother had come as newlyweds. They had broken their backs turning wild, frozen scrub into productive fields. 

But empires shifted like the winter winds. Prussia had lost the land to the French, and the French had lost it to the Russians. The Germans were now a hated minority on land they had bled to tame. The Tsar was not offering them a gift. The Tsar needed a wild border secured, just as the Prussian King had. And Father knew that Crown loans were simply another way to bind a man to the dirt.

Then, the Russian agent looked up from the heavy paper and delivered the final term. 

Permanent exemption from military conscription. 

A collective breath seemed to leave the men's side of the church. Karl turned his head and looked at his older brother. Michael was staring straight ahead, his jaw clenched so tightly the muscle jumped beneath his skin. Michael was twenty. He was of age. Under the new Russian-Polish regime, the drafts were already being drawn up. If they stayed in this village, Michael would be handed a uniform and a musket, and he would be marched away into the snow to serve a foreign Tsar. 

A sudden, desperate hunger filled the pews. If they could have hitched their wagons in the deep snowdrifts that very afternoon, half the village would have ridden out before sunset. Every man in the room seemed to be calculating how many months of bitter winter remained before the roads would thaw into passable mud. 

The agent rolled up the heavy paper. "The Crown has opened the door," he said softly. "But we do not harbor fugitives. Any man wishing to claim this land must present himself to me with a signed exit visa from your local Polish magistrate, proving all taxes and debts are settled. Only then will I issue the Crown Passports for your journey."

Father closed his eyes, the heavy weight of the reality sinking into his chest. The Tsar's paradise was locked behind a Polish gate. He knew the local magistrates despised the German colonists; they would demand exorbitant bribes and delay the exit visas for months. And every day they waited for those papers, Michael's name remained on the Polish draft lists. It was a race they could not afford to lose.

They sang *Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott* to close the service, but the voices were thin, choked by the ghosts in the empty pews and the terrifying sheer drop of the future standing before them.

---

After the service, the colony spilled out into the pale, freezing winter sun. The men did not linger by the wall to smoke; they stood in tight, hushed knots, their breath rising in white clouds. At the far end of the yard, the younger children played a quiet game in the packed snow, Dorothea watching over Ernst, who was bundled so thickly in wool he could barely waddle. 

The Leischners found the Isaaks near the snow-capped gate. 

Katherina Leischner went straight to Mother, taking her arm in a tight, desperate grip. "I know, Lieschen," Katherina whispered, her eyes wet but fierce. "I know."

While the women held each other, Wilhelm, now thirteen, practically ran across the icy grass toward Karl. His nose was bright red from the cold, but his face was lit up with the desperate relief of finding something normal in a morning that felt entirely wrong. 

"Karl, look," Wilhelm said, pulling a chipped, grey-brown stone from his heavy coat. "In the south field, where the wind blew the snow clear. It has to be an old arrowhead, doesn't it?"

Karl took the freezing stone, turning it gravely in his bare hand, asking the right questions in the right places. Under Karl's steady attention, Wilhelm went perfectly still. Over Wilhelm's shoulder, Karl caught sight of Johann leaning casually against the icy churchyard wall, wearing a jacket that was much too short for his long arms. Rosina Leischner, seventeen and tall with thick chestnut braids tucked under her hood, had drifted over to him. She was speaking to him from under her lashes. Johann was staring very hard at the horizon, trying desperately to pretend he didn't care that she was lingering, and failing completely. 

Then, Mr. Leischner spoke, and the quiet normalcy of the winter yard shattered. 

He stood before Father, square-shouldered in his dark wool frock coat, but his deep, careful voice was stripped of its usual measure. 

"We have survived the French, Michel," Mr. Leischner said quietly. "We survived the typhus. But we will not survive this peace."

Father looked at his oldest friend. "You are leaving."

"Katherina and I have decided," Leischner said, gesturing toward the empty church. "Half our neighbors are in the ground. I will not bury another child in this dirt, and I will not give my son to the Tsar's army. We are leaving for Bessarabia the moment the spring thaw dries the roads." 

"The Polish clerks will not let us go easily," Father said, his voice low and grim. "You heard the agent. An exit visa. They despise the Tsar’s offer. They will drag their feet, and they will extort every groschen we have before they sign the papers."

Leischner nodded heavily. "I know. We will have to sell the heavy plows. The surplus grain. The extra livestock. Everything we cannot put in the wagon."

"If we try to sell," Father said, "the whole village will be trying to sell. Prices will collapse to nothing." Father glanced over his shoulder at Michael, who stood silently near the icy fence. "And the draft boards are already writing lists. If the clerks delay the visas for months, and Michael’s name is drawn before the Russian passport is in my hand..." 

Father didn't finish the thought. He didn't have to. The race against time was suddenly, terrifyingly real. 

The two men stood together against the church wall. They did not speak for a long moment. They had stood together in the aftermath of plagues, crop failures, and regime changes, but this was the end of it. 

Beside Karl, Wilhelm had stopped looking at the arrowhead. The boy was staring at the snow. The lit-up thirteen-year-old who had run across the yard five minutes ago was gone. In his place was a child who had just learned, here in his own freezing churchyard, that he was leaving the only world he knew—and leaving Karl behind. 

Karl didn't say anything. He just reached out and put his hand firmly on Wilhelm's thick woolen shoulder. Wilhelm didn't look up, but he leaned his small weight, just slightly, against Karl's leg. 

---

The Isaaks walked home across the fields. The pale sun was already sinking, and the silence among them was heavier than it had ever been. Mother walked ahead with Father, Ernst on her hip with his small hooded head against her neck. Michael walked with Christian. Dorothea was between them. 

Karl and Johann fell behind. By twenty paces, then thirty. 

Johann looked at the rutted tracks beneath his boots, his pace fast and restless. The eagerness was practically vibrating out of him in the cold. "Karl," he said, his voice low but fierce. "Did you hear him? One hundred and sixty acres. Travel allowances, seed grain, a house built on the Tsar's ruble. It’s an estate, Karl. We could go too. As soon as the snow melts."

Karl stared at the frozen post-road. "It's a single estate, Johann. It can't be divided. Michael gets it all. You and I would just be working his dirt until we have to go find our own."

"It's better than waiting here for the Polish draft board to come for him!" Johann shot back, his breath pluming in frustration. "It’s a way out."

"A way out to what?" Karl asked quietly. "It's a loan, not a gift. You heard Father. To get the visas, we'll have to sell everything for nothing. We'd be sleeping under a wagon in the winter, and we'd be in debt to an Emperor."

"There's no war there," Johann insisted, refusing to let the dream die. 

Karl didn't answer. In his pocket, his freezing fingers closed around the French sou. He had been carrying it for nearly two years now, rubbing it with his thumb whenever he was thinking, until Napoleon's profile was almost entirely worn away. The Roman wreath was still there, faintly. The emperor's face was gone, wiped blank by time and friction. Empires fade, Karl thought. Only the dirt remains.

They reached the farmyard. 

Father stopped. The family stopped behind him. 

He stood in the center of the snow-covered yard and looked at the *Wohnstallhaus*—the long timber-framed building that housed their family on one end and their cattle on the other. It was the house he had built with his own hands ten years ago. He looked at the thick clay-and-straw daub packed between the wooden beams, remembering the freezing autumn weeks he and Elisabeth had spent pressing it into place so the children wouldn't freeze. 

He looked past the barn to the rye fields, buried under a thick blanket of white. He remembered the grueling, back-breaking agony of clearing it, hauling stones out of the earth yard by yard until his hands bled, just to force a harvest from it. And beyond the rye, at the edge of the tree line, were the three small wooden crosses marking the typhus winter—neighbors they had buried when the ground was like iron, because there was no one else left to dig. 

This dirt was his life's work. It held his sweat, his youth, and his dead. 

To leave it meant walking away from everything they had built, stripping themselves of their wealth just to bribe a clerk, and plunging into a wild, untamed steppe hundreds of miles away to start from nothing. 

But to stay meant losing Michael to the Tsar’s army. It meant feeding his sons to the same senseless, violent machine that had broken the French sergeants on the road. 

Father stood perfectly still for a long time. The howling January wind caught the edge of his long wool coat. 

Then, he turned around. The grim, silent exhaustion that had worn him down over the past two years was suddenly gone. In its place was a terrifying, absolute clarity. Dirt was just dirt. A house was just wood. Blood was the only thing that mattered.

He looked at Michael. Then he looked at Karl. 

"Michael. Karl," Father said, his voice booming across the quiet, frozen yard. "Open the barn doors. Pull the wagon out."
