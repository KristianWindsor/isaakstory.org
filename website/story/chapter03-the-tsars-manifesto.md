## Chapter Three

# The Tsar's Manifesto

Pabianice, Duchy of Warsaw — January 1814

---

The winter sky over Pabianice was the exact color of Father’s greying temples—a flat, unyielding iron.

There was no bell to call them over the snowdrifts. Father broke the trail. The rest of them followed in his bootprints in a thin line, breath torn away white over their shoulders. On Sunday mornings, the household bundled into their Sunday best for the hour-long trek into town, but the clothes offered little comfort against the Polish winter. Karl, seventeen, and Johann, fifteen, wore Michael’s outgrown coats, the stiff woolen cuffs riding uncomfortably high on their freezing, plow-calloused wrists.

Mother walked slower than usual, her face pale but resolute. Beneath her thick shawl, shielded fiercely against her chest to share her body heat, was tiny Anna Maria—just weeks old and the newest Isaak. Mother carried her hymnal tucked awkwardly under her arm. As they marched in silence through the frozen, snow-crusted ruts, the biting air seemed to magnify the suffocating weight hanging over the family.

Karl read the fields by their silhouettes the way he read faces. The Leischners' chimney smoking. The Flegels' roofline heavy with snow. He looked for the familiar smoke rising from the Hille and Banko households further down the ridge, and checked the Kisons' small farm near the tree line. It was a quiet tally of who was surviving the freeze, but survival was no longer just about the weather. The Duchy of Warsaw was dead. From the snows, a Russian-controlled Polish state was rising. Word had reached the colony that Napoleon’s grand army was crumbling, leaving the world to be carved up once again by men in distant rooms. For the German colonists, this changing of the guard felt like a tightening noose.

The straggling line emerged from the dark pine forests, and the modest huddle of thatched-roof cottages along the frozen Dobrzynka River came into view. Rising starkly above the snow was the Zamek, the fortified manor house of the Kraków Chapter where the Lutherans merely rented space.

It was not a church. It had no soaring steeple, no stained glass, and no cross. This was a squat, lime-washed box gone grey with weather, hunkered low in the wet ground between the river's backwaters as though it had sat down in the cold two hundred years ago and could not be persuaded to rise. As Karl approached the south entrance, the building’s defensive corner alcoves, pierced with narrow slit-windows, stared blindly out at the unpaved dirt square. Ghost-like patches of vibrant Renaissance frescoes peeled through the drab, utilitarian whitewash slapped on by bureaucrats.

They shuffled through the heavy wooden doors on the south wall, stepping out of the wind into a freezing, cavernous vestibule, and finally into the great hall.

Inside, the chill seeped up through the worn Renaissance brick floor. The men sat on the right and the women on the left in the old way, crowding onto rough, backless wooden benches the colonists had dragged in themselves. Above them, heavy, dark wooden beams pressed down from the flat ceiling. The room felt entirely secular, smelling of old plaster and cold dust. The walls were plain and whitewashed, and in the corner stood a massive, ornate white stone fireplace from the previous century, unlit and doing nothing to fight the bone-deep cold. Everywhere Karl looked, there were glaring gaps in the congregation—entire benches left vacant, marking the neighbors, friends, and whole families lost to the typhus epidemic the armies had left in their wake.

Pastor Schramm, looking older than his years and widowed by that same fever, stood behind a simple wooden table draped with a linen cloth. His breath plumed in the freezing air. He opened his heavy Bible to the Book of Genesis, looked out over his shivering congregation, and read a single verse.

*"Now the Lord had said unto Abram, Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee."*

He closed the book and stepped aside.

A stranger stood up from the front bench and walked to the front of the room. He wore the thick, forest-green greatcoat of a Russian imperial agent, his brass buttons gleaming like cold coins in the dim light spilling through the tall windows.

When he spoke, it was in perfect, heavily accented German. He unrolled a heavy sheet of crown paper bearing the black, two-headed eagle stamp of Tsar Alexander I.

"By the Imperial Manifesto of His Majesty," the agent began, his voice ringing off the flat timber ceiling, "signed the twenty-ninth of November, eighteen-thirteen... 'We, Alexander the First, by the Grace of God, Emperor and Autocrat of all the Russias, do hereby open the southern crown lands to you.'"

He spoke of a vast, untouched steppe on the Black Sea. A region the Tsar's men called *Bessarabien*.

The agent read the terms slowly, letting each one drop into the silent, freezing hall like a stone into a still pond.

First, the land. Sixty *dessiatines*—roughly one hundred and sixty acres—granted free and clear to any colonist family willing to work it. A quiet, feverish murmur rippled through the men's side of the aisle. One hundred and sixty acres was a staggering, almost magical number. A man could work his entire life in Prussia or Poland and never own a quarter of that. It was the size of a nobleman's estate.

Second, the Crown’s provision. "To ease the hardship of the road," the agent bellowed, his voice echoing over the rapt congregation, "the Tsar will provision your wagon trains with a travel stipend of silver. Upon your arrival, the Crown will front the cost of your survival—plows, seed grain, and livestock—as an interest-free advance. You will not be asked to repay a single ruble until ten years have passed."

Third, ten years of total exemption from all taxes and duties. For a village that had been bled dry by French foraging parties and Polish tax collectors for a decade, the promise of keeping the entirety of their harvest felt like a beautifully impossible dream.

Fourth, freedom of religion. They could build true churches, cast bronze bells, and call their own pastors in perpetuity.

In the third row, Father sat perfectly still, his large, scarred hands resting heavily on his knees. He had heard this song before. Twenty years ago, the Prussian King had offered these exact same promises to convince young German families to settle this very soil when it was newly conquered territory. Father and Mother had come as newlyweds. They had broken their backs turning wild, frozen scrub into productive fields.

Beside him, hidden beneath Mother's heavy shawl, little Maria stirred. She let out a tiny, breathy whimper that sounded incredibly loud in the quiet room.

Father looked at the small, bundled shape of his newborn daughter, brought into a dying country. Empires shifted like the winter winds. Prussia had lost the land to the French, and the French had lost it to the Russians. The Germans were now a hated minority on land they had bled to tame. The Tsar was not offering them a gift. The Tsar needed a wild border secured, just as the Prussian King had. And Father knew that Crown loans were simply another way to bind a man to the dirt.

Then, the Russian agent looked up from the heavy paper and delivered the final term.

Permanent exemption from military conscription.

A collective breath seemed to leave the men's side of the room. Karl turned his head and looked at his older brother. Michael was staring straight ahead, his jaw clenched so tightly the muscle jumped beneath his skin. Michael was twenty. He was of age. Under the new Russian-Polish regime, the drafts were already being drawn up. If they stayed in this village, Michael would be handed a uniform and a musket, and he would be marched away into the snow to serve a foreign Tsar.

A sudden, desperate hunger filled the hall. If they could have hitched their wagons in the deep snowdrifts that very afternoon, half the village would have ridden out before sunset. Every man in the room seemed to be calculating how many months of bitter winter remained before the roads would thaw into passable mud.

The agent rolled up the heavy paper. "The Crown has opened the door," he said softly. "But we do not harbor fugitives. Any man wishing to claim this land must present himself to me with a signed exit visa from your local Polish magistrate, proving all taxes and debts are settled. Only then will I issue the Crown Passports for your journey."

Father closed his eyes, the heavy weight of the reality sinking into his chest. The Tsar's paradise was locked behind a Polish gate. He knew the local magistrates despised the German colonists; they would demand exorbitant bribes and delay the exit visas for months. And every day they waited for those papers, Michael's name remained on the Polish draft lists. It was a race they could not afford to lose.

They sang *Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott* to close the service, but the voices were thin, choked by the ghosts in the empty hall and the terrifying sheer drop of the future standing before them.

---

After the service, the congregation spilled out of the heavy doors and into the pale, freezing winter sun. They stood in the Old Market square, an unpaved expanse of dirt that had frozen into treacherous, muddy ridges. The men did not linger to smoke; they stood in tight, hushed knots, their breath rising in white clouds against the backdrop of the drab, plastered manor walls. At the far end of the yard, near the remnants of an old wooden defensive palisade, the younger children played a quiet game in the packed snow, Dorothea watching over Ernst, who was bundled so thickly in wool he could barely waddle.

The Leischners found the Isaaks near the frozen banks of the Dobrzynka.

Katherina Leischner went straight to Mother, taking her arm in a tight, desperate grip, careful not to press against the sleeping newborn. "I know, Lieschen," Katherina whispered, her eyes wet but fierce. "I know."

While the women held each other, Johann Wilhelm, now ten, practically ran across the icy dirt toward Karl. His nose was bright red from the cold, but his face was lit up with the desperate relief of finding something normal in a morning that felt entirely wrong.

"Karl, look," Wilhelm said, pulling a chipped, grey-brown stone from his heavy coat. "By the old palisade posts, where the wind blew the snow clear. It has to be an old arrowhead, doesn't it?"

Karl took the freezing stone, turning it gravely in his bare hand, asking the right questions in the right places. Under Karl's steady attention, Wilhelm went perfectly still. Over Wilhelm's shoulder, Karl caught sight of Johann leaning casually against the manor's peeling plaster wall, wearing a jacket that was much too short for his long arms. Rosina Leischner, seventeen and tall with thick chestnut braids tucked under her hood, had drifted over to him. She was speaking to him from under her lashes. Johann was staring very hard at the horizon, trying desperately to pretend he didn't care that she was lingering, and failing completely.

Then, Mr. Leischner spoke, and the quiet normalcy of the winter yard shattered.

He stood before Father, square-shouldered in his dark wool frock coat, but his deep, careful voice was stripped of its usual measure.

"We have survived the French, Michel," Mr. Leischner said quietly. "We survived the typhus. But we will not survive this peace."

Father looked at his oldest friend. "You are leaving."

"Katherina and I have decided," Leischner said, gesturing back toward the heavy doors of the manor. "Half our neighbors are in the ground. I will not bury another child in this dirt, and I will not give my son to the Tsar's army. We are leaving for Bessarabia the moment the spring thaw dries the roads."

"The Polish clerks will not let us go easily," Father said, his voice low and grim. "You heard the agent. An exit visa. They despise the Tsar’s offer. They will drag their feet, and they will extort every groschen we have before they sign the papers."

Leischner nodded heavily. "I know. We will have to sell the heavy plows. The surplus grain. The extra livestock. Everything we cannot put in the wagon."

Father glanced over his shoulder at Michael, who stood silently near the frozen riverbank. He was twenty, strong, and already marked by the Polish recruiters. If they stayed another year, Michael would be marched off to die for a foreign Tsar. 

Then, Father looked down at the fragile, shifting bundle of Anna Maria tucked against Mother's chest. Taking a three-month-old infant out onto the deep, half-frozen mud of the spring thaw was a different kind of death sentence. The brutal pioneer road would kill her just as surely as a musket ball would kill his son.

"How do I choose?" Father whispered, his voice stripped of its usual booming authority. It was the first time Karl had ever heard his father sound entirely lost. "If we leave now, the road kills the baby. If we stay a year to let her grow, the Tsar takes Michael."

Leischner looked at the small bundle against Mother's chest, his face softening with grim sympathy. "I cannot make that choice for you, Michel. But the draft boards are already writing their lists. If the clerks delay the visas, and Michael’s name is drawn before you have the Russian passport in your hand..."

Father didn't answer. He stood perfectly still, paralyzed between two terrible fates. The race against time was suddenly, terrifyingly real.

The two men stood together against the cold wall of the manor yard. They did not speak for a long moment. They had stood together in the aftermath of plagues, crop failures, and regime changes, but this was the end of it.

Beside Karl, Wilhelm had stopped looking at the arrowhead. The boy was staring at the snow. The lit-up ten-year-old who had run across the yard five minutes ago was gone. In his place was a child who had just learned, here in the shadow of a repurposed manor, that he was leaving the only world he knew—and leaving Karl behind.

Karl didn't say anything. He just reached out and put his hand firmly on Wilhelm's thick woolen shoulder. Wilhelm didn't look up, but he leaned his small weight, just slightly, against Karl's leg.

---

The Isaaks walked home across the fields. The pale sun was already sinking, and the silence among them was heavier than it had ever been. Mother walked ahead with Father, Ernst holding tightly to her skirt while she kept both arms wrapped fiercely around the swaddled newborn against her chest. Michael walked with Christian. Dorothea was between them.

Karl and Johann fell behind. By twenty paces, then thirty.

Johann looked at the rutted tracks beneath his boots, his pace fast and restless. The eagerness was practically vibrating out of him in the cold. "Karl," he said, his voice low but fierce. "Did you hear him? One hundred and sixty acres. Travel allowances, seed grain, a house built on the Tsar's ruble. It’s an estate, Karl. We could go too. As soon as the snow melts."

Karl stared at the frozen post-road. "It's a single estate, Johann. It can't be divided. Michael gets it all. You and I would just be working his dirt until we have to go find our own."

"It's better than waiting here for the Polish draft board to come for him!" Johann shot back, his breath pluming in frustration. "It’s a way out."

"A way out to what?" Karl asked quietly. "It's a loan, not a gift. You heard Father. To get the visas, we'll have to sell everything for nothing. We'd be sleeping under a wagon in the winter, and we'd be in debt to an Emperor."

"There's no war there," Johann insisted, refusing to let the dream die.

Karl didn't answer. In his pocket, his freezing fingers closed around the French sou. He had been carrying it for nearly two years now, rubbing it with his thumb whenever he was thinking, until Napoleon's profile was almost entirely worn away. The Roman wreath was still there, faintly. The emperor's face was gone, wiped blank by time and friction. Empires fade, Karl thought. Only the dirt remains.

They reached the farmyard.

Father stopped. The family stopped behind him.

He stood in the center of the snow-covered yard and looked at the *Wohnstallhaus*—the long timber-framed building that housed their family on one end and their cattle on the other. It was the house he had built with his own hands ten years ago. He looked at the thick clay-and-straw daub packed between the wooden beams, remembering the freezing autumn weeks he and Mother had spent pressing it into place so the children wouldn't freeze.

He looked past the barn to the rye fields, buried under a thick blanket of white. He remembered the grueling, back-breaking agony of clearing it, hauling stones out of the earth yard by yard until his hands bled, just to force a harvest from it. And beyond the rye, at the edge of the tree line, were the three small wooden crosses marking the typhus winter—neighbors they had buried when the ground was like iron, because there was no one else left to dig.

This dirt was his life's work. It held his sweat, his youth, and his dead.

To leave it meant walking away from everything they had built, stripping themselves of their wealth just to bribe a clerk, and plunging into a wild, untamed steppe hundreds of miles away to start from nothing.

But to stay meant losing Michael to the Tsar’s army. It meant feeding his sons to the same senseless, violent machine that had broken the French sergeants on the road.

Father stood perfectly still for a long time. The howling January wind caught the edge of his long wool coat.

He turned to face his family. The silent exhaustion that had worn him down over the winter was gone, replaced by a cold, absolute clarity. He looked at his eldest son, whose name was already on a Polish draft list, and then at the tiny, swaddled shape of his newborn daughter in his wife's arms. He still did not know how to save them both. He only knew that standing still meant losing them.

"Michael," Father said, his voice flat and heavy in the wind. "Keep your boots on."

Michael blinked, his face tight with tension. "Father?"

"Tomorrow morning, we go into Pabianice," Father said, staring down the frozen track. "We find the magistrate. We need to know exactly how much silver it costs to buy an exit visa, and how many months he will make us wait to hold it."
