## Chapter Three

# The Tsar's Manifesto

Pabianice, Duchy of Warsaw — January 1814

---

The winter sky over Pabianice was the exact color of Father’s greying temples—a flat, unyielding iron.

There was no bell to call them over the snowdrifts. Father broke the trail. The rest of them followed in his bootprints in a thin line, breath torn away white over their shoulders. On Sunday mornings, the household bundled into their Sunday best for the hour-long trek into town, but the clothes offered little comfort against the Polish winter. Karl, seventeen, and Johann, fifteen, wore Michael’s outgrown coats, the stiff woolen cuffs riding uncomfortably high on their raw wrists.

Mother walked slower than usual. Beneath her thick shawl, held against her chest to share her body heat, was tiny Anna Maria—just weeks old and the newest Isaak. Mother carried her hymnal tucked awkwardly under her arm. They marched in silence through the frozen, snow-crusted ruts.

Karl read the fields by their silhouettes the way he read faces. The Leischners' chimney smoking. The Flegels' roofline heavy with snow. He looked for smoke at the Hille and Banko households, and at the Kisons' small farm near the tree line. The first Cossacks had appeared the March after the retreat. The Russians had held the country ever since. The war had moved west, but the empty houses along their road were still empty.

The straggling line emerged from the dark pine forests, and the thatched roofs of Pabianice came into view along the frozen Dobrzynka River. Above them stood the Zamek, the old fortified manor of the Kraków Chapter—government property now—with its whitewashed walls and the single hall the Lutherans used as a chapel.

It had been standing for nearly two and a half centuries. Sandstone frames surrounded the tall windows; above them, the high parapet hid the roof. Karl could pick it out long before he could see the people gathering at its doors.

![Pabianice Castle, 1814](/img/photos/1814-pabianice-castle.jpg)

A covered sleigh stood in the manor yard, its team blanketed and steaming—horses better fed than any Karl had seen on their road—and a driver stamping his feet beside it. Karl looked at the sleigh, and then at Father, who had already looked away.

They shuffled through the heavy wooden doors on the south wall, out of the wind into a cold stone vestibule, and climbed the narrow stair to the great hall on the upper floor.

Inside, the chill rose through the soles of their boots. The men sat on the right and the women on the left in the old way, crowding onto rough, backless wooden benches the colonists had dragged in themselves. Dark wooden beams crossed the ceiling. Winter light fell through five tall windows onto the heads of the congregation. The room smelled of old plaster and cold dust, and the ornate fireplace in the corner stood unlit. Nothing about the hall had been made for them except the little table at its end, covered with a linen cloth. Karl sat beside Father, opposite Mother across the aisle. There was room to spare on benches that had been full before the typhus.

Near the end of the service, Pastor Schramm, his face raw from the cold of the long ride from Piotrków, stood behind the table. His breath plumed in the freezing air. He opened his heavy Bible to the Book of Genesis, looked out over his shivering congregation, and read a single verse.

*"Now the Lord had said unto Abram, Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee."*

He closed the book and stepped aside.

The stranger Karl had been watching from the corner of his eye stood up from the front bench and walked to the front of the room. He wore a thick, forest-green greatcoat, its brass buttons gleaming like cold coins in the dim winter light.

When he spoke, it was in fluent, heavily accented German. He unrolled a sheet of crown paper bearing the black, two-headed eagle stamp of Tsar Alexander I.

The agent announced a manifesto of Tsar Alexander, signed on November 29, 1813, inviting colonists to settle on the southern crown lands.

He spoke of a vast, untouched steppe on the Black Sea. A region the Tsar's men called *Bessarabien*.

First, the land. Sixty *dessiatines* to every full family—more than a hundred and sixty acres—granted, the paper said, *in perpetual and hereditary possession*. A murmur ran down the men's side of the aisle. Karl tried to picture that much ground with no neighbor's boundary running through it. The agent waited for the room to quiet before he went on.

Then the Crown’s provision. "To needy families," the agent read, "a loan for your establishment—two hundred and seventy paper rubles." There would also be bread money for those without provisions: five kopecks a day for each person, until the first harvest. Karl caught the clause the man read a little faster than the rest: *from the day of arrival in Russia*.

"And until we reach Russia?" Karl asked.

Father turned toward him. Karl kept his eyes on the agent.

"You must be able to bring your family to the Russian border."

The agent lowered the paper. "The Governor-General in Warsaw has assured us that these advances will be paid in silver. Each family is also to receive oxen, horses, and milk cows upon arrival."

Karl watched him. He was no longer reading.

"And you will not be asked to repay one ruble," the agent continued, "until ten years have passed. After that, you will have another ten years to repay the Crown."

"For ten years," the agent said, "you will be free of taxes and duties."

Father bent forward.

Ten harvests. Karl tried to imagine even one without Father setting aside what the collectors would take. Grain for their own bread, for the next sowing. Enough to keep another cow through winter.

The agent mentioned a small payment to the local leaseholders, then passed to the next promise.

"You may build your own churches, keep your own pastors, and worship according to your faith."

Karl looked toward Pastor Schramm, whose journey from Piotrków had been longer than theirs. A pastor who lived among them. A church they would not have to share with government offices.

Father settled back, his big scarred hands on his knees. Karl knew the story of the journey from Brandenburg: the land, the free years, the promises that had brought Father and Mother east. They had broken their backs turning scrub into fields. Now they were being offered another place to begin.

Across the aisle, hidden beneath Mother's shawl, little Maria stirred. Her small whimper carried through the quiet room.

Father looked at the bundle. Mother eased the shawl away from Maria's face, checked her, and tucked it back.

Then the agent looked up from the paper and delivered the final term.

Permanent exemption from military conscription—for them, and for their sons, and for their sons' sons, forever. No recruiter would ever take a colonist's boy.

A collective breath seemed to leave the men’s side of the room.

Karl turned toward Michael. His brother was staring straight ahead, his jaw clenched so tightly the muscle jumped beneath his skin. He was twenty. Karl thought of the men who had passed their farm the previous winter, wrapped in rags, stumbling west through the snow. He could imagine Michael among them.

He wanted to see his brother grow old.

Father had turned toward Michael too. For the first time that morning, Karl hoped the agent would keep talking. There might be something more—some way to reach the Russian border without money they did not have.

Around them, men who had listened without speaking began to ask questions. How far was Bessarabia? When could the wagons leave? Karl looked across the aisle at Mother, at the sleeping child beneath her shawl. If the roads had been clear, he thought, some of these families would have left before the week was out.

The agent raised a hand. "His Majesty’s invitation does not release you from your obligations here, gentlemen. Your rents, taxes, and debts must be paid before you may obtain permission to depart."

Father closed his eyes.

They sang *Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott* to close the service. Karl knew every word, but twice he lost his place.

---

After the service, the congregation spilled out of the doors and into the pale winter sun. They stood in the Old Market square, an unpaved expanse of dirt that had frozen into treacherous ridges of mud. The men stood in tight, hushed knots, their breath rising in white clouds against the manor's weathered plaster walls. At the far end of the yard, near the old west gate where a bridge crossed to the river, the younger children played a quiet game in the packed snow, Dorothea watching over Ernst, who was bundled so thickly in wool he could barely waddle.

The Leischners found the Isaaks near the frozen banks of the Dobrzynka.

Katherina Leischner went straight to Mother, taking her arm in a tight, desperate grip, careful not to press against the sleeping newborn. "I know, Lieschen," Katherina whispered, her eyes wet but fierce. "I know."

While the women held each other, Wilhelm Leischner, ten years old, came running toward Karl across the icy dirt, his nose bright red from the cold.

"Karl, look," Wilhelm said, pulling a chipped, grey-brown stone from his heavy coat. "Down in the old moat ditch, where the wind blew the snow clear. It has to be an old arrowhead, doesn't it?"

Karl took the freezing stone and turned it in his bare hand.

"Show me which end you think was the point."

Wilhelm leaned close, tracing an edge with his finger. For a moment there was only the stone between them. Karl turned it once more and handed it back. Over Wilhelm's shoulder, he could see Johann talking with Rosina Leischner beside the manor wall, his hands tucked into the sleeves of his outgrown coat.

Then Mr. Leischner spoke.

He stood before Father, square-shouldered in his dark wool frock coat, but his deep, careful voice was stripped of its usual measure.

"We have survived the French, Michel," Mr. Leischner said quietly. "We survived the typhus. But we will not survive this peace."

Father looked at his oldest friend. "You are leaving."

"Katherina and I have decided," Leischner said, gesturing back toward the heavy doors of the manor. "Too many of our neighbors are in the ground. I will not bury another child in this dirt, and I will not wait for another army to empty the barns. We mean to leave when the spring roads are dry, if we can settle what we owe by then."

"There is still rent to pay," Father said. "And the taxes. A Russian promise will not settle those accounts."

Leischner nodded slowly. "I know. We will have to sell the heavy plows. The surplus grain. The extra livestock. Everything we cannot put in the wagon."

"Sell to whom?" Father said. "Half the colonies from here to Łaznów will be selling the same plows in the same week." He looked toward the road. "And you still have horses to pull your wagon. The hospitals in Warsaw have mine."

Leischner had no answer to that.

Father glanced over his shoulder at Michael, who stood silently near the frozen riverbank, a little apart from everyone.

Then he looked down at the fragile, shifting bundle of Anna Maria against Mother's chest, and said nothing.

"How do I choose?" Father said quietly. "Take her on that road this spring? Or stay here and hope the next man who wants a driver leaves Michael alone?"

Mother drew her shawl closer around Maria. "She is not going on that road this spring, Michel."

Father didn't answer.

Beside Karl, Wilhelm had stopped looking at the arrowhead. The boy stared at the snow, the stone held loose in his hand.

"Will you come too?" he asked.

Karl looked at Father.

"I don't know yet."

Wilhelm closed his fingers around the stone. Karl reached out and put his hand firmly on the boy's thick woolen shoulder. Wilhelm didn't look up, but he leaned his small weight, just slightly, against Karl's leg.

---

The Isaaks walked home across the fields. The pale sun was already sinking, and the silence among them was heavier than it had ever been. Mother walked ahead with Father, keeping both arms around the swaddled newborn. Michael carried Ernst. Christian and Dorothea walked beside him.

Karl and Johann fell behind. By twenty paces, then thirty.

Johann looked at the rutted tracks beneath his boots, his pace fast and restless. He kept glancing ahead to see whether Father could hear. "Karl," he said, his voice low but fierce. "Did you hear him? Two hundred and fifty Morgen. Silver for the road, horses, cows. Money to build a house. It’s an estate, Karl. We could go too. As soon as the snow melts."

"Not for the road," Karl said. "The bread money starts when we reach Russia. And he read out paper rubles. He only spoke of silver afterward."

"Two hundred and fifty Morgen," Johann said again.

"For the whole family. One farm. Father means Michael to have ours. Why would it be different there?"

"We could have families of our own one day," Johann said. "And farms. It's a way out."

"A way out to what?" Karl asked quietly. "It's a loan, not a gift. You heard Father. To get the papers, we'll have to sell everything for nothing. What would we live on until we got there? And then we'd be in debt to an Emperor."

"There's no war there," Johann insisted, refusing to let the dream die.

Karl didn't answer. In his pocket, his numb fingers closed around the French sou. Napoleon's profile was worn almost smooth. The man who had given it to him had been on his way to Russia.

They reached the farmyard.

Father stopped. The family stopped behind him.

He stood in the middle of the snow-covered yard and looked at the house—the long, whitewashed wooden house he had helped raise when Karl was a little boy, its clay chinking pressed into the seams between the timbers one freezing autumn so the children inside wouldn't feel the wind. He looked past the byre and the barn to the rye fields under their blanket of white. Karl remembered helping him clear the last of that ground, hauling at roots that would not come free, going back the next morning to haul again. And beyond the rye, at the tree line, the three small wooden crosses of the typhus winter, over graves dug when the ground was like iron because there had been no one else left to dig.

Father stood a long time. The howling January wind caught the edge of his long wool coat.

At last he turned to Michael, then looked at the baby in Mother's arms.

"Michael," Father said, his voice flat in the wind. "Until I hold Russian paper in my hand, you stay off the post-road and out of the town."

Michael's jaw tightened. A year ago he had argued. He did not argue now.

"Karl." Father was already looking down the frozen track toward Pabianice. "Keep your boots by the door. Tomorrow morning we take our accounts into town. You asked what it will take to get us there. Now we'll find out what we still owe before they'll let us leave."
