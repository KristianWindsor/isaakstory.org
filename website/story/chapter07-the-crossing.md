## Chapter Seven

# The Crossing

The Road to Odessa — February 1878

---

August came with the cart at dawn.

He had driven through the night from Kulm — fifteen miles in the dark, the horse picking its way along frozen ruts by moonlight — because August was the kind of man who would rather lose a night of sleep than risk being late for something that mattered. He pulled up in front of Gottlieb's house just as the sky was going grey in the east, and he sat on the bench and waited without knocking, because the lamp was already lit inside and he could hear the family moving.

They had been packing since before the sun went down. Louisa had not slept. She had spent the final hours walking through the empty rooms of the house — the house that was no longer hers, that had been sold to a family from Tarutino along with the tools and the livestock and everything else that would not fit in two trunks — touching the walls, the doorframe, the window ledge where she had set a candle every Christmas for eighteen years. She did not cry. She had cried earlier, in private, and she was finished with it now.

Gottlieb carried the trunks out. Jacob helped. David helped. Daniel, who was fifteen and furious at being treated like a child, carried the smaller bags with an exaggerated display of strength that fooled no one. Regina, twenty-two, moved through the house behind her mother, checking the rooms one last time, closing doors that would never need to be closed again.

Solomon stood in the yard, watching everything. He was twelve, and he had not stopped talking since the previous afternoon. "Where does the ship leave from? How big is the ship? How long does it take to cross the ocean? What language do they speak in America? Will there be Indians?" David, who had appointed himself Solomon's handler, answered each question with the patience of an older brother who has long since accepted that silence is not an option.

Magdalena came out last. She was nine, holding her younger sister Emilie by the hand — Emilie was three and had been crying since she woke up, not because she understood what was happening but because the house was empty and empty houses frightened her. Wilhelmina, ten, walked behind them, carrying a cloth bag with the hymnal and a wooden doll that had been hers since infancy and that she had refused, absolutely and without negotiation, to leave behind.

Louisa carried Antonie. The baby — not quite two — slept against her mother's shoulder, oblivious to the fact that she was leaving the only country she would never remember.

Ten Isaaks. Two trunks. One Bible, one hymnal, and the clothes on their backs.

---

The Schulzes arrived while August was still loading.

They came down the lane in two carts — Louisa's father Johann on the bench of the first one, her brother Wilhelm on the bench of the second, and between them the entire remainder of the Schulz family in Sophiental: Louisa's mother Christiana, Wilhelm's wife Susanne, and Wilhelm's eight children ranging from Friedrich, who was eighteen and already as tall as his father, to Christian, who was six months old and wrapped in three layers of wool against his mother's chest.

Louisa came out of the house and stopped on the step.

Her father climbed down from the cart. He was seventy-nine now, and the climb down was slower than it had been even six months ago, and when his boots hit the frozen ground he stood for a moment with one hand on the cart's rail before he let go. On the bed of the cart behind him, lashed down with rope, was the wooden trunk from Poland. She had watched him pack it a week ago. She had not expected to see it again until Bremen.

"I thought you were going to meet us at the station," she said.

"I decided not to."

"Father —"

"Your mother wanted to leave from the same door as you. She was quite firm about it." Johann Schulz looked past Louisa at the house behind her — the whitewashed walls, the dark windows, the door standing open to the empty *Stube* — and his face did something she had not seen it do before. Then it stopped doing it. "Your brother and I agreed. We come together or we do not come. We are coming together."

Christiana, still in the cart, held out both hands toward her daughter without speaking. Louisa walked over and took them. Her mother's hands were cold through the wool mittens and smaller than Louisa remembered, the way her mother had been getting smaller for about five years now, a little at a time, as though she were gradually folding herself away for transport.

"*Liebchen*," her mother said.

"*Ja, Mutter.*"

"I have never been on a ship."

"Neither have I."

"Good. We will learn together."

From the second cart, Wilhelm was already climbing down to help unload. Wilhelm was forty-three, a carpenter by trade like his father, a practical man who spoke only when there was something to say and who had said almost nothing about the decision to leave because he had not needed to — his wife Susanne had looked at the Dakota letters once, in October, and said *yes*, and that had been the whole of the Schulz family conversation. Now he was lifting the big trunk off his own cart with his eldest son Friedrich while his middle sons — Daniel, sixteen; Simon, thirteen — hung back, unsure whether they were supposed to help or stay out of the way. Lydia, twelve, and Wilhelmina, ten, stood together near the back of the cart, holding hands, watching the Isaak children with the careful attention of country girls meeting their cousins on a morning that was unlike any other morning in their lives. Solomon Schulz, who was six, had already found Solomon Isaak in the yard and the two of them had disappeared around the side of the barn within thirty seconds of arrival, because that is what small boys named Solomon do when there are two of them.

Little Louise — four years old, named for her aunt — stood in the cart bed holding the hem of her mother's coat, sucking her thumb, staring at everything.

Gottlieb came out of the house with the last of the bags and stopped when he saw the carts.

"I thought —"

"So did I," Louisa said.

He walked over to his father-in-law and extended his hand. Johann Schulz took it, and the two men stood there for a moment in the grey morning light, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the gesture had not already said. Gottlieb had been afraid — privately, without telling anyone, not even Louisa — that the old man would not survive the road to Odessa, let alone the ocean. He was still afraid. But he had been afraid alone, and now he was afraid in the presence of another man who was also afraid, and somehow that was easier.

"We ride together as far as Bremen," Johann Schulz said. "After that we ride together until we stop."

"*Ja*," Gottlieb said.

"*Ja*," Johann said, and let go of his hand, and went to help Wilhelm with the trunk.

---

They loaded both carts in the grey light. August and Wilhelm working together on the heavier bags, the older boys pitching in, the younger children finding places among the trunks the way children always find places, by argument and negotiation and the occasional sharp elbow. Louisa climbed onto the bench of August's cart next to her husband. Christiana rode in Wilhelm's cart, wrapped in a blanket, with little Louise in her lap. Gottlieb walked. Jacob and David walked. Daniel walked, though he made it clear through the particular angle of his shoulders that he would have preferred to ride.

The carts turned south toward Akkerman, and Sophiental fell behind them, house by house, until it was just a line of low rooftops against the steppe sky, and then it was nothing at all.

---

They reached Akkerman by midday.

The road came down from the steppe into the town along the shore of the Dniester estuary — a broad, flat arm of water, grey-green and still, stretching east toward the Black Sea — and as they descended, the fortress appeared.

Solomon Isaak saw it first. "What is *that*?"

It rose from the rocky shore like something out of a story that no one in the family had ever been told — massive walls of white limestone, fifteen meters high in places, stretching for what seemed like miles along the water's edge. Towers — round ones, square ones, some intact and some half-crumbled — punctuated the walls at intervals, and behind the walls the citadel loomed, its own towers rising above the rest like a castle within a castle. The whole structure was enormous and ancient and utterly unlike anything in the German colonies, where the tallest building was the church steeple and the oldest thing was a fifty-year-old acacia tree.

"It's the fortress," Gottlieb said. "Akkerman. It's been here since before the Turks."

He did not say more, because Akkerman held other memories for him — the stone courtroom, the judges, the contract — and he did not want to think about those things on this morning, on this road, heading in this direction. But Solomon could not stop staring, and Magdalena could not stop staring, and even Daniel, who was pretending not to be impressed by anything, turned his head as they passed and watched the walls slide by with the involuntary attention of a boy seeing something that is simply too large to ignore.

They did not stop. They did not enter the town. The carts rolled south along the coast road, and the fortress receded behind them — first the towers, then the walls, then just a pale smear of limestone against the grey sky.

---

South of Akkerman, the road followed the coast, and for a few miles they could see the Black Sea.

The children had never seen it. Solomon and Daniel scrambled to the back of the cart to look. Even Emilie stopped crying and stared, her small mouth open, at the flat grey expanse stretching to the horizon. It was the biggest thing any of them had ever seen — bigger than the steppe, bigger than the sky, bigger than anything they had been taught to imagine.

"That's the Black Sea," David said to Solomon, who was gripping the side of the cart with both hands.

"It doesn't look black."

"It's the name."

"Why is it called that if it's not black?"

David did not know. Nobody knew. The sea was grey and green and silver, and it moved in slow, flat swells that caught the light and scattered it, and the wind coming off it smelled like salt and fish and distance, and the gulls wheeled above it, crying in voices that sounded nothing like the hawks of the steppe.

Louisa looked at the water and then looked away. She had never been comfortable with large things she could not understand, and the sea was both. Gottlieb watched her face and said nothing, but he thought about the ocean — the real ocean, the Atlantic, the one they would cross in a few weeks — and he thought, quite clearly, that he could not swim. He had never learned. There had been nowhere on the steppe to learn. He was forty-three years old and he was about to put his wife, his nine children, his wife's elderly parents, and his wife's brother's entire family on a ship, and if that ship went down he would not be able to save any of them, not one. He looked at Louisa's profile against the grey water and did not say any of this out loud.

---

**Odessa**

They reached Odessa in the late afternoon — sixty-five miles from Sophiental — and the carts pulled up at the railway station, and August climbed down and stood in the street and looked at his brother.

This was where they parted. August would drive the cart back to Kulm, sell the horse, settle his own affairs, and follow in six months. Gottlieb would board a train heading west, toward Bremen, toward a ship, toward the other side of the world.

The brothers did not embrace. They stood facing each other in the noise and dust of the Odessa street — two middle-aged farmers from the steppe, sunburned and rough-handed, dressed in their church clothes because they owned nothing better — and they said the things men say when they are trying not to say the thing that matters.

"Write when you get there," August said.

"I will."

"Tell Johann I'll be behind you."

"I will."

August looked at the children in the cart — at Solomon, who was already asking questions about the train station; at Magdalena, who was watching him with her wide, quiet eyes; at little Antonie asleep on Regina's shoulder. He looked at Louisa, who met his gaze and nodded once, firmly, the way she did everything.

"Take care of them," August said.

"I always have."

August climbed back onto the cart. He picked up the reins. He looked at Gottlieb one more time, and then he turned the horse and drove north, back toward the steppe, back toward Kulm, back toward the life he had six more months of living before he would stand in this same street and board this same train and follow his brothers west.

Gottlieb watched him go. Then he turned to his family — to Louisa, to Regina, to Jacob and David and Daniel and Solomon and Magdalena and Wilhelmina and Emilie and Antonie, and to the Schulzes gathered with their own trunks a few yards away — and he said, in a voice that was not loud but that carried: "Let's pray."

They bowed their heads right there on the platform, twenty-one people standing among their trunks while the crowd of a Russian port city flowed around them like water around stones. Gottlieb prayed in German — the plain, direct German of the Lutheran colonies, without flourish, without poetry, just a man asking God to protect his family on a journey that was too big for any man to manage alone. He asked for safe passage. He asked for strength. He asked for the courage to keep walking when the road got dark, and he asked — this last part quietly, almost to himself — for forgiveness for leaving the place where his parents were buried, and for the faith to believe that the place he was going would be worth what it cost to get there.

Louisa said *Amen*. The children said *Amen*. Johann Schulz said *Amen* in a voice that was older than any of theirs, and Wilhelm said *Amen* beside him, and Solomon Isaak said *Amen* slightly too loud, the way he did everything.

They boarded the train.

---

**The Train to Bremen**

The railway connected Odessa to the European network, and the journey that had taken Gottlieb's grandfather four months by wagon would now take days. The children pressed their faces to the windows. Most of them had never been on a train. Solomon Isaak kept asking how fast they were going, and Daniel Isaak kept inventing numbers, until Louisa told them both, in the voice, to sit down and be quiet.

They crossed the border out of the Russian Empire. No ceremony. No farewell. Just a change in the uniforms of the officials and a stamp on the passport and a line on a map that meant they were no longer subjects of the Tsar — that Russia was finished with them and they with it. Gottlieb felt the moment pass through him like a shudder, though he said nothing about it and no one noticed.

Johann Schulz noticed. He was sitting across the aisle with Christiana asleep against his shoulder, and when he saw Gottlieb's face he reached across and tapped his son-in-law's knee once, and then left his hand there for a moment before withdrawing it. It was the closest thing to an embrace that had passed between the two men in eighteen years of marriage, and Gottlieb would remember it for the rest of his life.

Austria-Hungary. The bread tasted different. The language on the platform signs changed. The officials wore different uniforms but looked at them with the same indifference.

Then Germany.

Germany was not what they expected. It was green. It had trees — not the stunted, wind-bent acacias the colonists had planted on the steppe, but forests of them, thick and old and everywhere. The towns were close together, built of brick and stone, and there were factories with chimneys that smoked day and night, and the trains ran on time, and the language on the station signs was the same language they spoke at home but also somehow different — faster, sharper, full of words they didn't know. The Bessarabian Germans were steppe people, a century behind the industrialized nation that Germany had become. They passed through it like visitors from another age.

Jacob stood at the window for hours, watching the countryside, and did not speak. David noticed, the way David noticed everything, that his older brother's eyes were wet, and he said nothing about it. It was the Fatherland. They had heard about it their entire lives — in hymns, in stories, in the way their grandparents said the word *Deutschland* with a particular softness, like a prayer for a place they would never see again. And here it was, green and prosperous and utterly foreign, and they were passing through it on a train, headed somewhere else.

Magdalena pressed her nose to the glass and fogged it with her breath and drew pictures in the fog with her finger — houses, trees, a horse. Louisa pulled her back and wiped the glass with her sleeve, but Magdalena did it again a minute later, and Louisa let her.

---

**Bremerhaven**

Bremen. The end of Europe.

The Norddeutscher Lloyd operated out of Bremerhaven, where passengers were processed, housed, and medically examined before boarding. The through-ticket they had purchased covered everything — the train to the port, the Atlantic crossing, and the railway from New York to Dakota. It was the most expensive thing Gottlieb had ever purchased. It represented the entire proceeds of a liquidated life.

They were housed in emigrant barracks near the quay — long, clean, institutional rooms where families slept in rows, waiting for their ship. The barracks smelled like carbolic soap and boiled potatoes and the particular anxiety of people who have sold everything they own and are now waiting for the next thing to happen. Solomon Isaak made friends with a boy from a Swabian family headed for Nebraska. Emilie cried most of the first night and would not be consoled until Regina took her outside and showed her the ships in the harbor — enormous iron-hulled vessels with smokestacks and masts, the largest things any of them had ever seen.

The city itself, the part of Bremen they had walked through on the way to the barracks, was a shock to noses that had spent their lives on a steppe where the wind carried everything away. Bremen in February smelled like horses — thousands of them, pulling cabs and drays and private carriages through streets that had not been cleaned since the last good rain — and like coal smoke and river mud and the sour, closed-up smell of too many people living on top of each other. Solomon Isaak held his nose theatrically for the first half-mile and then gave up because his arm got tired. Little Louise Schulz, walking between her mother and her aunt, asked in a whisper whether the whole city smelled like this, and Susanne said yes, and Louise said she wanted to go home, and Susanne picked her up and did not answer.

On the second evening, Gottlieb gathered both families in their corner of the barracks — the Isaaks and the Schulzes together, twenty-one people in a cluster of bunks — and read from the Bible. He chose Psalm 107: *Those who go down to the sea in ships, who do business on great waters; they see the works of the Lord, and His wonders in the deep.* His voice, reading the familiar German words in this unfamiliar place, drew the attention of the families in the neighboring bunks, who stopped what they were doing and listened, because the words were the same words they all knew, and the fear was the same fear they all carried, and for a few minutes the barracks was not a holding pen for emigrants but a church without walls.

Johann Schulz sat on the edge of a bunk with his hands folded over his knees and his eyes closed and his lips moving silently along with the German.

The ship was the SS *Mosel*. The Isaaks and the Schulzes were in steerage, with six hundred others.

---

**The Atlantic**

The *Mosel* departed Bremerhaven and steamed down the coast to Southampton, where she took on English passengers and coal.

Solomon stood at the rail as the ship entered the English Channel and watched the water with an expression that shifted, over the course of an hour, from excitement to awe to something that looked very much like fear. He had seen the Black Sea from the road — flat and grey and far away — but this was different. This was beneath him. The ship was *on* it, and it was moving, and there was no bottom he could see and no shore he could reach and the water stretched in every direction without end. He gripped the rail and did not let go until David came and stood beside him and put a hand on his shoulder and said, very calmly, "It's just water."

"It's not just water," Solomon said. "It's all the water."

David looked at the Channel — the grey-green chop, the distant smudge of the English coast, the sails and smokestacks of other ships — and he could not argue with this.

Steerage was below the waterline, in compartments fitted with rows of narrow wooden bunks stacked three high. The air was close — a permanent fog of body heat, damp wool, engine grease, and the sweetish stench of seasickness. The engines throbbed through the iron hull day and night, a vibration you felt in your teeth. Six hundred people in a space designed for efficiency, not comfort, rolling west across the Atlantic at thirteen knots.

The Isaaks and Schulzes had claimed adjacent bunks in one corner of the compartment, which meant that twenty-one people slept and ate and were seasick within arm's reach of each other for ten days. Christiana Schulz got sick first — on the second day, hard, and did not get up for four days. Louisa moved between her own bunk and her mother's, sponging her mother's forehead with water from a tin cup, holding the cup to her lips, murmuring in the quiet voice of a daughter who had decided, without discussion, that this was her job now. She herself was sick — a steady, grinding nausea — but she refused to be in her bunk while her mother was in hers, and so she worked through it with her jaw set, the way she had worked through every hard thing in her life.

On the fourth day Christiana opened her eyes and whispered *Wasser*, and Louisa held the cup, and her mother drank, and then her mother said, in a voice too thin to carry: "I will not die on this ship, *Liebchen*. I have decided." And Louisa, who had been awake for most of four nights, put her face down against the edge of her mother's bunk and wept silently for about thirty seconds, and then she lifted her head and wiped her face on her sleeve and went to check on Antonie.

Regina took over the younger children. She had been her mother's deputy for years, and she moved through the compartment with quiet competence — feeding Emilie, keeping Solomon from climbing the bunk ladders, making sure Wilhelmina ate something even when the ship rolled and the smell made eating feel like an act of defiance. Susanne Schulz did the same on her side of the bunks, with Lydia and her own Wilhelmina helping, and between the two older women the twelve younger children were kept alive and reasonably clean through a week that none of them would ever fully remember.

Jacob helped without being asked and never mentioned it. David studied the rigging, asked a deckhand in broken gestures how the engine worked, and came back with information that no one had requested but that Solomon, at least, found fascinating. Friedrich Schulz, eighteen and the oldest of Wilhelm's children, appointed himself the runner between the two family groups, carrying messages and cups of water and occasional small bundles of food from one set of bunks to the other.

Gottlieb spent hours on deck, which was technically reserved for the higher classes, but the crew did not enforce the boundaries strictly, and Gottlieb was the kind of man whose quiet persistence made people step aside rather than argue.

The Atlantic was not the Black Sea. He had known this in theory, but knowing it and standing in the middle of it were different things entirely. The Black Sea had been large but comprehensible — you could stand on the shore at Akkerman and see the curve of the coast, the line of the estuary, the fishing boats coming in at evening. It was a sea with edges. The Atlantic had no edges. It moved without reference to anything human — calm one hour and violent the next, the water heaving in long, slow swells that lifted the bow and dropped it and lifted it again, endlessly, day after day, as though the ocean were breathing and the ship were a thing caught in its throat.

It was beautiful. It was terrifying. It was both at once.

On the seventh day, Magdalena came up on deck and stood beside him. She did not say anything. She was nine, and she had already learned the Isaak habit of standing next to someone in silence as a form of conversation. They watched the water together — the grey-green swells, the white crests, the occasional shadow beneath the surface that might have been a whale or a trick of the light. After a long time, she reached up and took his hand, and they stood like that until Louisa sent Daniel to fetch them for supper.

That night, in the bunk, Louisa lay awake with Antonie asleep against her chest. The baby's breath was warm and steady against her collarbone. Louisa could feel the ship's iron hull shuddering beneath her, and she could hear the ocean through it — not loudly, just a low continuous pressure that was never quite silent — and she thought, very clearly, for the first time since the morning they had loaded the carts in Sophiental: *I may have killed her. I may have killed all of them.* She put her lips against the top of Antonie's head and did not move. The baby slept on, warm, oblivious, trusting. Louisa stayed awake until dawn.

On the tenth night, with the ship rolling in heavy seas and the steerage compartment groaning and retching around them, Gottlieb knelt beside the family's bunks and prayed again. The children were awake — all of them, even Emilie, even Antonie — because sleep was impossible when the floor tilted thirty degrees in each direction and the timbers screamed. He prayed the Lord's Prayer in German, and one by one the children joined in, their voices thin and frightened in the dark — *Vater unser im Himmel, geheiligt werde dein Name* — and from the bunks around them, other families joined too, other German voices, other Lutheran voices, the prayer spreading through the compartment like a candle being passed from hand to hand.

In the bunk above Gottlieb's, Louisa opened her eyes in the dark. She did not join the prayer out loud. She reached her hand down over the edge of her bunk and felt for her husband's, and when she found it — calloused, familiar, steady — she held it, and he held hers back, and neither of them let go until the prayer was over.

The ship brought them through the night.

---

**New York Harbor — April 13, 1878**

They came up on deck in the grey light of early morning and saw America.

No Statue of Liberty — not yet. Lady Liberty would not be dedicated for another eight years. What they saw instead was the harbor: a forest of masts and smokestacks, ferries and tugs and barges in every direction, and beyond them the skyline of lower Manhattan — not the skyscrapers of the next century, but a dense wall of brick and stone that rose from the waterline like a cliff.

Solomon stood on his toes at the rail, trying to count the ships, and gave up at forty. David stood beside him, quiet for once, his hand on his younger brother's shoulder. Magdalena watched from behind them, recording everything with her wide, serious eyes.

Johann Schulz came up on deck with his wife on his arm. Christiana was thinner than she had been ten days ago, and paler, and she moved with the particular care of a woman who had decided she was going to walk off this ship on her own feet and who was concentrating every scrap of her strength on that decision. When she reached the rail she let go of her husband's arm and put both hands on the iron and stood looking at the city.

"It stinks," she said.

"Yes, *Mutter*," Louisa said, coming up behind her. "The whole world stinks now, I think."

"Then we will get used to it."

Castle Garden. The old circular fort at the southern tip of Manhattan, converted into an immigrant processing station in 1855. The Isaaks and the Schulzes entered together through the main doors into a vast circular hall echoing with a thousand conversations in a dozen languages. Clerks sat behind counters recording names. Interpreters shouted. Currency exchangers called out rates. The air smelled like disinfectant and sweat and the sea, and, underneath all of it, the horse-smell of the city outside, which was even stronger here than it had been in Bremen — denser, older, baked into the walls.

They registered. Russia, they said, because that was what Bessarabia was, officially, though they had never felt Russian and never would. Farmer. Dakota Territory. A clerk wrote it down. A doctor checked their eyes, their throats, their hands. Healthy. Admitted.

Then they were out — into New York, into the noise and light and impossible density of the largest city in the Western Hemisphere. Louisa pulled Antonie tight against her and held Emilie's hand and did not let go of either until they were inside the railway station. Jacob carried one trunk on his shoulder. David carried the other. Friedrich Schulz shouldered his own family's trunk with the easy pride of an eighteen-year-old given a real job. Solomon and Daniel ranged ahead like scouts, drawn by everything, restrained by nothing except the sound of their father's voice.

They boarded a westbound train. New York vanished behind them before they had time to understand it, which was perhaps for the best.

---

**The Train West**

Four days across a continent so vast it made the Bessarabian steppe look like a garden.

Pennsylvania — green, rolling country, thick with forests and rivers. Gottlieb pressed his face to the window and stared. In Bessarabia, a single tree was an event. Here, trees grew in uncountable millions, covering the hills like fur, and no one seemed to notice.

Pittsburgh at night. The furnaces glowing orange against the dark sky, the rivers reflecting the fires of the steel mills so that the water itself seemed to burn. Solomon pressed his face to the glass and whispered, "Is it always on fire?" and nobody answered because nobody knew.

Chicago. The platform swarming with more people than lived in the entire Akkerman district. A man selling sausages from a cart called out in German — Bavarian German, not the Bessarabian kind — and Gottlieb bought sausages for everyone, Isaaks and Schulzes both, and divided them out on the platform while the train took on coal, and for a moment, standing in the middle of the largest railroad hub in the world, they were just two families eating sausages.

West of Chicago, the country changed.

The forests thinned. The towns shrank. The hills flattened. The green gave way to gold, then to the pale straw of prairie grass in early spring — not yet fully green, the winter brown giving way to the first pale shoots. The sky opened up, wider and wider, until it was the whole world again.

Gottlieb sat up straight. He knew this. He *knew* this landscape — the same treeless plain, the same wind-bent grass, the same enormous sky. Louisa saw it too. She put her hand on his arm and said nothing, because there was nothing to say.

West of the Mississippi, the prairie opened into something beyond language. It was April, and the spring thaw had turned the low places to mud and standing water, and the higher ground was covered in last year's grass, bleached white by winter, with the new green just pushing through. The light was different here — clearer, sharper, throwing long shadows across a landscape that had no end.

Then Solomon saw the antelope.

A herd of them — thirty, maybe forty — standing in the grass a quarter mile from the tracks. Tan-and-white bodies catching the afternoon light. Not deer, not goats, not cattle — something else entirely, something native to this place the way the acacias had been native to the steppe. They stood watching the train with enormous dark eyes that held no fear, only curiosity.

"What are they?" Solomon said, his face flat against the glass.

"*Antilopen*," David said, though these were not the antelope of any book he had read. They were pronghorn — the fastest animals in North America — and they watched the train pass with the calm indifference of creatures who had been watching things cross this prairie for ten thousand years.

The herd broke and ran. Not in panic — in a fluid, coordinated burst of speed that made Solomon gasp — and then they were gone, flowing over a ridge and into the grass as though the prairie had swallowed them.

"I want one," Solomon said.

"You can't have one," Magdalena said, without looking up from the window.

"Why not?"

"Because they're faster than you."

The train pushed north through Minnesota and into the Dakota Territory. At each stop, families disembarked — Germans, Norwegians, Swedes — stepping off into the grass and the wind and the mud, met by a man with a wagon or sometimes by no one at all, just the prairie and the sky and 160 acres of unbroken sod waiting somewhere beyond the horizon.

The April air smelled like wet earth and snowmelt and the sharp green smell of grass deciding, after a long winter, to come back to life.

---

**Yankton, Dakota Territory**

The train reached the end of the line on a Wednesday afternoon.

Gottlieb stepped down onto a wooden platform. The wind hit him immediately — the same wind, the same direction, the same steady, grass-scented pressure that had blown through every day of his childhood in Kulm. He stood on the platform with Louisa and the children and the Schulzes and their trunks, blinking in the afternoon light, and looked out at the Dakota prairie.

Then he saw his brother.

Johann — John, as he called himself now — was standing at the far end of the platform next to a wagon and a pair of horses, squinting against the sun. He was older than Gottlieb remembered — weathered by two Dakota winters, browned by Dakota sun — but he had the same Isaak jaw, the same patient eyes, the same unhurried way of moving that their father Karl had carried from Poland to the steppe. He had done what he said he would do. He had crossed first. He had found the land. And he had come to the station with a wagon, the way he promised, because Johann was the kind of man who kept his promises.

He walked over and picked up the heaviest trunk without a word, as if his brother had been gone for an afternoon instead of two years. Then he said something in the quiet German of the colonies — something about the road being muddy from the thaw — and the sound of that voice, speaking that language, in this place, broke something open in Gottlieb's chest that he had been holding shut since Sophiental.

Louisa looked at the prairie — the flat, treeless, wind-scoured, limitless prairie — and she looked at Johann, and she said, "Show me the house."

"It's sod."

"I've lived in sod before."

"I know you have."

Johann turned and saw the Schulzes coming down the platform behind her, and his eyes widened just perceptibly, and then he recovered and walked over and took Johann Schulz's hand with both of his.

"Father-in-law."

"Son-in-law."

"I did not know you were coming."

"Neither did we, for a long time. And then we did."

Johann Isaak looked at the crowd of Schulzes — Wilhelm, Susanne, the eight children — and then at his brother, and his mouth twitched in what was almost a smile.

"I will need a bigger wagon."

"Tomorrow is soon enough," Gottlieb said.

"Tonight I will get you all to a boarding house in town. Tomorrow we will sort the wagons. The day after we will go to the land."

He took the reins of the wagon. The Isaak children piled in first, finding places among the trunks — Jacob and David on the tailgate with their legs hanging over, Regina holding Antonie, Solomon already asking Johann questions about the quarter section, Magdalena watching the prairie, Daniel pretending not to be impressed, Wilhelmina sitting quietly next to Emilie, who had fallen asleep against a flour sack. The Schulzes followed on foot, Johann Schulz walking slowly with Christiana on his arm, Wilhelm and Friedrich carrying the heavy trunk between them, Susanne shepherding the younger children along the wooden platform.

The wagon rolled west, into the grass, into the wide open nothing that looked almost exactly like the Budschak — the same treeless plain, the same enormous sky, the same wind.

David was quiet for a long time, watching the prairie unfold around them. Then he said, softly: "It looks like home."

From somewhere in the wagon, Solomon was asking what kind of birds those were — the big ones, circling high above the grass.

"Hawks," David said.

"What do they eat?"

"Rabbits, mostly."

"Are there rabbits here?"

"There's everything here."
