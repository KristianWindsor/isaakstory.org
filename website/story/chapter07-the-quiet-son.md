## Chapter Seven

# The Quiet Son

Parkston, Dakota Territory — 1880 to 1887

---

David broke his own ground in the autumn of 1880, on a corner of his father's quarter section that nobody else had gotten around to.

He was twenty years old. He had been working his father's land for two years and his father's father's land for as long as he could remember before that, and he had decided, sometime in the late summer after Emanuel was born, that he wanted a piece of earth that nobody else had a claim on. Not legally — he was too young yet to file his own homestead, and the corner he picked was still officially Gottlieb's. But Gottlieb had said yes when he asked. Gottlieb had said yes the way he said most things to David, which was without making a speech about it.

The corner was three quarters of an acre on the western edge of the section, where the grass grew slightly taller because of how the rain ran off the rise. David walked it once at sunrise to see what he was dealing with, and then he went back to the barn and took down the old plow — not the borrowed one, the one Gottlieb had bought in July with the *Baschtam* money — and he hitched up the team and walked them out to the corner alone.

He stood at the edge of the grass for a moment with the handles of the plow in his hands.

He had watched his father do this in May of '78, with Wilhelm beside him and the boys lined up behind, and he had knelt down in the open furrow and put his hand on his father's shoulder without saying anything because there had been nothing to say. He remembered the smell of the dirt. He remembered his father's face. He had not understood, that day, that he was watching the moment his father became the man he would be in America — but he had felt it, the way you feel a door closing somewhere in a house, and he had carried the feeling with him for two years without a word for it.

He was twenty years old. He wanted his own door.

He set the share. He spoke to the team in the low tone he had learned from his father. The oxen leaned. The plow went in.

The first furrow on his own corner took him forty minutes, because he was working alone and the share kept catching, and twice he had to stop and lever the plow back to the surface with his shoulder under the handle. By the time he turned at the end of the row he was sweating through his shirt and the muscles in his back were singing and the cut earth behind him was a long ragged line in the grass, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever made.

He stood there for a long minute, breathing.

Then he went back to the start and cut the second furrow, and the third, and he kept cutting until the sun was directly overhead and Louisa rang the bell on the porch for dinner, and he drove the team back across the prairie toward the house with his hands shaking from the work and his mouth set in something that was not quite a smile.

He did not say anything about it at dinner. Magdalena, who was twelve and noticed everything, watched him eat and watched him not talk, and after the meal she followed him out to the yard and stood beside him while he washed his hands at the pump.

"You broke ground today," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Your boots. There's prairie dirt on them, not the dirt from Father's field."

He looked at her. She was twelve years old and she had her mother's eyes and her father's stubbornness and a way of standing very still while she figured something out.

"Don't tell anyone," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because I don't want them to come look at it yet."

"Why not?"

He thought about it. He could have given her a dozen reasons, none of them quite right. The real one, when he found it, surprised him.

"Because right now it is mine," he said. "If they come look at it, it will be everybody's."

Magdalena considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything. Then she nodded once and went back inside, and she did not tell anyone, and David broke another quarter acre on his corner the next morning before breakfast.

---

The letter from Bessarabia came on a Tuesday in July of 1882.

It was thinner than most letters from the old country, and the handwriting on the envelope was not Caroline's brother's, who usually wrote the Kulm news. Gottlieb opened it at the kitchen table after the children had gone out, and David, who had come in for water, saw his father's hands stop moving on the paper and knew before his father said anything.

"My mother."

David sat down across from him.

"Two months ago. June twelfth. She was eighty-three." Gottlieb folded the letter in half and then in half again and laid it flat on the table and put his palm on it. "She was eighty-three and she had been ill since the winter and she went in her sleep, my brother says. He says she did not suffer at the end." He looked up at David. His eyes were dry, but the muscles around them were doing something David had not seen before. "I was not there."

"No."

"I was not there for my father either, but I was nineteen then, and a boy, and the choice was not mine. This time the choice was mine. I made it in 1878. I made it the day I sent her a letter saying we had arrived in America and we were not coming back, and she wrote back and said she was glad for us, and she did not say the other thing because she was the kind of woman who did not say the other thing." His hand was still flat on the letter. "She died in the same village she walked into when she was a girl. She is in the ground next to my father, on the hill where the wind never stops. I will never see that hill again."

David did not say anything. He was twenty-two years old, and he had not yet lost anyone the way his father had just lost his mother, and he understood that the right thing to do at this moment was to be in the room and to be quiet and to let his father say the next thing if there was a next thing.

After a long minute his father got up. He went to the shelf where the family Bible sat — the one from Sophiental, the one he had carried in the wooden box across the ocean — and he took it down and laid it open on the table to the front flyleaf.

The flyleaf already had one line on it, in his careful schoolmaster's hand:

*Den 14. September 1878. Die russische Schuld bezahlt.*

Below it, Gottlieb wrote a second line:

*Den 12. Juni 1882. Meine Mutter ist gestorben in Kulm. Anna Elisabeth Louise Schimke Isaak. Sie war 83 Jahre alt.*

*June 12, 1882. My mother died in Kulm. Anna Elisabeth Louise Schimke Isaak. She was 83 years old.*

He set the pen down. He did not close the Bible right away. David watched him look at the two lines together — the debt paid and the mother dead — and he understood, with a clarity that twenty-two-year-olds do not usually have, that his father was beginning to keep a record. That this Bible was going to hold a list. That the list was going to grow.

Gottlieb closed the Bible and put it back on the shelf.

"Tell your mother when she comes in from the garden," he said. "I am going to walk for a while."

He walked west, out across the broken section, toward the corner where David had been cutting his own furrows for two years now without telling anyone except Magdalena. David watched him go and then went back outside to find Louisa.

---

David met Hanna Hetzler at a wedding.

It was June of 1883, and the wedding was for a Schmidt girl from a neighboring farm marrying a Wenzel boy from one of the Tarutino families that had come over the year before. The whole German-Russian community for ten miles around had turned out, because that was what you did for a wedding in the colonies, and after the church service everyone had ridden in wagons to the Schmidt farm for the meal.

There were two long tables under canvas — the same kind of canvas Gottlieb had stretched over the Sunday meal three Junes earlier — and after the eating there was dancing in the yard, and the bride's father had hired a fiddler from Yankton who played the old Bessarabian tunes badly enough that Wilhelm Schulz, listening from the bench by the door, said the man was being paid by the wrong note.

David was not dancing. David did not dance. He was standing near the food table with a piece of bread in his hand, watching his younger siblings dance with the Schmidt cousins, when a young woman walked past him, stopped, walked back, and said in clear unaccented English: "Are you going to eat that bread or use it as a hat?"

David looked at her.

She was about his height. Brown hair pinned up under a small dark hat. A face that was not quite pretty in the way the Bessarabian girls were pretty — sharper, more angular, with a small line between her eyebrows that suggested she was used to thinking about things — and grey eyes that were looking at him directly, the way girls in the colonies almost never looked at men.

"I was going to eat it," he said. In English. He had learned some English in the four years since they arrived, mostly from buying tools at the general store in Parkston, and his English was slow and careful.

"You were going to eat it about ten minutes ago. Now you are just standing there with it. People are starting to wonder."

"What people?"

"Me. I am people."

David looked down at the bread in his hand. He looked back at her. He could not tell whether she was making fun of him or whether she was just talking, and he was not used to either possibility from a woman he had not been introduced to.

"Who are you?" he said, in German now, because he wanted to see what she would do.

"Hanna Hetzler," she said, in German that was perfectly fluent and slightly different from the German he spoke — softer in the consonants, no Bessarabian flatness in the vowels, a German that had grown up in a different country. "My father is Friedrich Hetzler. He is talking to your father over there about pigs. We came over from Eureka for the wedding because my mother knows the bride's mother from before."

"Eureka?"

"Eureka, Dakota Territory. About thirty miles north." She tilted her head slightly. "You are one of the Isaaks. Gottlieb's son."

"I am David."

"I know which one. You are the quiet one. The one who watches."

David did not know what to say to this, because it was true, and because nobody had said it to his face before. He took a bite of the bread, partly to have something to do with his mouth.

"Where did you learn English?" he asked, after he had swallowed.

"I was born in it. I was born in Missouri. My parents came over from Württemberg in the fifties and they spoke German to me at home but English on the porch and English at the store and English to the neighbors, and so I have always had both. It is not unusual where I come from." She paused. "It is unusual here. Most of the people here speak German all day and English only when they have to, and badly."

"My English is bad."

"Your English is slow, which is not the same as bad. You think before you speak it. That is actually the right way to learn a language. The people who learn fastest are the ones who don't care about being wrong, and they are also the people who never get past being wrong. You will be better in two years than they will be in five."

She said this matter-of-factly, the way another person might tell you the weather. David stared at her.

"How old are you?" he asked.

"Twenty-two. Why?"

"You sound older."

"I am the oldest of seven and my mother has been ill since I was fifteen. I sound older because I have been older for a long time." She glanced over her shoulder at the dancers. "Are you going to ask me to dance?"

"I do not dance."

"That is also unusual. Why not?"

"Because I am bad at it, and because the dancing here is the dancing my parents did in Bessarabia, and I never learned it properly."

"I will teach you. It is not difficult. You move your feet and you pretend you are enjoying yourself."

"I am enjoying myself."

"Then you only have to move your feet."

He looked at her for a long moment. The fiddler was playing something that might have been a polka and might have been a man falling down stairs. The bread was still in his hand.

"All right," David said. He set the bread down on the food table. "But I am going to be terrible."

"I know," Hanna said, and she took his hand and led him out to the dancing.

He was terrible. He was so terrible that after the third measure she stopped and looked at him with real curiosity and said, "How is it possible that you are this bad? I have seen oxen with better rhythm." And David, who almost never laughed in front of strangers, laughed out loud in the yard of the Schmidt farm in front of half the German-Russian community of Hutchinson County, and Hanna laughed with him, and from the bench by the door Wilhelm Schulz looked over and nudged Gottlieb in the ribs and said, "*Schau dir das an. Dein leiser Sohn.*" Look at that. Your quiet son.

Gottlieb looked.

He watched his second-eldest son trying to dance with a girl he had clearly just met, and he watched the girl laughing at him without a trace of meanness in it, and he watched David — David, who never laughed in public — laughing back, and he understood, the way fathers understand these things, that something had just happened that he was going to have to make room for.

He turned to Friedrich Hetzler, who was standing beside him with a beer in his hand, and he said: "Tell me about your daughter."

Friedrich Hetzler grinned. "Which one? I have four."

"The one who is currently embarrassing my son."

"Ah. Hanna." Friedrich took a long pull on the beer. "Stubborn as her mother. Sharper than is good for her. Reads everything she can get her hands on. She has been waiting two years for someone to ask her to dance who was not afraid of her, and I was beginning to think nobody would." He looked at Gottlieb sideways. "Your boy does not look afraid."

"My boy does not know what he is in for."

"None of us do," Friedrich said cheerfully. "It is the only way the species continues."

---

David and Hanna were married on the thirtieth of August, 1883, in the prayer house in Eureka, by a circuit-riding Lutheran pastor who served three congregations and arrived dusty from forty miles of road. David was twenty-three. Hanna was twenty-two.

Gottlieb gave a short speech at the meal afterward. It was the longest speech he had ever given in his life, which was not saying much — it was four sentences — and the third sentence was: "I do not know how this happened, but I am very glad it did." Hanna laughed at this, and David laughed at this, and Louisa, who was sitting next to Gottlieb, reached under the table and squeezed his hand once, hard, the way she had been squeezing his hand at decisive moments for twenty-nine years.

After the wedding, David and Hanna moved to a small house on the western edge of the Isaak section — David's corner, the corner he had broken alone in the autumn of 1880 and that he had been quietly working ever since, with Magdalena's help in the keeping of the secret. By the time he brought Hanna there as his wife, the corner was no longer a secret. It was twelve acres of broken ground with a small frame house on it that David and Jacob and Daniel had built together over the previous winter, and it was the first piece of earth in the family that belonged to a man who had broken it with his own hands without any help from his father.

Hanna walked into the small frame house on her wedding night and looked around the one room — bed, table, stove, two chairs, a shelf with three books on it — and she said: "It will do."

"It will do for now."

"It will do for as long as it has to."

She put her hands on her hips and turned in a slow circle and looked at the walls and the small window and the dirt floor that David had not yet had time to plank over.

"I am going to need a garden," she said. "And a clothesline. And I am going to need you to put a window on the south wall, because there is not enough light in here to read by. We will not be living in the dark like Bessarabian peasants. I refuse."

"You are not a Bessarabian peasant."

"No. But you are, and you are going to have to get used to a wife who is not."

David looked at her standing in the middle of the small room with her hands on her hips and her hat still on, and he felt a kind of joy he did not have words for in either language.

"All right," he said. "I will put a window on the south wall."

"Good. Now come help me unpack."

---

Their first child was a girl, Josephine, born in October of 1885. Their second was a boy.

He came on the thirteenth of June, 1887, after a long quiet labor at home — Hanna had refused to go anywhere, refused to let Gottlieb send for the midwife from Parkston until the very end, and then had delivered the baby in her own bed in the small frame house on the corner that David had broken, with Louisa and Caroline on either side of her and David in the kitchen with Josephine on his lap because Hanna had told him to stay out and not come in until she said so.

She said so at sunset.

David came in. The baby was in Hanna's arms, wrapped in the linen Susanne had made for him three months ago. Hanna's hair was damp at the temples and her face was the color of chalk and her grey eyes were enormous and very, very tired.

"A boy."

"I see."

"He is the size of a small loaf of bread, David. He is going to be tall."

"What shall we call him?"

"Albert. Albert Friedrich, after my father. Friedrich for the second name."

"Albert Friedrich Isaak."

"Yes."

David sat down on the edge of the bed and looked at his son. Josephine, two years old, climbed up beside him and looked at the baby with the suspicious interest of a small child meeting her replacement, and Hanna laughed, the small tired laugh of a woman who had just done the hardest thing she would ever do and was already on the other side of it.

That evening Gottlieb came to see the baby. He walked the half-mile from his own house in the long June twilight, and when he came in, Hanna held the bundle out to him without ceremony.

"Your grandson, *Vater*."

Gottlieb took the bundle. He held the baby the way old farmers hold babies — carefully, with rough hands, as if the child were something that had come up unexpectedly from good soil — and he looked down at his second-eldest son's first-born son and he did not say anything for a long moment.

Then he said, in the German of the colonies: "*Albert Friedrich. Willkommen.*"

The baby, who had been about to start crying, decided not to. He looked up at his grandfather with the unfocused intensity of newborns and made a small contented sound, and Gottlieb's face did something it almost never did in public, and David, watching from across the small room with Hanna's hand in his, understood that his father had just become a different kind of man for the second time in nine years.

---

That autumn, after the harvest was in and the wheat was sold and the year's work was as finished as it ever got, the brothers started to talk.

It happened at supper at Gottlieb's house on a Sunday in October, with Jacob and David and Daniel and Solomon all there, and Magdalena clearing dishes, and Louisa at the head of the table refilling everyone's coffee. The conversation started with prices — wheat had been good that year — and moved to the question of next year's planting, and from there to the question of where next year's planting was going to happen, because the answer was no longer obvious.

"There is no land left around Parkston," Jacob said. He was twenty-nine now and had a wife of his own and two children and his own claim half a mile north, and he had been thinking about this for a year. "Every quarter section is filed on. The boys coming over from Bessarabia next spring will have to go somewhere else, and the boys after them, and our own sons in another fifteen years, and there is nowhere for them to go from here."

"There is land north," Daniel said. He was twenty-four. "The railroad is pushing into McIntosh and Logan and Emmons. They say the soil is good. Not as good as here, maybe, but good enough. And there is space."

"How much space?"

"All of it. Nobody is up there yet except Norwegians and a few cousins from Tarutino. We could start a new colony. Real land, real claims, our own quarter sections. Not farming on a corner of Father's section."

He said this last part without looking at David, but David heard it anyway, and Hanna, who was sitting next to David with Albert asleep against her shoulder, felt David's hand tighten very slightly on his coffee cup.

Gottlieb was at the other end of the table. He had been listening without saying anything, the way he listened to most things, with his coffee cup in both hands and his eyes on the middle distance.

"How far north?" he said.

"Hebron. About a hundred and fifty miles."

"By rail?"

"By rail. Two days, maybe three with the family."

Gottlieb set down his coffee. He looked around the table at his sons — Jacob, David, Daniel, Solomon — and at his sons' wives and his grandchildren and his daughters, and he was quiet for a long moment.

"If you boys are going to leave," he said finally, "I am not staying here without you."

David looked up sharply. "Father —"

"I am fifty-three years old. I am not too old to break new ground. I broke this ground at forty-three and I am stronger now than I was then, because the food is better and the work is mine. If you are going north, I am going north."

"You have the best farm of your life here."

"I will have a better one there."

"Father, you do not have to —"

"I am not doing it because I have to, David. I am doing it because I am not going to sit on this porch and watch my sons disappear over the horizon." He looked at Louisa. "Am I?"

Louisa was refilling Solomon's coffee. She did not look up from the pot.

"No," she said. "You are not. And neither am I."

That settled it.

The conversation went on for another hour — practical questions, train schedules, who would go first, what to take, what to sell, when to leave. Hanna listened with Albert asleep on her shoulder and watched her father-in-law and her mother-in-law and her husband and his brothers begin to plan a third move that would take them all to a place none of them had seen, and she understood that she had married into a family that was not going to stop moving, and that this was the price of the man she had chosen and that she had known the price when she chose him.

Later, walking back to the small frame house in the cold October dark with David, she shifted Albert to her other shoulder and said: "How far is Hebron from a town with a school?"

"I don't know."

"Find out before we go. I am not raising this child without a school."

"All right."

"And David."

"Yes."

"We are going to need a bigger window than the one in the bedroom. The light up there is going to be different."

David, walking beside her under the cold prairie stars, with his sleeping son's small foot just visible against his wife's coat, smiled in the dark where she could not see him, and said: "All right, Hanna. A bigger window."
