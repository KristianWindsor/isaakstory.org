## Chapter Eighteen

# Apparel Art

Eureka, South Dakota, and Cedar Falls, Iowa — 1935 to 1939

---

Four days after the train.

Alice was upstairs in bed with the stitches still in her forehead. Edith was in the kitchen with a pencil stub and a piece of paper, writing. Albert was at the table with his hands around his coffee cup.

Eva was at the stove.

"Eva."

"Yes."

"We're leaving."

She did not turn around. She went on stirring the pot on the stove for another moment — a pot of oatmeal that did not need stirring — and then she set the spoon down and turned around and looked at her husband at the table.

"Where."

"Cedar Falls. Iowa. I was there on the train back from Fort Riley in 1919. There is a river. There are trees. The streets are paved and the houses have running water and the wheat does not matter because nobody there grows wheat."

"When."

"As soon as I can sell the store to Ed."

Eva looked at him for a long moment. Then she sat down across from him at the table and put her hands around her own cup of coffee.

"All right, Albert."

"That is all?"

"That is all. I have been waiting for you to say it since September."

---

Albert sold the store to Ed in the small back office on a Thursday morning at the end of January.

He did not ask very much for it. The store in the winter of 1935 was not worth very much — half the storefronts on Main Street were dark and the wheat was still not coming up and the customers who came in were the customers who had been coming in for fifteen years and did not have any more money than they had had last month — but the store was still the store, and Ed was still the brother, and Albert sold it to him for a hundred dollars and a handshake and the promise that Ed would look after Hanna.

Ed shook his hand.

"You are sure."

"I am sure."

"It is a long way, Albert."

"It is not so long. I went to France."

Ed laughed once — the short surprised laugh of a brother who had not expected a joke at a moment like this — and then he did not laugh, and the two of them stood in the small back office with the ledgers on the shelf behind them, the same ledgers Albert had been keeping since the autumn of 1919 when he had come home from France and started running the books for his father's store. Ed put his hand on the ledgers.

"I will keep them the way you kept them."

"I know."

Albert walked out of the back office and across the floor of the store he had run for sixteen years and out the front door onto the main street of Eureka, and he did not look back, because Albert was not a man who looked back.

---

Hanna was at the long wooden table in her kitchen the evening Albert came to say goodbye.

She had made him a supper of *Knoephla* and dark bread and the bitter coffee she had been making since her own mother had taught her in a kitchen in Sophiental in 1864. The lamp on the table was the same lamp she had been lighting every evening for forty years. Her hands on the table were the hands he had known his entire life — the small strong hands that had pinned up her hair in the mornings and kneaded the bread in the afternoons and washed his face when he had come home with a scraped knee in 1895.

She was seventy-three.

"*Mutter.*"

"Eat your soup, Albert."

He ate his soup. She watched him eat. When he had finished she took the bowl and refilled it at the stove and brought it back to the table and sat down again and put her hands around her own cup.

"You are going tomorrow."

"Tomorrow morning."

"You will write."

"I will write."

"Every week."

"Every week, *Mutter.*"

She nodded. She was not going to cry. She had not cried when David died and she had not cried when Albert had come home from France with damaged lungs and she was not going to cry now. She reached across the table and put her hand on top of his hand on the wood.

"Your father would have been proud of you, Albert."

"I know, *Mutter.*"

"You are doing the thing he would have done. You are moving your family to where there is more than this. That is what we do. That is what the Isaaks have always done."

"Yes, *Mutter.*"

She did not say anything else. They finished the coffee in silence and then Albert kissed her on the top of her head the way he had kissed his father on the top of the head on the morning he had left for Camp Funston in 1917, and he walked down the wooden stairs at the side of the building and out into the cold January street, and he did not look back at that either.

He had understood, sitting at the table with her hand on his, that he was not going to see his mother again. He had understood it the way he had understood most things in his life — slowly, quietly, without anyone having to tell him. The train to Cedar Falls was going to leave in the morning, and Hanna was going to stand at the top of the wooden stairs to wave him off, and he was going to wave back, and they were both going to be pretending, and both of them were going to know the other was pretending, and that was how they were going to say the real goodbye.

---

The last drive out to Grandma Roesch's was on a Sunday afternoon in the first week of February.

The road was packed snow and the wind was from the north. Albert drove slowly with Eva beside him and the girls in the back seat, and Alice had her hand on her forehead against the gash that was still healing under the bandage. The wheat fields on either side of the road were brown and flat and empty. The sky was the flat grey sky of a Dakota February.

Grandma Roesch was in the yard.

She was in a black coat she had owned since 1928 and a headscarf the color of old cream and a pair of leather boots that had belonged to her husband. She had the collie dog at her heel. The dog was old now and mostly deaf and walked with a small limp, and Grandma had been feeding him from her own plate for two years because the dog was the last thing of Opa Roesch's that was still alive.

She did not cry when Eva got out of the car and came across the yard and put her arms around her.

She held her daughter for a long moment with her chin on Eva's shoulder and her hands on Eva's back, and then she let her go and stepped back and put one hand on Eva's cheek and said: "*Schreib mir.*" Write to me.

"*Jede Woche, Mama.*" Every week, Mama.

"Good. Come inside. I have made chicken."

The girls came in from the car with their coats still on. The kitchen smelled of the chicken and of the bread Grandma had been baking that morning and of the same wood-smoke smell it had smelled of every Sunday of Edith's life. Grandma gave them each a glass of warm milk. Edith drank hers standing up by the stove and looking out the window at the brown winter yard where she had built her stone houses with the fourth room at the back that nobody else knew about.

She did not ask if they would come back.

She was seven and she knew they would not.

Grandma Roesch stood in the yard to watch the Model T pull away. The collie dog stood beside her. She did not wave. She stood very still with her hands in the pockets of her black coat and her headscarf catching the wind, and Edith turned around in the back seat and watched her grandmother get smaller and smaller through the back window, and the brown winter fields went past on either side of the road, and the farm disappeared around a bend, and Edith turned back around and sat facing forward with her hands folded in her lap.

She did not cry either.

---

The train to Cedar Falls left Eureka on a Tuesday morning at nine.

Hanna was at the top of the wooden stairs to wave. The Schmidt brothers came out of the bakery to wave. Hank stood on the sidewalk in front of the Rexall with his hat in his hands and did not wave — Hank did not do waves, Hank did salutes — and he gave Albert the small two-fingered cornet-player's salute he had been giving him since 1917 and Albert returned it, and the train pulled out of the station.

The prairie went past.

Eva sat beside Albert with Edith on her lap. Alice sat across from them with her book open and her bandaged forehead against the cold glass of the window. The train went east through the flat brown country and the flat brown country kept going and kept going the way the prairie always kept going, and sometime in the late afternoon it began to change — the flatness giving way to the low rolling hills of eastern South Dakota, then to the small wooded draws of the Missouri valley, then to the brown frozen river itself, and the train crossed the river on a long iron bridge with the water grey and slow underneath it, and Edith pressed her face to the window and watched.

She had never seen a river that wide.

"Look, Mama."

Eva looked.

"That is the Missouri, *Liebchen.*"

"It is very big."

"It is very big."

They slept in berths on the train that night. In the morning the land outside the window was Iowa — green in places where the snow was thin, with farms closer together than the farms in South Dakota and small red barns and the silhouettes of trees along the creeks and the first towns Edith had ever seen that had two streets. The train pulled into Cedar Falls at noon on a Wednesday in February of 1935, and Albert carried the trunks off the train and the girls followed him onto the platform, and Edith looked up and saw the sky over Cedar Falls and thought — very clearly, in a small seven-year-old thought she would remember for the rest of her life — *this is a new place.*

---

The house was at 621 West Seventh Street.

It was a small white frame house with green shutters and a maple tree in the front yard and a porch across the front with three wooden steps up to it, and it had a yard — a real yard, fenced in white pickets, with a patch of grass under the maple tree — and it had a front door that opened onto the porch and not onto a staircase, and when Albert unlocked the front door on the afternoon they arrived and pushed it open, the air that came out of the house was the smell of a house that had been closed up for two weeks and was waiting.

Edith walked inside.

The hallway. The sitting room. The kitchen at the back. Electric lights on the walls that came on when you turned the small brass knob by the door. A wooden staircase going up to two small bedrooms under the eaves. Edith went up the stairs by herself because Edith always went up stairs by herself, and she opened the door to the smaller of the two bedrooms at the top, and she stood in the doorway.

It had a window with a curtain. It had a small wooden bed with a blue quilt on it. It had a chair. It had a rag rug on the pine floor and a small wooden desk under the window where the light came in.

She had never had her own room before. She had always shared with Alice.

She did not know what to do. She stood in the doorway for a long moment and then she went back down the stairs, and Eva was in the kitchen at the back of the house and Eva was standing at the sink, and Eva had turned on the tap, and the water was coming out of the tap into the basin, and Eva was watching the water come out, and the water did not stop.

Eva had been standing there for two minutes.

"Mama."

"Yes, *Liebchen.*"

"The water."

"I know."

"It does not stop."

"I know."

Eva held her hand under the tap. The water ran over her fingers and down into the basin and down the drain and out of the house and away somewhere Eva did not have to think about, and the hand that had been washing breakfast dishes in rust-colored cistern water for four years held itself under the clean cold running water of a house in Cedar Falls, Iowa, and Eva's face did the small breaking thing her face had been holding in for four years.

She turned the tap off. She turned it on again. She turned it off.

Then she bent down and cupped both hands under the tap and filled them with water and brought them up and washed her face — her whole face, cheeks and forehead and eyelids — with both hands in the way a person washes her face when she has been waiting a long time to have water enough to wash with. She did it twice. Then she turned off the tap and dried her face on the dish towel and looked at Edith standing in the doorway of the kitchen and said, very quietly:

"We are going to take a bath tonight, *Liebchen.* A real bath. In a tub. With hot water all the way to the top."

Edith nodded.

She had never been in a bath with hot water all the way to the top either.

---

The bath that night.

The tub was in the small bathroom at the top of the stairs — a white enameled iron tub on four small iron feet, the kind of tub that had not been in any house the Isaaks had ever lived in. The water came out of the cold tap and did not stop, and Eva filled the kettle in the kitchen and boiled it on the stove and carried it up the stairs and poured the boiling water into the tub to warm the cold water, and she did this three times until the bath was warm the whole way through and the steam rose off the surface and fogged the mirror over the sink, and Eva sat down on the closed lid of the toilet and watched the tub.

Edith went first because Edith was the youngest.

She stepped into the warm water up to her knees and looked down at her own bare feet under the water and laughed in the small uncertain way a child laughs when she does not quite know what is happening to her, and she sat down and the water came up to her shoulders, and she slid down a little farther and the water came up to her chin, and she looked across the bathroom at her mother on the closed lid of the toilet and said: *Mama, it is warm everywhere.*

"Yes."

"All the way to my toes."

"Yes, *Liebchen.*"

"I like it."

"I know you do."

Eva washed Edith's hair in the bath. She used the new bar of soap she had bought at the grocery store on the way from the train station. The soap smelled of lavender. Edith had never smelled lavender soap. She closed her eyes while her mother poured warm water over her head from a clean tin cup and rinsed the soap out of her hair, and she thought about the brown cistern water in Eureka and about how the girls at school in Eureka had sometimes had hair that smelled like the cistern because there had been no water to wash it with, and she understood that she was never going to have hair that smelled like the cistern again.

Alice went next. Eva boiled another kettle and carried it up and added it to the water. Alice sat down in the warm water and closed her eyes and did not say anything for a long time. Eva washed her hair too.

When Alice was out of the bath and in her nightgown, Eva drained the tub and filled it again with fresh water and got in herself, and the girls went to their own beds in their own rooms, and Eva lay in the hot clean water up to her collarbone with her eyes closed for a long time. Albert knocked on the bathroom door at some point and said *are you all right, Eva*, and Eva said *yes, Albert, I am all right*, and Albert went back down the stairs to the sitting room and did not ask again.

She stayed in the bath until the water went cold.

---

Albert spent the first month walking Main Street.

He walked up one side and down the other every morning. He looked at the storefronts. He looked at what was in the windows. He counted the women going in and out of the department stores — Hansen's and the Cedar Falls Dry Goods and a small dress shop called Miller's — and he watched what they bought and how they paid and what they were wearing when they came out, and he started a small notebook in his coat pocket where he wrote down what he saw.

The empty storefront was at 217 Main Street.

It had been a hardware store until 1933 and had been empty for two years. The landlord was a man named Henderson who had inherited the building from his father and did not know what to do with it. The rent was thirty dollars a month. Albert looked through the plate-glass front into the dusty empty space one afternoon in the third week of February and stood there for a long moment with his hands in his coat pockets, and then he went around the corner and up the outside stairs to Henderson's office and said he wanted to lease it.

Henderson looked at him over the top of his spectacles.

"A dress store, Mr. Isaak."

"A dress store."

"In this economy."

"In this economy, Mr. Henderson, a woman will spend the last dollar in her pocket on a dress that makes her look better than she feels. The men have already stopped spending. The women are going to start spending soon. I have been watching the women on this street for three weeks and the women on this street are going to buy what I am going to sell, and I would like to sign a two-year lease on the space at two hundred and seventeen starting the first of March."

Henderson signed the lease.

Albert walked back down the outside stairs to the main street with the lease folded in his coat pocket and the notebook in his other coat pocket and the small quiet feeling in his chest of a man who has just started a thing that is going to be the thing.

---

He hired an architect from Waterloo named Halsey.

Halsey was thirty-four, trained at the Armour Institute in Chicago, and had designed three storefronts in Waterloo and none in Cedar Falls, and when Albert came to him with the empty space at 217 Main Street and said he wanted something that looked like Fifth Avenue, Halsey laughed and said that was not what Cedar Falls wanted. Albert looked at him across the small draughting table in Halsey's second-floor office and said: *Cedar Falls does not know what it wants yet. I am going to tell it.*

Halsey stopped laughing.

They worked on the plans for six weeks. Albert came up to Waterloo every Tuesday and Thursday on the morning bus and sat beside Halsey at the draughting table and pointed at things and said *no, like this*, and Halsey drew what Albert wanted, and sometimes Halsey pushed back and sometimes Albert pushed back, and the plans came together in the way plans come together when a man who has a picture in his head is working with a man who can draw the picture on paper.

Chrome and glass fixtures. Rose-tinted mirrors along the long wall. Four lighted shadow boxes along the front window for displaying the best dresses one at a time like small lit paintings. A polished wood floor — imported walnut, which Halsey had never put into a storefront before and had to order from a supplier in Minneapolis. Three rich blue velvet curtains in the dressing rooms. A small brass bell above the front door that would ring a single clean note when a customer walked in. The whole thing lit by electric lamps with shades the color of old cream, because Albert had seen a lamp like that in the window of a restaurant in New York in 1919 and had never forgotten it.

Halsey looked at the finished plans at the end of April and said: *Mr. Isaak, this is not Cedar Falls.*

"It will be," Albert said.

---

The naming contest was Eva's idea.

She put a small advertisement in the *Cedar Falls Record* in the first week of May. *A new ladies' dress store opening this summer at 217 Main Street invites the ladies of Cedar Falls to submit names. The winning name will be chosen by Mr. A. F. Isaak, proprietor, and the winner will receive a twenty-dollar credit at the store upon opening.* The submissions came in by mail and by hand for two weeks. Albert sorted them on the kitchen table at the house on West Seventh Street in the evenings after supper, and Eva sat across the table reading each submission aloud.

Most of them were bad.

*Isaak's Ladies' Apparel.* *Cedar Falls Fashion Shop.* *The Style Center.* *The Dress Box.* *Main Street Millinery* — which was not even a dress store. Albert shook his head at each one. Eva kept reading.

On the third evening of the sorting, Eva picked up a small folded note from the middle of the pile and read it aloud.

"*Apparel Art.*"

Albert looked up from the ledger he had been writing in.

"Read it again."

"*Apparel Art.*"

"Who sent it."

"A Mrs. Caroline Bjornson. She did not put an address."

Albert set down his pen.

"That is the name."

"You are sure."

"I am sure, Eva. That is the name. *Apparel Art.* I want the letters in chrome above the door and I want them tall, and I want the second word to be slightly larger than the first, and I want the *A* in *Art* to be the biggest letter of all."

Eva looked at him across the table.

"All right, Albert."

"Write it down on the lease tomorrow."

She wrote it down on the lease the next morning.

---

Apparel Art opened on the eighth of September, 1935.

Albert hung the chrome letters above the door on the morning of the seventh. He stood on a wooden ladder on the sidewalk of Main Street with a wrench in his hand and installed each letter one at a time, and the chrome caught the afternoon sun, and people walking past stopped to look. By the time he had finished installing the letters, a small crowd of women had collected on the sidewalk in front of the store, looking up at the chrome and at the plate-glass windows behind which were the four lighted shadow boxes with the four best dresses displayed one at a time the way Albert had wanted, and one of the women in the crowd said to another woman: *Lord, Margaret, look at that.*

Albert heard her from the top of the ladder.

He did not turn around. He tightened the last bolt on the last letter and climbed down the ladder and folded it under his arm and carried it around the corner to the alley where he had left his toolbox, and he did not look back at the crowd, and he did not look back at the chrome, because Albert had been a man who did not look back for eighteen years now and was not going to stop today.

Eva was inside the store.

She had been arranging the dresses on the new chrome racks all morning — the ready-made dresses Albert had ordered from wholesalers in Chicago and Minneapolis, the good dresses for Sunday church and the everyday dresses for weekday errands and the special dresses for evenings and weddings — and when Albert came in the front door the brass bell rang its single clean note above his head and Eva looked up from a rack of navy-blue crepe dresses and smiled.

"It sounds nice."

"I told you it would."

He came across the polished walnut floor to where she was standing and looked at the dresses she had been arranging and nodded once at her arrangement — Eva was the one who knew how dresses should look on a rack, and Albert did not argue with her about it — and then he turned around and looked at the store.

The long wall with the rose-tinted mirrors. The electric lamps with the shades the color of old cream. The three blue velvet curtains of the dressing rooms. The polished walnut floor. The chrome and the glass and the four lighted shadow boxes along the front window. The small brass bell above the door.

He looked at it all for a long moment.

He did not say anything. Eva was watching him from beside the rack of navy crepe, and she did not say anything either, because Eva knew what her husband looked like when he was trying not to cry in public and she knew that the best thing she could do was let him not cry in private.

After a while Albert said, very quietly: "Eva."

"Yes."

"We built this."

"Yes, Albert. We built this."

The brass bell above the door rang. A woman came in — the first customer of the first day of Apparel Art, a woman in a brown coat with her hair pinned up under a dark hat — and she stopped just inside the door and looked up at the chrome letters and the rose-tinted mirrors and the blue velvet curtains and the lighted shadow boxes, and her face did the small astonished thing a woman's face does when she has just walked into the kind of store she had only seen before in magazines, and she said, in the small uncertain voice of a woman who was not sure if she belonged in the room:

"Good morning. Is this — the new store?"

Eva stepped out from behind the rack of navy crepe.

"Yes, ma'am. Welcome to Apparel Art. Can I show you something?"

The woman came in. Eva showed her a dress. The woman bought it.

That was the first sale.

---

Edith at eight had her own small desk in the back office of the store behind Albert's ledger desk, where she did her homework after school every afternoon and wrote her stories in the evenings on the backs of invoices Albert brought her from the wholesalers. She had been in school in Cedar Falls for seven months by the time the store opened. Her teacher was a Miss Hoffstetter who had said, on the second day of school in February, that Edith wrote better than any seven-year-old she had ever taught, and Miss Hoffstetter had been teaching for fourteen years.

Edith walked to the store after school every afternoon and let herself in through the back door and sat at the small desk in the back office and did her spelling and her arithmetic, and when her homework was finished she took out whichever story she was writing that week and worked on it until Eva came to get her for supper. The stories were longer now than the stories she had been writing in Eureka. Her handwriting was better. Her pencils were still short stubs because Eva still did not throw out pencils until they were too small to hold, even in Cedar Falls where pencils were easier to come by, because some habits a woman did not give up even after the rain had come back.

She wrote a story that autumn about a girl who lived in a small white house on Franklin Street.

The girl in the story had a yard with a maple tree in it and a room of her own with a window and a small wooden desk, and the girl in the story took baths in a tub with hot water all the way to her shoulders, and the girl in the story wrote stories in the afternoons in the back office of her father's store. Edith wrote fourteen pages of it on the backs of invoices from a hosiery company in Milwaukee. When she finished she folded the pages in half and put them in the top drawer of the small desk in the back office, and she did not show them to anyone, because she had not shown her stories to anyone since Eva had read *The Story of Tante Frieda* on the dusty floor of the attic above the store in Eureka in April of 1934.

But sometimes, in the evenings when Albert was closing up the store and Eva was sweeping the walnut floor, Edith looked up from her desk and watched her parents move around in the lamplight of the store her father had built, and she thought about the girl in the story she was writing.

The girl in the story was her.

She had always known that. She had not known until Cedar Falls that she was allowed to write about herself.

---

Edith was confirmed at St. John Lutheran Church on the sixth of April, 1941.

She was thirteen. She stood before the congregation in a white dress Eva had sewn for her in the evenings at the kitchen table on West Seventh Street, with the small embroidered handkerchief that had belonged to Tante Frieda folded into the pocket. She recited the catechism in English. The questions were the same questions her great-great-grandfather Gottlieb had answered in German in a country church in Kulm in 1848, and the answers were the same answers, and the God on the other end of the questions was the same God. Albert sat in the pew beside Eva and watched his younger daughter stand in the white dress at the altar rail and thought about the small Bible his father had given him at the kitchen table in Eureka in 1917, which was still in the top drawer of his dresser at home.

Two years later she was the church organist.

The organ at St. John was a full pipe organ — three keyboards, foot pedals, a rank of pipes that rose toward the ceiling like the interior of a cathedral compressed into a small Lutheran church in Iowa. Edith was fifteen. She practiced alone in the empty sanctuary in the afternoons after school, the great instrument responding under her hands and her feet with a sound so large it vibrated in her sternum, and she thought, *this is what it feels like to hold thunder in your hands.* She played for the service on Sunday mornings. She played *Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott* — the hymn Albert had played on the violin in the barracks at Camp Funston, the hymn his father had liked best — and Albert sat in the pew and listened to his daughter play the hymn his father had loved, and the pipes of the organ filled the church the way the field guns had filled the Argonne, and Albert did not cry in church because Albert did not cry, but he closed his eyes.

---

The boys started leaving in the spring of 1942.

Pearl Harbor had been in December. The seniors went first, volunteering before they were drafted. Then the juniors. The hallways of Cedar Falls High thinned the way the hallways of a school thin when the boys are going to a war — one desk empty at a time, then two, then a whole row. Edith watched the empty desks and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. The boys were gone. The girls who remained took the boys' seats in the front row of the classrooms and did the work the boys had been doing, and the war economy reached Cedar Falls the way it had reached every small city in the Midwest, with rationing cards and scrap drives and the strange new prosperity of a country that had turned its factories to munitions.

Apparel Art was thriving. Women who had not been able to afford a dress in 1934 had husbands working in the defense plants in Waterloo now, and the dresses Albert was ordering from Chicago and Minneapolis sold out as fast as he could put them on the chrome racks. Eva hired a second girl to help with the counter. Alice, now twenty-one and still thin from the scarlet fever of her childhood, worked at the store three afternoons a week between the classes she was taking at the teachers' college. Albert at fifty-five stood behind the register most mornings with his hands folded on the glass countertop and watched the women of Cedar Falls come in through the brass-belled door and walk across his polished walnut floor, and he thought sometimes about the dugout at Saint-Mihiel and about the older German farmer in the column of prisoners saying *thank God you are Americans*, and he thought that the polished walnut floor was a thing built by a man who had decided, in a canvas tent in the Argonne in November of 1918, that if he came home he was going to spend the rest of his life making beautiful rooms for other people to walk into.

Edith worked at the store too. She dressed the mannequins in the front window. She designed the shadow boxes. She wrote her stories in the back office on the backs of invoices the way she had always written them, and sometime in the spring of 1944, in the middle of her junior year of high school, she told Albert she wanted to go to college to study writing.

Albert looked at his daughter across the desk in the back office.

"Writing."

"Yes, *Papa.*"

"There is a school."

"The teachers' college. Right here in Cedar Falls. They have a writing program."

"You have been planning this."

"I have been planning this since Tante Frieda taught me to hold a pencil."

Albert nodded. He did not say anything for a long moment. Then he reached across the desk and put his hand on top of her hand the way his mother had put her hand on top of his in her kitchen in Eureka in 1935, and he said, in the small quiet voice he used for the things that mattered most: "*Dann gehst du.*" Then you will go.

---

The war in Europe ended on the eighth of May, 1945.

The church bells of Cedar Falls rang at noon. They rang for half an hour — every bell in every church tower in the city at once, because the news had come over the radio in the morning and the bells had been waiting for the news for three years. Albert was in the doorway of Apparel Art at 217 Main Street when the bells started. He stood there with his hands in the pockets of his coat and listened to the bells rolling over Main Street in great waves of brass sound, and the people in the street stopped walking and stood still and listened, and some of them cried and some of them did not.

Albert did not cry. Albert at fifty-seven, with the scarred lungs and the small Bible in the top drawer of his dresser and the memory of a canvas tent behind the lines in the Argonne, stood in the doorway of the store he had built and listened to the bells of Cedar Falls ringing for the end of the war, and he thought about the silence in the canvas tent on the eleventh of November, 1918, and about how the silence then had been the loudest silence he had ever heard, and about how the bells now were the loudest sound, and that the silence and the bells were the same thing twenty-seven years apart.

That same month, Edith graduated from Cedar Falls High School.

She walked across the stage in the school auditorium on a warm evening at the end of May in a white dress with her blonde hair pinned up and the small embroidered handkerchief with the blue forget-me-nots folded into the pocket, and she took her diploma from the principal and turned to face the audience, and in the third row Albert and Eva were sitting beside Alice, and Albert had his hands folded in his lap and Eva had her hands folded in her lap, and both of them were watching their younger daughter on the stage with the small private pride of parents who had brought her out of a drought and a train wreck and a scarlet-fever winter and an apartment above a dying store in a dying town, and who were now watching her take a diploma that was going to send her to the college down the street to study writing.

Edith looked out at the audience.

She found her parents in the third row. Her father's face in the auditorium lights was the face of a man who had come home from a war and built a store and raised a daughter who was about to become the thing he had never had the chance to be. Her mother's face was the face of the woman who had stood in the kitchen of a small white house at 621 West Seventh Street in February of 1935 and watched the water come out of the tap and had washed her face in it and had known the drought was over.

Edith smiled at them from the stage.

Albert smiled back.

Outside the auditorium, the bells of Cedar Falls had been quiet for two weeks. The war in the Pacific was not yet over. Alice would marry a Waterloo boy home from the war in the autumn. Edith would start at the Iowa State Teachers College in September to study writing — the love of her life, the thing she had been doing since a German nanny had taught her to hold a pencil at the kitchen table above a five-and-dime store on the main street of a dying prairie town in the winter of 1932.

She was seventeen.

She was going to be a writer.

She had always been going to be a writer.
