## Chapter Eight

# The Same Stars

Kulm, Bessarabia — August 1853

---

Gottlieb found his father spreading a mat beside the wheat.

The grain lay in a long heap on the hard threshing floor, still mixed with husks and broken straw. All afternoon they had waited for wind. Without it, everything they tossed into the air came down together. Now the sun was gone, and Karl Isaak was folding his coat to put beneath his head.

“Are we sleeping here?”

“Unless you can fetch a breeze.”

Gottlieb looked toward the house.

“It might be blowing by morning.”

“It might be blowing while we're asleep.”

His father lowered himself onto the mat. He had coughed since winter, and his shirt hung loose now where it had once pulled across his shoulders. Sometimes Gottlieb heard his mother changing the damp bedding long before dawn. But this evening Karl had eaten his supper and asked for another potato.

Gottfried brought the wooden shovels from the shed. Little Martin followed him with a sheepskin dragging behind him.

“No,” their mother called.

“But they're sleeping outside.”

“They're working.”

Martin looked at the mats.

“So will I.”

“Then you can start with the dishes,” Elisabeth said.

His father rubbed his beard. Gottlieb looked away before Martin could see him smiling.

She steered the boy indoors, then returned with water and a blanket. She spread it over Karl's legs.

“You'll wake stiff.”

“Then you can stand me by the gate.”

“And frighten the hens?”

She sat beside him. Gottfried lay beyond the heap with his cap over his face.

“Wake me when it comes.”

“We'll try,” his father said.

---

The earth still held the day's warmth. Gottlieb stretched out with his hands beneath his head. From another yard came the scrape of a shovel. Someone was trying again.

“You were a long time at the well,” his father said.

“There were people.”

“One of them was Schulz's daughter.”

“She needed help with her pails.”

“Did you fill both?”

“Yes.”

His father nodded gravely. “Then she'll be back tomorrow.”

“Leave him alone,” his mother said. “You knocked your own bucket against a stone when I looked at you.”

“Johann was behind me.”

“Was he the one you were watching?”

Gottlieb laughed. His father's mouth lifted at the corners.

“Your mother had spilled hers down her dress. I was being useful.”

“Eventually.”

Her shoulder rested against his before she stood and went to settle the younger boys.

Gottlieb watched his father pull the blanket over his feet.

“How old were you?”

“Eighteen. Nineteen when we married.”

Gottlieb was eighteen himself. He tried to picture his parents beside a stream, before there had been any of them.

“Did you speak to her father yourself?”

“My father did.”

“Oh.”

Karl turned toward him.

“Have you something you want me to ask Schulz?”

“No. I only wondered.”

“All right.”

His father left it there. Gottlieb was grateful, and a little disappointed.

One by one, the last lit windows went dark. The moon had not risen. Above the roofs, stars appeared between the stars he had already found, until the familiar seven of the Wagon were almost lost among them. He traced the four stars of its crooked box, then the three that curved away to make the shaft.

The Milky Way stretched over the yard and down toward the fields, a pale, ragged brightness. In places it thickened into clouds; elsewhere dark gaps cut through it. When he looked straight at a faint patch it nearly vanished. Looking beside it brought it back.

He had seen it all his life. Lying here, with nothing between his face and that enormous sky, he could not stop looking. The village had become a few black roofs at the edges of his sight. Above him there seemed to be room to fall forever.

“Was it like this in Poland?”

“More trees,” his father said. “You had to find a gap.”

“But the same stars?”

“The same.”

A dog barked far down the street. Gottlieb pressed his heels into the warm ground.

At the well that morning the men had been talking about the Tsar's army, across the Pruth since July. Now it came back to him.

“They say the army needs wagons. What if they come for ours?”

His father was quiet. Gottlieb could hear him breathing.

“We haven't been ordered to give it.”

“But if we are?”

“Then we'll have to see.”

Gottlieb turned his head. “What did your father do when the French came?”

“Hid what he could. Gave them what they found.”

“Did they pay him?”

“They gave him paper.”

“What about the coin with Napoleon on it?”

“That was a soldier at the spring. He offered it to Johann. I took it for him.”

Gottlieb smiled in the dark. “You're still keeping it for him?”

“He hasn't asked for it.”

His father drew the blanket higher. From the shed came the slow tearing sound of a horse eating hay.

“Were you frightened?”

“Yes.”

Gottlieb waited, but his father said no more.

When they knelt for the evening prayer, Gottfried pushed his cap back and their mother's voice joined them from the doorway. At *deliver us from evil*, Gottlieb looked toward the dark shed.

Afterward he lay beside his father again. A thin streak of light crossed the sky and was gone before he could point.

“Did you see?”

“I saw.”

He waited for another. Beside him, his father's breathing slowed.

---

Something cool moved across Gottlieb's forehead.

He opened his eyes. The edge of the blanket lifted, settled, lifted again. Air slipped beneath his damp collar and ran cold down his back.

His father was on his feet.

“Wind. Get them up.”

Gottlieb shook Gottfried, then hurried to the house. His brothers came out pulling on shirts. Elisabeth followed with her sleeves rolled. Even Martin appeared, wrapped in his blanket, and sat against the wall to watch.

The moon had risen while they slept. Its light showed the heap, the pale blades of the shovels, his father's bare feet. The faintest stars had disappeared.

Gottlieb scooped a handful of grain and husks and threw it high.

For a moment it opened against the sky, a spray of small bright points. The kernels fell hard, ticking against his forehead and teeth. The lighter husks hung above him. A gust pushed them into his face, and suddenly the air was full of shining flecks. He shut his eyes too late. Dust scratched beneath one lid; a sliver caught on his wet lower lip.

“Other side,” his father said.

“I know.”

“Good. Go there.”

Gottlieb spat and moved.

His father thrust a shovel into the heap and lifted. The grain rose, spread, and pattered onto the cleared floor. Husks streamed farther away. Gottlieb took another shovel and worked beside him.

Gottfried sent two brothers for the sieve and sacks. The younger boys swept; their mother held a sack open for the cleaned grain. Across the fence someone called that the wind had come, and farther down the street another voice answered. Doors opened. A child cried at being woken.

The breeze pressed Gottlieb's shirt against his chest, then billowed it away. He could smell the dry wheat and, whenever the air strengthened, the sharper smell of the horse yard. He bent, lifted, threw. Grain rattled on the earth; husks whispered past his ears. Each throw rose briefly among the stars before falling back within reach.

His father paused more often. Once he stooped for a scoop and came up without it.

“I'll take that,” Gottfried said.

“Finish what you're doing.”

The shovel scraped again. Then it stopped.

Karl stood bent over the handle, his mouth open. He drew a breath, swallowed, and tried again.

Gottlieb put down his own shovel.

“Sit down, Vater.”

His father shook his head.

“The wind will go.”

“Then let us use it.”

He reached for the handle. His father's hands stayed closed around it. Gottlieb waited, feeling the breeze tug at his shirt.

At last Karl loosened one hand, then the other.

Gottlieb passed the shovel to Gottfried and brought back the mat. His father sat down. Gottlieb covered his knees and held the cup while he drank. Water ran into the white hairs beneath his mouth.

He stayed beside him until his breathing eased.

“Go on,” his father said.

A narrow strip of rose opened above the eastern fields. The walls showed white again, and the wheat beneath Gottlieb's feet took on a faint gold.

By the time the breeze failed, the uncleaned heap was small. Gottfried tried one last throw. Everything fell straight down.

“Leave it,” Karl said.

They swept the clean wheat together. Gottlieb brought him a handful. His father rubbed it between his palms, picked out a broken stem, and let the kernels run through his fingers.

Then he held out his empty hand.

Gottlieb took it and helped him stand.

---

Later that month, his father coughed blood into the barley straw.

Gottlieb and Gottfried brought him inside between them. By evening he had a fever. His mother held broth to his mouth. He asked how much barley remained, and she told him, and he closed his eyes to reckon it.

The next day he did not ask.

At night the sweat soaked his shirt. When Gottlieb helped change it, his shoulder blades stood out beneath the skin. The cough left him exhausted, and the cloth at his mouth came away stained again. Between bouts he slept against the pillows, waking thirsty.

Once Gottlieb helped his mother wash him. Karl tried to pull his shirt over his bare thighs, and she caught his hand.

“Let us finish.”

She wiped the cloth along his leg, talking about a hen that had begun laying in the woodpile. Gottlieb held the basin closer.

On the afternoon of the twenty-fifth, just before four, Johann came running for him.

His mother knelt beside the bed with Karl's hand in hers. Gottlieb stopped at the foot. He waited through the spaces between breaths, letting his own out whenever his father managed another.

Then he waited too long.

Elisabeth laid her palm against Karl's chest. She kept it there while Gottfried came in, and while Martin asked why everyone was standing in the room.

At last she bowed her head over the hand she held.

Gottlieb knelt beside her. “Our Father, who art in heaven.”

His voice failed. Gottfried said the next words, and Gottlieb followed as well as he could.

---

His mother and Aunt Maria washed Karl and put him into a clean shirt. Gottlieb helped lift him. His head rolled against Gottlieb's arm, heavy and loose, and he held it more carefully when they lowered him again.

For that night he lay straight on the bed, the sheet pulled to his chest. His beard was combed. Without the strain of breathing, his face looked strangely narrow and still. The hollows at his temples showed beneath the smoothed hair. His lips had lost their color.

Gottlieb touched his hand. It was cool already. A dark line remained beneath the thumbnail where the washing had not reached.

The carpenter brought the coffin the following day.

On the evening of the twenty-seventh, it stood open outside the house. The low sun lay across Karl's clean shirt. Neighbors came through the gate and paused beside him. His hands lay together on his chest, the split at the base of his thumb showing white at the edges. His collar sat loose around his neck.

During the hymn, the lid was lifted into place. Gottlieb watched the strip of his father's shirt disappear beneath it.

At the cemetery they passed the children's rows. Elisabeth looked toward Justine and Louisa's crosses but kept hold of Gottlieb's arm.

Farther along were his uncle Michael's grave and Aunt Dorothea's. His grandfather Michel's cross had weathered gray. He knew his grandparents only from stories, from his father's way of saying *my mother* or *my father* and expecting the boys to understand.

Uncle Johann stood beside him at the open grave, his cap in both hands.

They set the coffin down. The schoolmaster read Christ's promise of resurrection. Gottlieb heard his mother's voice answering the prayer, and found the words with her.

When the hymn began, his uncle's voice was so like his father's that Gottlieb turned toward him. The ropes tightened and the coffin descended. When the first earth struck the lid, his mother caught Gottlieb's wrist.

When the men laid their tools on the mound, the family knelt. Gottlieb's knees pressed into loose soil. Beside him his mother prayed without speaking, her lips moving. He waited until she stood, then offered his arm for the walk home.

---

The following morning, Gottfried began giving orders before breakfast.

“August, fetch the rake. Gottlieb, help me harness.”

Gottlieb sat on the doorstep with his boots beside him.

“Did you hear?”

“I heard.”

Gottfried waited, then went to the shed alone.

Gottlieb watched him lift a collar over the horse's head. The buckle would not fasten. Gottfried pulled the strap free and tried again.

His hands were shaking.

He stopped and pressed his forehead against the horse's neck. The animal shifted, and Gottfried moved with it, keeping his face against the warm hair.

Gottlieb put on his boots.

He brought the other collar from the shed, hung it over the rail, and stepped in beside his brother.

“Hold it steady.”

Gottfried held the buckle. Gottlieb drew the strap through.
