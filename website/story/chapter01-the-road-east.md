## Chapter One

# The Road East

Duchy of Warsaw, 1812 — 1814

---

![Map of the Duchy of Warsaw, 1809-1813](/img/maps/1809-duchy-of-warsaw.png)

The byre was hot and the stink of it stuck in the back of Karl's throat. He had pitched out three barrows of old straw and there were two more to go, and his shirt was already through with sweat, and for the third time in a quarter of an hour he wondered why Mother had sent Johann to the chicken yard instead of him. Johann was thirteen and Johann was useless. Johann was the only person in the family who could find a way to make feeding chickens take longer than it took to write a letter.

Karl pitched another forkful into the barrow. Hexe the one-eyed grey barn cat watched from the rafters with the contempt of all cats. Sweat ran down his neck into his collar. He'd been doing this byre on summer mornings since he was eight, and it had not got better.

From the yard came the sound of his brother Johann not feeding the chickens. The hens were screaming, the rooster was screaming, and Tasso the yellow farm dog, who had been asleep in his patch of sun, was barking at the screaming. Karl set the pitchfork against the post and went to the byre door.

Johann was throwing handfuls of grain at the rooster's head and laughing. The rooster — a vicious old red-and-black thing that ruled the chicken yard like a small bad king — had decided the grain was war: he was running at the hens with his wings out and his beak open, and the hens were running at the fence, and Johann was bent double, pleased with himself.

“Johann.”

“What?”

“Feed them. Don't fight them.”

“He started it,” Johann said, of the rooster, with the absolute conviction of a thirteen-year-old. He scattered the rest of the grain in a wide arc, generously, and the chickens forgave him at once. Karl wished he could throw something at him.

Through the open kitchen door Karl could see Mother kneading dough on the long table, the morning's loaves taking shape under her hands. Father and Michael were off in the far field hilling potatoes, and Christian had been sent with them; their absence was the shape of the morning. The sun was already too high. The geese at the well had gone quiet. The stork on her wagon-wheel platform on the gable had her head tilted at the same angle as Tasso's.

Tasso growled.

It was a low growl, almost in his chest. He had stopped barking at the chickens. He was on his feet now, head turned toward the post-road beyond the rye field, ears forward.

“Tasso,” Karl said. The dog did not look at him.

Karl set down the pitchfork properly this time and stepped out into the yard. After a moment he felt it through the soles of his bare feet — a faint, even pressure. Not a sound. A shaking.

“Johann. Come here.”

“What is it?”

“Come here.”

Johann, hearing something in his brother's voice, came. They walked across the yard and along the cart-track to the rise at the edge of the rye field. The rye was up to Karl's hip, green and just beginning to gold at the tops, two weeks short of being ready. The shaking grew stronger as they walked. Johann opened his mouth to say something — thunder, probably; the boy thought everything was thunder — and then closed it. The sky was empty.

They climbed the rise and looked east.

---

The first thing was the dust: a long yellow fog along the post-road as far as Karl could see in either direction, hanging in the still air at the height of a man's chest. The road, when his eyes found it through the dust, was full — not crowded but full, edge to edge, surface to bottom, going one way.

Then the sound came up to them on the rise: a rolling murmur made of a thousand kinds of noise at once. Hooves on packed earth, iron rims on stone, boots, harness, voices, the lowing of cattle, a single sharp distant whistle. Karl had never heard so many sounds together in his life, and they added up to something that was almost not noise any more but a kind of weather.

Blue coats. White crossbelts. Brass buttons catching the high sun and winking. Cavalry with lances longer than the men were tall, the pennons hanging limp in the dust. Wagons with green-painted canvas. Black squat cannon on their wheels. Behind it all, a shambling herd of cattle being driven with sticks toward slaughter.

The languages came up the rise in pieces. French first, sharp, snapped from somewhere in the column like a whip. Then a singing one he had never heard before, and that he later learned was Italian. Polish too, low and muttered. Dutch, which sounded like German with stones in its mouth. And then — he leaned forward — German itself, but with the vowels chopped short, a hard clipped German from somewhere he had never been.

“Vorwärts!” a voice was shouting somewhere down in the river of blue. “Vorwärts, vorwärts!”

His own word, in another man's mouth, marching for a French emperor toward a country Karl could not properly imagine. He felt the small jolt of it. Beside him, Johann had gone very still.

“Karl,” Johann whispered. “Who are they?”

“French.”

“All of them?”

“Not all.”

A wagon halted in the road below the rise. One of its team-horses had gone down in the harness, and the driver — a small wiry man — jumped from the box and began to whip the horse where it lay, methodically, across the flank and the neck, as if he were beating a rug. The horse did not get up. After the third blow the driver took out a knife, cut the traces, and helped two other men roll the horse out of the road into the ditch on the far side, where it sank into the long weeds with one yellow eye half-open. The wagon moved on. A fly settled on the horse's eye, and another, and another.

“Karl,” Johann hissed, pointing. “The chickens.”

Two soldiers had vaulted the low fence behind the kitchen garden. They were running low and quick across the chicken yard. One of them swept up a brown hen by the legs as easily as a man picking up his hat; the other got two. The hens screamed. Karl waited for Tasso to bark. Tasso did not bark — he had stayed back by the kitchen door. Karl waited for Mother to come out. She did not. The men ran back across the fence with the hens, and were absorbed into the column, and one of them was already wringing the first neck as he walked.

Johann opened his mouth to shout. Karl clapped a hand across it, fingers spread over Johann's nose and chin. Johann's eyes went wide above his hand.

“No,” Karl said, very low. “They have rifles. Be quiet.”

Johann nodded against his palm. Karl held him a second more before letting go.

There was a small spring at the foot of the rise, a thread of clear water that came out of a flat rock and made a green pool before vanishing into the ditch. A French soldier had broken from the column and was kneeling at the spring with his shako tilted back, filling a tin canteen. He was older than the others, grey-haired under the shako, his face dark from a long sun. When he had filled the canteen he did not stand at once; he pressed his hand into the small of his back the way old men do. Then he looked up and saw the boys, and came up the rise.

“Jungen,” he said. “Brot?”

“Nein,” Karl said.

The sergeant nodded as if that were the answer he had expected. He drank from his canteen, deeply, his throat working. Water ran down his chin into the grey stubble. When he was finished he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked east, where the column was going. He looked east for a long time.

“Russland,” he said, “ist groß. Junge — Russland ist zu groß.”

He looked at Johann — the smaller of the two — and fished in his coat pocket for a small dull copper coin, almost the colour of a roof-tile, and held it out. Johann did not move. The sergeant made a small wry face and gestured at Karl: take it for him. Karl took it. Stamped on the coin was a profile he had never seen before, a man with a Roman wreath and short hair, a stern profile, almost a god's. The metal was warm from the man's coat.

“Danke,” Karl said.

The sergeant gave a small nod that was not a smile. He turned and walked down the rise to the road and back into the column. Within thirty paces Karl could not pick him out of the river of blue.

Karl closed his hand around the coin. “Home,” he said. “Now.”

They went down the back way through the rye, which whipped at their bare legs. Behind them the army continued to pass.

---

By dusk the household had pulled itself in. Father had taken the pig deep into the woods that afternoon and tied her to a young oak; the horses were standing behind the orchard, out of sight of the road; the granary door was locked, and the key was on Father's belt and no one else's. From across the rise, very faintly, like a millstream in another valley, the road was still going.

When the meal was put on the table Mother lit the candle without a word — the only one, on a Wednesday in June, which was unusual but which no one remarked on. The family sat down around it in the order they always sat. Father at the head, his huge scarred hands folded, the missing fingertip on the left ring finger catching the light. Mother opposite him with Ernst on her lap, his small head against her breast. Michael on Father's right — the heir's place, though no one would ever have said so. Karl beside Michael, Johann beside Karl. Christian at the foot. Dorothea on Mother's left, very small and very upright.

There was milk soup on the table with crumbled black bread and a knot of bacon-rind in it for the grease, and the hard cheese on its board, and a pile of green onions from the garden, and the stoneware jug of small beer Mother had brewed two weeks ago. Beside each plate, an egg. They had not had an egg apiece since Easter. Karl looked at his and knew what his mother had done. Sometime between his telling her about the road and her setting the table, she had decided that her family was not going to be hungry tonight. That was her answer.

Father bowed his head. The family bowed.

“Komm, Herr Jesu, sei unser Gast,” he said, in the voice he had said it in every night of Karl's life, “und segne, was du uns bescheret hast. Amen.”

“Amen,” the family said.

They ate. The boys told it: the road, the horse in the ditch, the woman with the silent baby, the two stolen hens, the sergeant at the spring. Russland ist groß. Karl laid the coin on the wood between him and his father, where it stayed for the rest of the meal. Father did not interrupt them. When they had finished he sat for a long moment with his hand resting beside the coin, not touching it.

It was Christian — twelve, gap-toothed, the world still small enough that any question could be asked at table — who broke the silence. “Vater,” he said. “Why is there a French army on our road?”

Father chewed his bread. He looked at the candle. Then he said, “Because we live in the Duchy of Warsaw, and the Duchy belongs to the French. For now.”

Mother set down her spoon. She looked at Father a long moment. Then she said, low: “And our sons are old enough to die for it.”

Father did not answer.

Christian, with the unguarded curiosity of a twelve-year-old, asked: “Will they come back? The soldiers?”

Karl, beside Michael, watched his mother. He saw the small piece of bread she had been moving in slow circles in the milk soup in front of Ernst stop. He saw her bend her head, as if listening to a sound nobody else in the room could hear. He saw her look down at the small soft head of her youngest son against her breast — the round skull, the pale hair, the curl of the little ear pressed against the dark wool.

She did not look at Christian. She said, very quietly, into the top of Ernst's head:

“Some of them have mothers.”

And then she did not say anything else.

Karl felt something in him give. Not a loud thing — the small inward shifting of a beam under load. Mother was not at this moment thinking about the soldiers. She was thinking about her own boys, all five of them at the table with her right now, and she was holding the smallest one.

Beside her, Dorothea — eight, who did not understand the line, who understood enough — reached across and laid her thin small hand on the bare top of Ernst's foot where it dangled out of the swaddling of Mother's apron. Just her hand on his foot. A child's gesture. Karl saw it, and the thing in him gave a second time.

After a while Father reached out and laid his great scarred hand briefly over Mother's on the wood. He did not squeeze. He laid it there, and then he took it back.

The candle burned down. Father read a short passage from the Gospel of Matthew, and the family knelt for the Vater unser. Ernst was carried already deep asleep to the cradle by the cold tile stove. Karl lay on the bench by the Kachelofen with Johann beside him. Through the wall the cows breathed. From the cradle Ernst breathed. From across the room two breaths went on, one slow and even, the other listening.

Outside, very faintly, the road was still going.

II.  The Return

He had turned sixteen in August. Johann had turned fourteen two days later. Mother had baked a single Streuselkuchen with two candles in it, as she had every August for as long as Karl could remember, and Ernst had lunged across the table to blow out the candles before either of his older brothers could and had to be stopped, laughing, by everyone at once. The harvest had come in. The army had not come back. People had begun to say, cautiously, that perhaps it would not.

Now it was the second week of December, and the Kachelofen was roaring with split birch, and the whole Stube was warm. The cattle were wintering inside the byre on the far side of the wall, and their breath warmed the wall and the wall warmed the room. The pantry was full. November had been slaughter month — a smoked ham hung in the chimney, and the kitchen smelled of smoke and salt and good fat. Mother stood at the hearth rendering goose fat into Schmalz in a black iron pan, the white fat clarifying slowly to gold. Dorothea was at her elbow stirring. Ernst, almost three, sat on the floor banging a wooden spoon against an iron pot and laughing at the noise. Tasso slept against the warm tiles of the stove. Hexe lay flat on top of the stove like a thing she owned.

Outside, Karl was breaking the ice on the cattle trough with the back of an axe. His breath steamed; his fingers were dead inside his wool mittens. The sky was a flat grey-white that did not look like sky any more, only like ceiling. He chopped through and the cows pushed in. He hauled an armful of split birch from the woodpile to the basket by the door. The wind was from the east. The wind was always from the east in December. The wind brought a faint sound on it that he had been not-hearing for an hour.

He stopped on the path and listened.

---

It was the road again — not as it had been in June. There was no surf-roar this time. This was thinner: a dry rasping shuffling, with sometimes a single voice in it, sometimes a single high sound that was perhaps a horse, sometimes nothing at all. He went up to the rise. His feet broke through the crust at every step. He got to the top, and he had to put his hand on the gatepost to keep his knees.

They were not an army. They had not been an army in some time. They were a procession of dying men, with no formations, no banners, no officers any one could tell from the men. Their faces were wrapped in things that had not been made to be wrapped on faces — looted shawls, women's skirts torn into strips, strips of cured horse-hide. Their feet were wrapped likewise. Some had paper-stuffed boots that had once been blue leather and were now a cracked grey. Some were barefoot in the snow, and the footprints they left were not white but black, and the black was the colour of frostbite where the skin had gone past saving.

One man cradled an icon in his arms the way a man cradles a sleeping child.

As Karl watched, a man at the front of the section he could see fell down in the road. He fell forward, slowly, knees first, then hips, then face. He did not put his hands out. The men behind him stepped over him. Not around him — over him. None of them looked down. The snow began at once to drift up the side of him.

Twenty paces further on, a horse staggered out of the column and went down. Three men were on it before it had stopped kicking, kneeling around it in the road with small knives in their hands. For a moment Karl thought they were trying to help it — cutting the harness, perhaps, or putting it out of its misery. Then he understood that they were not. They were cutting strips of flesh from the haunch, fast, working with the practiced quickness of men who had butchered an animal many times before. The horse was still alive. One of them, with his knife in his teeth, said something to it in French — almost tenderly, the way one speaks to a hurt child — and the horse made a low sound, and the men kept cutting.

Karl had to look away. He stood with his hand on the gatepost and his eyes on the snow at his feet, and his breath was very loud in his ears. He would not be sick. He would not be sick. He waited a long moment for it to pass. The men were not cruel, he thought — he kept thinking it, almost helplessly — they were only hungry. They were only hungry. He kept saying it to himself.

Behind him, footsteps came up the path. He did not turn; he knew the step.

Johann came and stood beside him. Johann was fourteen now. He did not speak. He put both his hands deep in the pockets of his coat and watched, and his face was very pale, and his face was very still, and his face after a while was the face of a different boy than the one who had thrown grain at the rooster six months ago. They watched together.

After a while Karl said: “Go find Father. Tell him.”

Johann ran.

Within ten minutes Father and Michael were at the gate with axes — not raised, but carried at thigh-height, where they could be seen. A small group of perhaps six men had broken from the column and was stumbling up the path through the snow toward the gate. They had decided, by some unspoken signal among themselves, that the small thread of smoke from the chimney of a Lutheran farmhouse was their only chance.

They stopped, when they saw the axes, ten paces from the gate. The man at the front put both hands up, palms out. He had been crying, and the tears had frozen on his cheeks in a small white rime.

“Bitte,” he said. The German was bad. “Bitte. Brot. Wasser.”

Behind Karl the kitchen door opened. Mother came out in her shawl with a great brown loaf in one arm and an iron kettle of hot flour-water steaming in the other. She walked past her husband and her sons and the axes, out of the gate, into the snow, alone. She set the kettle in the snow at the men's feet. She broke the loaf in her hands — broke it in halves and quarters, fast, the way she broke bread at her own table — and gave a piece into each pair of hands. She did not give it to the strongest first. She gave it to the man who looked least likely to stand up. She moved among them the way she moved among her own children with bread.

There was a man at the back of the small group, older than the others, the only one wearing the rags of the blue. He had no hat. The end of his nose was a black knob. His right hand he held tucked into his coat against his chest as if it hurt to look at it. He took the bread from Mother in his left hand and chewed it very slowly — the way an animal that has only just remembered being a man chews.

He looked up. His eyes met Karl's, and lingered, and did not move on. Karl knew him then — the grey hair, the lined sun-darkened face under it, the missing teeth. It was the same man who had stood at the spring on the rise in June with a tin canteen on his hip and said Russland ist groß. The man, looking back at him now, did not know him. The man was looking at him the way a man looks at a wall.

Karl took a tin cup of the hot flour-water from his mother's kettle and brought it across. The man tried to take it with his left hand and could not; his hand shook too much. Karl held the cup to his mouth. The man drank. Some of the water ran down his chin and froze at once on his coat.

“Danke,” the man whispered. “Danke, Junge.”

Karl said, very quietly, “Was ist passiert?”

The man did not answer for a long time. He was looking at Karl's face and seeing something that was not Karl's face. After a long while he said one word.

“Beresina.”

Karl had never heard the word. The sergeant said it the way a man says a name. A river, Karl thought, looking at his face. A river, and every man he had marched east with in June. The sergeant would not say the word again to anyone.

“Alle tot,” the sergeant said. “Alle tot. Alle.”

Then he looked past Karl, and saw Mother, who had come up beside him with the kettle in her hand and was watching him. He looked at her white linen cap, and at her face under it, and Mother — forty-nine years old, with the strong calm face of a woman who had run a household and brought children up through fevers and bad winters — looked back at him as if he were one of them.

Something gave in his face.

“Meine Mutter,” he said. “Meine Mutter —”

And could not finish.

Mother set the kettle down in the snow. She lifted her hand — her bare red kitchen hand — and laid the back of it against the sergeant's frostbitten cheek, very gently, the way she might lay it against the cheek of one of her own when he had been sick all night.

“Geh nach Hause, mein Sohn,” she said. “Geh nach Hause.”

The sergeant wept. He wept without sound, the tears going down his cheeks and freezing in his stubble. He turned his face into Mother's hand and wept. After a moment, with the bread in his fist, he turned and stumbled back down the path toward the road. The other men went with him. They did not say goodbye. Within fifteen minutes they had been re-absorbed into the road, within an hour they were out of sight, and within a week, Karl knew without needing to be told, they would be dead in the snow somewhere west of here.

That night the family sat around the Kachelofen and did not speak for a long time. Ernst was asleep in his cradle. Dorothea had fallen asleep against Mother's side with her cheek on her mother's apron. Christian was on the floor with his back against Tasso and his hand idly in the dog's ruff. Johann sat closer to Karl on the bench than he usually sat; Karl could feel his brother's shoulder against his own.

Father, looking into the fire under the iron grate, said quietly: “This land will not stay quiet. The French will fall. The Russians will come. And then someone else. We are in the road.”

Mother said: “Where would we go?”

He did not answer.

Karl glanced sideways at his brother. Johann was staring into the fire, and there was something in his face that Karl had not seen there before — not fear, not grief, but a kind of wakefulness. Some small thing in him had opened tonight, looking at the men in the road, that would not close again.

III.  Sunday, and the Road East

The Russians came through, and then the Prussians. A typhus winter took Pastor Hartmann's wife. Two harvests came in, both thin. Word reached the village at Easter that Napoleon had fallen, that he had been sent to an island called Elba, that a king had been put back on the French throne. The Duchy of Warsaw was collapsing, and no one in the village yet knew what they would be.

Karl was seventeen. Johann was fifteen. Michael was twenty. Father's hair had gone grey at the temples in two winters.

On Sunday morning the household put on its Sunday clothes. Mother fastened her white linen cap, took the Gesangbuch under her arm, and they walked up the dirt road to the colony's small wooden Lutheran church together — the whole German colony converging on the low tower from across the fields. The bell was already ringing as the Isaaks came over the rise. Pastor Hartmann — grey now, widowed — stood at the door with his hands folded, naming each of them as they came past.

Inside, the men sat on the right and the women on the left in the old way. The wooden benches were dark with sixty years of use. They sang Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott in four parts, the women's voices riding the men's underneath, no organ, only the precentor with his pitch-pipe and the congregation. Karl, between Father and Michael on the men's side, opened his mouth and sang and felt the hymn lift out of him.

---

After the service the colony spilled out into the spring sun and broke into knots in the churchyard. The men went to the wall under the linden. The women clustered around the church door. The young people drifted to the edges, and at the far end, in the dirt by the wall, the younger children had begun some game with sticks — a mock duel by the look of it, with Tasso (who had come along, as he sometimes did) chasing the thrown sticks and refusing to bring them back. Christian and Dorothea were among them. Ernst, four now, toddled between the bigger children, and one of them caught him neatly by the back of his coat each time he wandered toward the road.

The Leischners came out behind the Isaaks, the way they always did, and made for them across the trodden grass.

They came in their Sunday order. Mr. Leischner, square-shouldered, with the short grey beard and the deep careful voice he had always had. Katherina at his elbow, thin and sharp-eyed, who said something to Mother that Karl did not catch and got back one of Mother's rare returning laughs. Rosina, seventeen, Karl's age, tall and slim with thick chestnut braids; she said good morning to Karl from under her lashes in the way she had, and walked past him toward Johann.

And, last, Wilhelm. Thirteen, lit up at the sight of Karl, almost running across the grass.

“Karl. Karl. Come and see.”

Wilhelm caught Karl's sleeve and pulled. Karl let himself be pulled. From the pocket of his Sunday coat Wilhelm produced a small grey-brown stone, chipped on one edge to something that might have been an arrow point. He had found it on Wednesday in the south field, he said, and was almost certain it was old. Karl turned it gravely in his hand and asked the right questions in the right places, and Wilhelm, under his attention, went very slightly still.

Karl handed the stone back. Over Wilhelm's shoulder, against the church wall, he could see Father and Mr. Leischner standing a little apart from everyone else, talking low. Mother and Katherina had gone to the church door together, arm in arm. Katherina was laughing at something Mother had said, but the laugh was softer than her usual laugh.

He looked back at Wilhelm. He looked, past Wilhelm, at Johann — Johann leaning against the churchyard wall with his hands in his pockets, watching the younger children play and pretending not to. Rosina had drifted over to him. She was saying something to him about the sermon. Johann answered without quite looking at her, and Rosina did not move on. She lingered. Johann did not notice he was being lingered at.

Karl watched it for the time it took to draw a breath. He felt his mouth twitch into the first smile of the chapter. He said nothing, and he filed it away.

---

He drifted closer to the church wall, with Wilhelm at his elbow turning the stone in his hand, and caught the end of it.

Mr. Leischner was telling Father something quietly, slowly, as if the words had been in his mouth a long time.

“We are going east, Michel. Katherina and I have decided. The whole house. To Bessarabia.”

Father said nothing for a moment. Then he said, “When?”

“After the harvest. Late summer.”

Mr. Leischner went on, in pieces. The Russian Tsar Alexander had issued a Manifest — free land for German colonists in his new southern provinces, on the Black Sea. Tax exemption. Freedom from military service. Freedom of religion: they could build their own Lutheran churches and call their own pastors. A cousin of Mr. Leischner's had gone last summer, to a place called Kulm, named for the old Kulm in West Prussia. The cousin had written. The land was good, the cousin said. The wheat grew tall. There was no Napoleon there, and there would be no French sergeant on the road.

Father asked two practical questions only.

“How far?”

“Hundreds of miles.”

“All of you?”

“All of us.”

The two men stood together against the church wall. They did not speak for a long moment. They had stood together against this wall every Sunday for nearly twenty years.

Karl looked across the churchyard at Johann. Johann was no longer leaning casually against the wall. Johann was listening; he had heard. His face had the same fixed, awake, hungry look Karl had seen on it the night the sergeant came back — the look of a fifteen-year-old who had been waiting for something like this since he was thirteen, and who had at last been given its name. Bessarabia. Wagons. East. Rosina was still standing beside him, talking. Johann was no longer answering.

Karl looked at the boy beside him. Wilhelm had stopped turning the stone. He had heard. He was staring at the ground, and the lit-up thirteen-year-old who had run across the grass five minutes ago to show Karl the stone was gone. In his place was a small boy who had just learned, here in his own churchyard, from his own father, that he was leaving.

Karl put his hand on Wilhelm's shoulder. Just for a moment. He did not say anything. Wilhelm did not look up, but Karl felt the small body lean, very slightly, into his hand.

Across the churchyard, by the door, Mother had reached Katherina. They were standing close together with their heads almost touching. Mother's hand had found hers. Mother said, very quietly, only the one word: “Käthe.”

Katherina's eyes were wet but she was not weeping. She put her free hand over Mother's, so that her two hands held Mother's one.

“I know,” she said. “I know, Lieschen. I know.”

They had been each other's company at every birth and every death for nearly twenty years. They stood that way — their hands stacked together at the church door — and did not say anything else for a while.

---

The Isaaks walked home together across the fields in the late Sunday morning. The light was high and clean. Mother walked ahead with Father, Ernst on her hip with his small head against her neck. Michael walked with Christian. Dorothea was between them. Tasso had come out across the strip of common pasture and was trotting beside Michael's heel. Above them, slow and high and on the home wing, a stork glided over toward the chimney.

Karl and Johann fell behind. By twenty paces, then thirty. No one called them. Karl thought perhaps Mother had even slowed her pace a little to let them. He could not be sure.

They walked in silence for a long while. Karl knew what his brother was going to say before he said it.

Johann said it, finally, without looking at him. He said it to the road.

“Karl. We could go too.”

Karl did not answer at once. He looked at his father's back ahead of him — the slope of the shoulders, the steady way the man walked. He looked at Mother carrying Ernst. He thought of Mr. Leischner's careful voice against the church wall: We are going east, Michel. He thought of Wilhelm leaning into his hand.

He thought of the sergeant on the road in summer, with the canteen on his hip: Russland ist groß, Junge.

He thought of the sergeant on the road in winter, with the bread in his shaking left hand: Beresina. Alle tot. Alle.

In his pocket, his fingers closed around the French sou. He had been carrying it for two years now, and his thumb had worn Napoleon's profile almost smooth. The Roman wreath was still there, faintly. The man's nose was almost gone.

He looked at his brother.

“Maybe we will,” he said.

Johann nodded once. He did not say anything else. The brothers walked on. Behind them, the rye was green and not yet tall.

Ahead of them, their mother turned and called them home, and they came.
