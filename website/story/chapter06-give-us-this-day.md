## Chapter Six

# Give Us This Day

Kulm, Bessarabia — July 1846

---

The first sunlight reached the fields above Kulm while the village still lay in shade.

From the rise, Gottlieb could see the long street following the hillside, with a row of houses on either side. Their roofs were brown thatch, their walls white. Behind them, the narrow yards ran back through sheds and vegetable gardens to the fields. Farther down, the Kogälnik wound through its green valley. A little mist clung to the reeds. Beyond it, another slope rose into the light.

He found their own roof and held up his thumb. At the right distance, he could hide the whole house.

Then he moved his thumb and there it was again: the house, the yard, the curl of smoke from his mother's cooking fire.

The mare pulled him toward a patch of grass.

“Come on.”

She took another mouthful.

Gottlieb had been sent to fetch her from the horse herd. He was eleven, old enough to be trusted with the horse and still young enough to be asked what had taken him so long. Looking at things was seldom accepted as an answer.

He drew the rope tight. The mare came reluctantly, tearing up one last tuft as she moved.

A lark was singing above the field. He could hear it clearly but could not find it. He walked with his head tipped back until his foot caught a rut, then looked down to see whether anyone had noticed. Only the horse was there.

Along the unplowed edges, the tall grass bent in pale waves. In the hollows stood dark patches of oak. Most of the high ground was open—wheat, pasture, a fallow field going gray with weeds. The wind came over it all and lifted the hair from his damp forehead. It was cool yet. By noon the same wind would feel as though it had crossed an oven.

At the village, gates were opening. A woman emptied a basin into the street. Geese stepped through the water, searching for something worth eating. From a yard came the steady squeak of a well rope, and from another the hard little streams of milk striking a pail.

His father, Karl Isaak, was waiting at their gate.

“Did you have to grow the horse first?”

“She wouldn't come.”

His father looked at the mare, who was still chewing.

“I see she suffered on the journey.”

He took the rope. Gottlieb went in for breakfast.

At the door he brushed the wall with his shoulder and came away white. He rubbed at the mark. That spread it.

His mother kept the house whitewashed, from the low stone foundation to the thatch. Beneath the whitewash, the walls were clay and straw pressed into blocks and dried in the sun. Beside the door, where a piece had chipped away, short yellow ends of straw showed through.

Inside, the room was cool and dim, with the beds crowded along the walls. In winter the great stove took up more room than anyone could spare and was worth every bit of it. Now it was cold. His mother, Elisabeth, cooked in the shed outside so the house would stay cool under its thick thatch.

He found her there, with a cake of dried dung smoldering beneath a pot of water. Through the sharp, grassy smoke he could smell bread. A swallow swept past his ear, so close that he ducked. Its nest was tucked beneath a beam, with a row of open mouths showing over the muddy rim.

“They'll take your head off one morning,” his mother said.

“They know where they're going.”

“So do you, I hope. Wash.”

She poured water over his hands. He turned them obediently, watching the dust run away. She caught his chin and rubbed a wet thumb beside his mouth.

“And that.”

“What?”

“Whatever you were saving.”

The table stood in the shade beside the house. His eldest brother, Gottfried, sat at the far end with the two other older boys. At the near end, August was trying to keep the youngest from reaching his cup. Little Johann, four years old, had his shirt on backward. He saw Gottlieb coming and shuffled along the bench, patting the place beside him.

Gottlieb squeezed in. Seven sons around one table, and Gottfried took up nearly as much room as two of them. Their mother rescued August's cup and lifted the youngest onto her lap. Johann pushed the loaf toward Gottlieb with both hands.

Gottlieb reached for the crust. Then his father joined them, and they folded their hands.

During the blessing he watched a fly walk along the crust he wanted.

*Give us this day our daily bread.*

The fly reached the far end. He wished his father would finish before it came back.

“Amen.”

He took the crust.

The bread pulled at his teeth. He dipped it in his milk and caught the dripping piece in his mouth before it could fall apart. The milk was still warm from the cow, rich enough to leave his lips slick. He licked them and drank again.

Across the yard, the mare stamped at flies. His father had tied her in the shade.

His mother set aside a loaf and wrapped it in a cloth.

“Take this to your cousin before we go,” she told him. “Put your clean shirt on first.”

He glanced toward the house, where she had laid it out.

“Will we go to the field afterward?”

“Of course.”

There was a baby to be buried that morning.

---

His cousin Johann Carl, Uncle Michael's son, met him at the gate. His eyes were swollen. He took the loaf in both hands and nodded without speaking.

Gottlieb stepped into the yard. A little wooden box stood on two stools in the shade of the house. He had almost passed it before he saw the white cloth inside and understood that it was the coffin.

There were people in the yard already, speaking quietly. Gottlieb stood near the gate until his parents arrived. Then he went with them to look.

Carl's baby was about five months old. The baby's name was August—the same as Gottlieb's little brother.

The baby lay in a white shirt, a cloth drawn up to the chest and the small hands resting on top. The cheeks were gray-white and waxy. A purple patch showed below one ear, disappearing beneath the collar. The baby's mouth was slightly open, the lips dark and shriveled at the edges. One eye showed through a slit between the lids, dull and filmed over.

A fly landed on the baby's lower lip and crawled toward the open mouth. Gottlieb pressed his own lips together. The baby's mother, Wilhelmina, brushed it away.

Up close, he could smell the new wood and, beneath it, something sweet and rotten. He swallowed and moved back half a step.

For a moment he watched the cloth, waiting for it to rise.

Wilhelmina bent over the coffin and straightened a sleeve that was already straight. Then she did the other one. A tear fell on the baby's wrist. She wiped it away with her thumb, then lowered her face until her cheek rested against the baby's.

A sob shook her. She tried to draw breath and sobbed again. Her father, Mr. Roloff, put a hand on her back. Carl stood beside her, wiping his face with the heel of his hand. His cheeks were still wet when he lowered it.

Gottlieb looked away. A neighbor woman stood a little apart with Carl and Wilhelmina's other boy on her hip. He was not yet three and kept twisting toward the ground. She shifted him to her other arm and held him there.

The schoolmaster opened his book. His voice was lower here than in the schoolroom. Men took off their caps. The singing began uncertainly, then filled out as everyone found the tune.

Gottlieb joined in. He liked singing with other people. Alone, his voice sounded thin and rather foolish; with the village around him, he could sing as loudly as he liked.

Across the yard, a girl's voice rose clear above those nearest her.

Louise Schulz stood beside her mother, holding the edge of her sleeve. Mrs. Schulz rested a hand on her round belly. Mr. Schulz held the hymnbook open for them. Beside him stood their son Wilhelm, who shared Gottlieb's bench at school. He gave Gottlieb a quick nod over the book.

In winter Louise sat among the smaller girls and bit her lower lip when she copied her letters. Sometimes she bent so close to the page that he wondered how she kept her nose out of the ink.

She looked up. He looked back at the book in the schoolmaster's hands, though he was much too far away to read it.

The schoolmaster read, and they bowed their heads for the prayer. As the next hymn began, a man lifted the lid of the coffin.

Wilhelmina reached in once more and laid her hand on the baby's chest. The man waited. At last she drew it back.

The first hammer blow made Gottlieb flinch. The next sounded louder. Wilhelmina pressed her hand against her mouth while the man worked along the edge. Gottlieb could hear her crying through the hymn.

A man lifted the coffin from the stools and carried it in both arms. The schoolmaster led them out into the street. Carl and Wilhelmina came last.

The sun had reached the houses. Dust rose around their shoes and settled on the hems of the women's dresses. A dog followed them until someone sent it back. Gottlieb's clean collar scratched his neck. He put a finger inside it and pulled, then let go when his mother glanced at him.

At the grave they sang again. Then the schoolmaster said the child's name: August Isaak.

Beside Gottlieb, his little brother looked up. He was six, with a loose thread hanging from the cuff he had been picking at. Gottlieb reached for his hand.

Across the grave stood their cousin August, Uncle Johann's son. At thirteen he already held his cap in front of him in both hands, the way the men did. He caught Gottlieb looking at him, and for a moment neither looked away.

His brother tugged at Gottlieb's hand. He had been holding it too tightly. When he loosened his grip, August left two fingers hooked around his.

Gottlieb looked back at the coffin.

The grave was small, but the heap beside it seemed large. Black earth lay on top, with pale clay in the clods brought up from below. A beetle climbed a dry stalk at the edge. When the stalk bent under its weight, it opened its wings and flew.

The schoolmaster read Christ's words about the little children.

His father stood bareheaded, his cap held against his stomach. Sweat ran down beside his ear. He did not wipe it away.

They said the Lord's Prayer. The schoolmaster gave the blessing, and another hymn began. The coffin went down beneath their voices. Earth struck the lid with a hollow sound, then a softer one.

When the singing and the shovels stopped, the baby's parents knelt beside the mound. Everyone waited in silence.

The mourners began to leave in little groups. His mother stayed behind.

She went a short way along the children's graves and knelt. Gottlieb followed. There were two places here that belonged to them: his little sisters, both dead before he was old enough to remember them properly. He thought he remembered one of them crying. But there had always been someone crying in the house, and he could no longer be sure.

His mother pulled a weed from beside a cross. He crouched to help. Its root came up with a damp black clump attached, and he shook the earth back onto the grave.

She rested her hands in her lap and bowed her head.

He waited.

Beyond her were more little mounds, some flat enough to miss if the crosses had not been there. Farther along, among the older graves, Carl had stopped beside a weathered cross. His brother Christian stood with him.

Gottlieb could read the name: Michael Isaak. Under it, 1831. Their father, dead before Gottlieb was born.

Carl crouched and put a hand on the cross, near the ground. Gottlieb's father went over and rested a hand on his shoulder. Christian stood close beside them, looking down. None of them spoke.

When his mother stood, there was dirt on the front of her skirt. Gottlieb brushed it off for her.

She let him.

At the cemetery gate, his mother loosened his collar.

“Go home and change. Fold this one.”

He nodded and went ahead. Behind him, some of the mourners were turning back toward Carl's house for the funeral meal.

---

He was halfway home when he saw the camels.

Two of them stood harnessed to a wagon beside a watering trough. One was drinking. The other had turned its long neck around to examine the load, as though it suspected somebody had put something extra on it.

Gottlieb stopped.

He had seen camels before. That did not make them look any more sensible. Each had two humps, a dangling lower lip, and enough neck to reach places a neck had no business reaching. Their winter hair was coming off in hanging patches. The nearer one shifted its feet. Even standing still, it seemed to have too many knees.

A Tatar trader sat in the wagon’s shade, eating bread, with bundles of wool roped behind his seat. He said something to the drinking camel in a language Gottlieb did not understand. The animal went on drinking.

The nearer camel turned and regarded Gottlieb with one enormous brown eye. It had the longest eyelashes he had ever seen. Its lower lip hung loose, and its jaw moved from side to side as though whatever it was eating had disappointed it.

Gottlieb let his own lip droop and chewed at nothing.

“What are you doing?”

Louise stood behind him.

“Nothing.”

“You were making its face.”

“That’s how it eats.”

“Show me.”

He did it again, a little less boldly. She laughed.

The camel gave a deep, gargling groan, like an old man complaining about his dinner. Gottlieb stepped back.

The trader looked from the boy to the camel. Then he slowly worked his own jaw in the same circle.

Louise bent double. Gottlieb felt his ears grow hot, but the man’s eyes were creased with laughter, and he began to laugh too.

“Does it spit?” he asked.

The trader nodded.

“When it’s angry?”

Another nod.

“Is it angry now?”

The man considered his animal.

“Always.”

Gottlieb moved another step away.

The trader finished his bread and brushed his hands. As he stood, Gottlieb pointed at the bundles.

“Wool?”

The man pulled a loose tuft from beneath a rope and held it out. Gottlieb took it. He had expected something coarse and prickly. It was soft.

The trader patted the camel’s neck. When it swung its head toward him, he pushed its nose aside as easily as Gottlieb’s mother moved a child away from the bread.

Louise touched the wool with one finger.

The man checked the harness and climbed onto the wagon. At his call, the camels leaned forward. Their great feet came down so softly that Gottlieb could hear the wheels more clearly than the animals.

He raised a hand. The trader lifted his in return.

“Are you keeping that?” Louise asked.

“Perhaps.”

She looked at him as though this was a peculiar thing to do. Then her mother called from farther up the street, and she ran to catch her.

Gottlieb put the wool in his pocket.

---

By the time they reached the field, the light was too bright to look into without narrowing his eyes.

His father and his older brothers cut. Gottlieb followed with his rake, drawing the fallen wheat together. The stalks rasped against one another. When he bent to gather them, the stiff heads scratched his forearms and caught in his shirt.

He had changed out of his clean linen. This shirt was softer, worn thin at the elbows. By the end of the first row it clung to his back.

The wheat was uneven. In one hollow it stood thick enough to hide the lower half of his father's legs. Farther up, where the ground dried first, it was short and thin. There were patches where Gottlieb could see too much earth between the stalks.

His father cut those too.

The scythe whispered, stopped, and whispered again. Gottfried worked beyond him. His shirt hung open at the throat, and sweat ran from his chin when he bent.

Gottlieb tried to keep pace. For a while he made a game of finishing one bundle before his father laid down the next swath. Then his father found his rhythm, and Gottlieb lost.

A grasshopper struck his cheek. He brushed at it angrily. Another sat on the rake handle, moving its jaws. When he brought his face close, it sprang away.

He wished he could do that. One jump and be at the water jug.

“Go and drink,” his father said without turning.

Gottlieb went.

The jug stood beneath a cloth in the narrow shade of the wagon. He lifted it with both hands. The first swallow tasted of warm clay. The second was better. Water ran down his chin into the opening of his shirt, and he let it.

When he straightened, his father was holding out his hand.

Gottlieb passed him the jug.

His father drank, wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist, and looked over the field. There were white hairs in his beard now. A crack at the base of his thumb had opened again; a little dark blood marked the handle of the scythe.

Gottlieb rubbed the sore place on his own palm.

“Did you have wheat here when you came?”

His father shook his head.

“Grass. Taller than you.”

“All this?”

“All this.”

Gottlieb looked across the cut rows, trying to put the grass back.

“And no houses?”

“Not ours.”

“Mutter says you lived in the ground.”

“Your mother lived there too.”

“Was it like our cellar?”

His father considered that.

“More people. Less wine.”

Gottlieb smiled. His father handed him the jug and returned to the wheat.

Later they ate in the wagon's shade. His father cut bread and an onion with his knife. The sheep's cheese was white and damp, breaking into rough crumbs beneath Gottlieb's thumb. It smelled a little of the sheep pen. The salt stung the split in his lip, but the cheese softened against his tongue, rich and sour, and he took another bite.

He tried to make it last as long as the bread. A crumb fell on his trousers. He picked it off and ate it, then bit into a cucumber, warm from the basket and still crisp enough to snap.

Gottfried lay back with his cap over his face. Gottlieb wanted to do the same, but a stork had come down beyond the cut strip.

It walked with great careful steps, lifting each foot clear of the stalks. Then its red bill darted down. Gottlieb leaned forward to see what it had caught.

“You'll wear your neck out,” Gottfried said from under his cap.

“How can you see?”

“I know you.”

Gottlieb lay back.

When his father stood, they stood with him.

---

By evening Gottlieb's hands hurt whenever he closed them. Chaff had worked down his neck and into his trousers. He stopped trying to pick it out.

Then the light changed.

The wheat warmed to gold. Shadows stretched from the bundles, long and blue, and every loose hair on his father's head caught the sun.

Gottlieb straightened. The sun hung above the western ridge, large and orange. He could follow the wind across the field by the darkening and brightening of the wheat.

A thin cloud turned red underneath. Farther overhead, the sky was still blue. He tried to find the place where one color became the other and could not.

“Sky-watcher,” Gottfried called.

Gottlieb did not turn.

Beside him, his father's scythe had stopped too.

They stood together until the sun touched the ridge. Then his father bent and picked up a fallen bundle.

“These last ones.”

Gottlieb bent to help.

On the way home, the wheels creaked and the wheat rustled behind them. Above the valley, the sky kept its light long after the road had gone gray.

He could smell supper before they turned in.

He washed at the door. The water found every scratch. His mother picked straw from his hair while he shook it from his shoes.

His father gave thanks. Gottlieb broke a potato against the side of his bowl and stirred the pieces into the broth. The onions were soft and sweet. There was enough bread for a second piece, and he wiped his bowl with it.

After supper he sat on the doorstep and took the wool from his pocket. His mother looked up from her sewing.

“What have you brought home?”

“Camel wool.”

She felt it. He had expected her to tell him to throw it away.

“Soft,” she said.

“Louise touched it too.”

His mother glanced at him.

He wished he had not said that. He began picking a bit of straw from the wool.

“Put it somewhere before I wash those trousers.”

He set it on the windowsill.

When they knelt for prayers, he was so tired that he lost his place. His mother's voice carried him through until he found it again.

The straw mattress gave beneath his aching back. August crowded against him, warm from head to foot.

“Move over.”

His brother moved an inch.

Gottlieb could feel him breathing. He thought of the baby's white shirt and the cloth that had not moved. For a while he listened to the small breaths beside him.

Then he turned his pillow to the cool side.

Through the open window, a pale strip of sky showed above the roof across the street. He watched until the first star appeared there.

He meant to stay awake for the next one.
