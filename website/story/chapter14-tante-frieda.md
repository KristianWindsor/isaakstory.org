## Chapter Fourteen

# Tante Frieda

Eureka, South Dakota — 1932 to 1934

---

The wheat did not come up in the spring of 1931.

It had come up in 1929 and it had come up in 1930 but the rains in 1930 had been small and the rains in 1931 did not come at all, and what went into the ground in April stayed in the ground, and by June the fields on the edge of town were the grey-brown color of old bone. Wheat that had sold for a dollar twenty a bushel in 1928 sold for twenty-seven cents in 1931, and then for nineteen cents, and then for less than it cost to grow.

Half the storefronts on Main Street went dark.

Isaaks Variety Store did not. Albert and Eva kept the doors open every morning at eight and closed them every evening at six, and the candy jars stayed full and the ready-made dresses from Chicago came in every season and the small bell above the door rang every time a customer walked in, which by the winter of 1932 was not very often. Eva stopped hiring Tante Frieda four mornings a week and started hiring her five, because Eva was in the store all day now and needed someone upstairs with the girls.

Alice was eleven. Edith was five.

---

Tante Frieda cooked on the small porcelain stove by the window with the red geraniums.

The stove put out the smell of fried onions and of the dark pumpernickel she baked twice a week and of the small cup of coffee she kept warming at the back of the range all day. She sang while she cooked. She sang in German, because her English was still not good in 1932 even though she had been in Eureka for five years, and the songs were the songs her own mother had sung in the Volga country before the Russians had come for the men in 1915 and Frieda had made her way alone across the ocean at twenty-two with the small embroidered handkerchief that had been her mother's still folded in the pocket of her coat.

Edith sat at the kitchen table with a pencil in her hand.

The pencil was short. Most pencils in the Isaak apartment in 1932 were short, because Eva did not throw out pencils until they were too small to hold, and Edith had learned from her mother not to waste the last inch. The paper was the back of a sheet from Alice's school arithmetic tablet that Alice had finished with. Edith was drawing the letter *A*.

"*Ah,*" Tante Frieda said from the stove. "*A wie Apfel.*"

"A for apple."

"Apple. *Apfel.*"

"Apple. *Ah-p-fel.*"

"Good, *Schatz.*"

Edith drew the letter *B*. Then *C*. Her *C* was backwards.

Tante Frieda came over from the stove and turned the paper around and looked at the backwards *C* and laughed — the small warm laugh she had brought across the ocean in her pocket alongside the handkerchief — and she took the pencil in her own hand and drew a *C* the right way round beside Edith's, and said *siehst du, Liebling. So.* See, darling. Like this.

Edith looked at the two *C*s on the paper side by side. Then she drew another one underneath, the right way round this time.

"Good. *Sehr gut.*"

Edith held the pencil very tight and drew a fourth *C*. And a fifth. And a sixth. Then she turned the paper over and started the alphabet again on the new side, because there was still room, and wasting paper was worse than wasting pencils. Tante Frieda went back to the stove and the onions.

---

Edith was sick that winter.

It started as a cough in the first week of January and by the second week she had a fever and by the third week the fever had not broken and the doctor from Aberdeen drove up through the snow in his Model A and came up the narrow wooden stairs to the apartment with his black leather bag and listened to her chest with the cold metal stethoscope and said it was whooping cough and there was not very much he could do. Whooping cough was whooping cough. You gave it water and honey and you kept the child warm and you waited, and most children got through it, and some did not, and the knowing which was which was a thing God did and not a thing a doctor did.

Tante Frieda did not leave the apartment for nine days.

She slept on the kitchen floor on a folded blanket beside the bedroom door, and she got up every hour through the night to put a cool cloth on Edith's forehead and to spoon small amounts of honey-water into her mouth and to listen to the small terrible whooping sound the child made when the coughing came. Eva came up from the store every two hours and looked in on them and went back down, because Eva had a store to run and customers who needed her and a husband who needed her in the back office, and Eva could not be the one sitting beside Edith's bed, and both Eva and Tante Frieda knew this and neither of them said it out loud.

On the ninth night the fever broke.

Tante Frieda was holding Edith's hand on the pillow in the dark when she felt the skin of the child's forehead go cool under her other hand, and she waited for the coolness to go away again — because sometimes the fever broke and came back, and sometimes the coolness was the last coolness before the end — and the coolness did not go away. It stayed. Tante Frieda began to cry very quietly without making any sound, and then she got up from the chair and went into the kitchen and put her forehead against the cold glass of the window over the sink and said the small German prayer she had not said since she had been a girl in the Volga country before the men had been taken.

Edith slept for the first time in nine days.

When she woke up in the morning she was thin and weak and the skin under her eyes was the color of skim milk, but she was alive. Tante Frieda brought her a cup of warm goat's milk and a small square of honey bread, and Edith ate half the bread and drank all of the milk, and then she looked up at the woman beside her bed and said, in the small clear voice of a child who had decided something in the night:

"*Tante. Teach me to pray.*"

Tante Frieda sat down on the edge of the bed.

"*In Deutsch oder auf Englisch, Liebling?*"

"Both."

"Both." Tante Frieda smiled the small warm smile. "*Dann beten wir in beiden Sprachen.*"

And she taught her the Lord's Prayer in German first — *Vater unser, der du bist im Himmel, geheiligt werde dein Name* — one line at a time, waiting for Edith to repeat each line before moving to the next, the way her own mother had taught her in a kitchen in the Volga country in 1901. Then she taught her the same prayer in English. *Our Father, which art in Heaven, hallowed be Thy name.* Edith learned both in one morning, because Edith was five and a half and her brain was the brain of a child who had been learning the alphabet in two languages for a year and was ready for more, and because Tante Frieda was a good teacher, and because the prayer was the same prayer in both languages and the sameness was the point.

From that morning on, Edith said the Lord's Prayer every night before she went to sleep. She said it first in German and then in English, in the small clear voice she had used when she had asked to be taught, and she said it to the German God her great-great-grandfather had learned to pray to in Kulm in 1840 and to the English God her great-granddaughter would someday pray to in a language neither Gottlieb nor Tante Frieda would have recognized, and the God on the other end of the prayer was the same God, and the God heard her.

---

Tante Frieda left in April of 1934.

She left to marry a widower in Bismarck, a German baker who had written to her through a mutual cousin and had come down to Eureka in March to meet her and had decided within two hours that he wanted to marry her, and Tante Frieda had been forty-one years old and alone in Eureka and had decided the same. The wedding was going to be in Bismarck at the end of the month. She told Eva first. She did not tell Edith, because Tante Frieda had never been any good at telling hard things to people she loved, and she had been putting off telling Edith for three weeks, and in the end she did not tell her at all.

On a Tuesday morning Tante Frieda was there at the kitchen stove making oatmeal, and on a Wednesday morning she was not, and Edith came out into the kitchen at seven with her hair uncombed and her nightgown still on and saw the empty stove and the empty chair at the table and the small embroidered handkerchief folded on the oilcloth where Tante Frieda always sat, and she stopped in the middle of the kitchen floor and did not move for a long moment and then she began to cry.

She cried for three days.

Eva could not comfort her. Albert could not comfort her. Alice, at thirteen, tried to brush her little sister's hair and read to her from a book and did not help. Edith cried in the way a six-year-old cries when the first person she has loved outside her own family has left without saying goodbye, and the crying was enormous and without bottom, and at the end of the third day the crying stopped because Edith had run out of tears, not because anything inside her had resolved.

She picked up the handkerchief Tante Frieda had left on the oilcloth.

It was a small square of white cotton with blue forget-me-nots embroidered in one corner in the small careful cross-stitch Tante Frieda's mother had done in the Volga country in 1895. Edith had never seen Tante Frieda without it. It had lived in the pocket of Tante Frieda's apron every day for the two years Tante Frieda had been in the apartment, and Tante Frieda had left it on the table on her last morning because Tante Frieda was a woman who believed in leaving one good thing behind when she left a place, and the handkerchief was the best thing she had.

Edith kept it.

She put it in the small wooden box under her bed where she kept the things she did not want to lose. The box had two marbles and a dried pressed flower and the backwards *C* she had drawn on the back of Alice's arithmetic paper in the winter of 1932 and now it had the handkerchief. She did not take the handkerchief out of the box for three years. She did not need to. She knew it was in there.

That afternoon she went up to the attic above the store.

Nobody had ever told her the attic was a place you could go. It was reached by a small wooden staircase at the end of the apartment's back hallway, and the door at the top of the staircase was not locked but it was stiff, and Edith had to push hard with her shoulder to get it open. Inside, the attic was one long room running the whole length of the building above the store — low ceilings, bare rafters, the dust-smell of old paper and old wood and the sweet metal smell of the tin roof warmed by the April sun. A small window at the far end looked out across Main Street. There were two trunks and a broken chair and a cardboard box of Alice's old school papers and Edith's old school papers and the backs of envelopes Eva had saved because Eva did not throw out paper that still had one side blank on it.

Edith sat down on the floor beside the cardboard box.

She took a sheet of paper from the box. It was the back of a bill from the wholesaler in Chicago that had come in the mail in 1933. She found a pencil stub in the pocket of her dress — a pencil she had been using for a week, now down to an inch and a half — and she put the paper on the floor in front of her and wrote, at the top of the page, in her careful six-year-old letters:

*The Story of Tante Frieda*

She wrote for an hour. She wrote what she remembered — the onions and the pumpernickel and the singing in German at the stove, the alphabet on the back of Alice's arithmetic paper, the nine nights of the whooping cough and the cool cloth on her forehead, the Lord's Prayer in two languages, the handkerchief with the forget-me-nots — and when the paper was full she turned it over and wrote on the back, and when the back was full she took another sheet out of the box. By the time Eva came up to find her for supper Edith had written six pages, front and back, in the careful unpracticed letters of a child who had been going to school for less than a year.

Eva knelt on the dusty floor beside her daughter and read the pages. She did not say anything. When she had finished reading she folded the pages in half and handed them back to Edith and put her hand on Edith's hair for a long moment and then she said, very quietly:

"You kept her."

"I kept her."

"Come down for supper."

Edith came down for supper. She put the six pages in the wooden box under her bed on top of Tante Frieda's handkerchief. That night, before she went to sleep, she said the Lord's Prayer in German and then in English, in the clear small voice she had used every night since the whooping cough, and she said it into the dark of the bedroom where her sister Alice was already asleep in the other bed, and the God on the other end of the prayer was the same God, and the God heard her.

The next morning she went back up to the attic and started a new story.

She wrote every afternoon after that for the rest of her childhood. Stories on the backs of school papers and on the backs of bills from the wholesaler and on the blank sides of Christmas cards the Isaaks had received and saved. Stories in pencil stubs down to the nub. Stories about Tante Frieda and stories about the lamb on Opa Roesch's lap and stories about the girl who lived in the stone houses on the prairie and stories about nothing at all. The attic above the store became the one place in the world that belonged to her alone, and the stories were the one thing in the world that belonged to her alone, and Edith Allegra Isaak at six years old became a writer, in the small warm tin-roofed attic above a five-and-dime store on the main street of a dying prairie town, in a family that had been writing things down on blank pages since Gottlieb had opened the Bible in 1914.

She did not know that yet. She was six. She thought she was just making stories.

She was right. She was just making stories. That was exactly what a writer was.

---

Sundays they drove to Grandma Roesch's.

Uncle Ed drove them — Albert did not like to drive on the gravel anymore, not since the dust storms had begun, not with Eva and the girls in the back — and the car had a muffler problem that turned every trip into a shouting match with the engine. Edith and Alice and their cousin Melvin wedged into the back seat and held their ears and shouted over the noise and were deaf by the time they reached the farm.

Grandma had fried chicken.

She had killed the chickens that morning herself, the way she killed chickens every Sunday morning, the way she had been killing chickens since she was nine years old in the Volga country in 1870. She had decapitated them on the chopping block in the yard with the hatchet her husband had sharpened on the grindstone the day before she had buried him in 1930. She had plucked them. She had eviscerated them. She had fried them in lard in the big cast-iron skillet on the wood range, and the skin was crackling brown and the meat was the color of cream and the smell filled the farmhouse kitchen from the front door to the back porch.

"Edith. Sit. Eat."

"I'm not hungry, Grandma."

"You are hungry. Sit."

Edith sat. She was always hungry. She ate a drumstick and a wing and a small cloud of mashed potatoes with butter. She drank a glass of milk from the morning's cows still warm in the glass. Grandma Roesch sat at the head of the table with the wooden spoon beside her plate that she had used to bite down on during the labor of her fourteen children in the back bedroom of the farmhouse, and the teeth-marks on the spoon were still visible in the wood, and Edith looked at the teeth-marks and then at her mashed potatoes and then at the teeth-marks again and made the small private decision not to ask about them this Sunday.

After dinner the girls played house in the yard with stones.

Each girl gathered a handful and laid them on the dirt to make the walls of her house and the doors. You had to enter through the door. Stepping over the wall was the worst crime a girl could commit on a Sunday afternoon at Grandma Roesch's, worse even than using the scissors — because scissors on Sunday was a sin against God, but stepping over the wall was a sin against the house.

Edith's house had four rooms.

She laid out the rooms carefully. A kitchen by the door, a sitting room beyond, a bedroom for the dolls, and a small fourth room at the back that she did not tell the other girls about. The fourth room was an attic. It was where the girl who lived in the stone house would go to write her stories.

She did not tell the other girls that either.

---

They played the fly game in the entry hall off the kitchen.

The entry hall was the best ground — the smell of Grandma's chicken had drawn every fly on the prairie in through the screen door, and the girls took turns with the swatter, and Grandma encouraged them from the kitchen because Grandma had been fighting flies since 1870.

"I got four!" Cousin Elsie yelled.

"I got six!"

Edith had the swatter. She lifted it slowly the way Grandma had taught her, the way a hunter lifts a bow. She brought it down.

Eleven.

Eleven flies in one swat.

The other girls counted them twice to be sure. They counted them a third time to make Edith count with them. Eleven flies on the floorboards of the entry hall, eleven small dark bodies in a loose arc under the swatter where the swatter had come down, and Edith Allegra Isaak at six and a half years old with the flyswatter still in her hand and the look on her face of a child who had just discovered she was good at something without having known she was going to be.

Grandma came in from the kitchen.

Grandma looked at the eleven flies. Grandma looked at Edith. Grandma, in her whole seventy-two years, had never killed more than six flies in a single swat, and she knew every fly-killer in McPherson County because she had been the reigning fly-killer of McPherson County since 1891.

"*Elf.*" Eleven.

"*Elf, Oma.*"

Grandma put her large warm hand on top of Edith's blonde head and left it there for a moment and then she went back to the kitchen without saying anything else, because anything else would have been too much. Edith stood in the entry hall with the swatter in her hand and looked down at the eleven flies and felt the particular quiet pride of a six-year-old who has just done a thing her grandmother could not do, and the pride was a thing she would remember for the rest of her life, and the pride was not about the flies.

That night she wrote the story of the eleven flies on the back of a Christmas card from 1932.

---

The drought in 1934 was worse than the drought in 1933 had been, and the drought in 1933 had been worse than the drought in 1932, and nobody in Eureka remembered a summer like the summer of 1934.

The cistern at the apartment above the store had a few inches of water at the bottom, cloudy and tasting of rust and of the inside of the metal tank, and Eva filled one sink basin from the cistern every morning and that basin was asked to do the work of a river. She washed the breakfast dishes in it. She washed her hands in it. She washed the girls' school clothes in it. The water turned grey and then brown and then the color of weak coffee and then the color of strong coffee, and still she used it, because there was no more.

Alice came home from school one afternoon in September with dust in her hair.

She had walked the four blocks home with her handkerchief pressed to her mouth because the air on Main Street had been the color of old paper that afternoon — a wall of brown had been rolling in from the west all morning, and by one o'clock the sky had been brown and by two o'clock the teacher had dismissed the school early, and Alice had walked home through the brown afternoon with her hand over her nose and her eyes half-shut. She came up the wooden stairs and into the kitchen and stood in front of her mother and said, without taking off her coat, *Mama, I do not feel well.*

Eva put her hand on Alice's forehead.

The forehead was hot.

By evening the fever had climbed. By the next morning the doctor from Aberdeen was back in the apartment with his black leather bag and his stethoscope, and he listened to Alice's chest for a long time and then to her throat, and then he looked at the small red rash that had come up on her arms and the inside of her thighs, and his face did the small careful thing a doctor's face does when he has understood and is deciding how to say so.

"It is scarlet fever."

Eva sat down on the edge of Alice's bed.

"How bad."

"Bad enough to keep her in bed for a month. Bad enough to watch her heart when she comes out of it. Scarlet fever in a girl her age can leave things behind — in the heart, in the joints — and the things it leaves behind do not always show themselves right away." The doctor closed his bag. "She will live, Mrs. Isaak. I am almost sure of that. But she will not be the same girl she was a week ago, and I am telling you that now so that you can decide how you want to carry it."

Eva nodded. Eva did not cry in front of the doctor. Eva did not cry in front of Alice. Eva waited until the doctor had gone back down the stairs and Albert had come up from the store and the two of them had closed the bedroom door with Alice sleeping inside it, and then Eva went into the kitchen and put her forehead against the cold glass of the window over the sink the way Tante Frieda had done two and a half years ago in the dark of the whooping cough, and her shoulders shook once.

Alice was in bed for six weeks.

She came out of the fever and the rash went away and the thirteen-year-old daughter who had walked home through the brown afternoon with her hand over her nose was there in the bed again, reading a book, asking for soup, asking when she could go back to school. But the doctor's warning stayed in Eva's pocket like a folded piece of paper she could feel without taking out. Alice's heart had been touched by the fever. The doctor in Aberdeen did not know yet how much. Neither did Alice. Neither did anyone.

Edith at seven sat in the chair beside her sister's bed every afternoon and read to her from the stories she had been writing in the attic. Alice listened. Alice had not known about the stories. She lay on the pillow with her dark hair loose and her face still thin from the fever and listened to her little sister read about Tante Frieda and the stone houses and the eleven flies, and at the end of each story Alice said *again* in the small patient voice of an older sister who has decided that her younger sister's stories are the best thing about being stuck in bed for six weeks, and Edith read the story again.

---

The train came the next winter.

It was a Saturday afternoon in January of 1935 and Albert was driving Alice back from the doctor's office in Aberdeen — the monthly checkup the doctor had insisted on after the scarlet fever, the quiet listening to her heart through the stethoscope to see what the fever had done and what it was going to do. Alice was fourteen now and still thin from the fever and still not quite the girl she had been in 1933, and the checkups were the small regular confession Eva paid for in cash every month that everything was still all right.

The road home was icy.

The snow had come that morning and the afternoon sun had melted the top of it and the evening cold had frozen the melt into a thin grey glaze over the gravel, and Albert was driving slowly because Albert always drove slowly now, with both hands on the wheel and his eyes on the road and Alice beside him in the passenger seat with her coat buttoned to the throat.

They came to the railroad crossing four miles outside Eureka.

Albert saw the train.

The train was coming from the left out of the low sun, a freight engine pulling a line of coal cars south toward the Aberdeen yard, and Albert saw it and his foot went to the brake and the brake did not do anything. The tires locked. The car did not slow. The car slid forward on the frozen glaze the way a spoon slides across a porcelain plate, slowly and without sound, and the train came across the crossing faster than the car was sliding, and the train was not going to stop, and the car was not going to stop either.

"*Alice.*"

"Daddy —"

The car slid into the side of the train.

The sound was the sound of steel hitting steel and of glass breaking in a single long white explosion and of the car folding in on itself like a tin can under a boot, and the world spun once and then stopped. Alice's head hit the dashboard. The light went white and then dark.

Silence.

Albert opened his eyes. His hands were still on the wheel. His hands would not stop shaking. He turned his head.

Alice was slumped in the seat beside him, covered in small cubes of windshield glass, a gash across her forehead bleeding freely into her eyebrows and down the left side of her face onto the collar of her coat. Her eyes were closed.

"*Alice.*"

Her eyes opened.

"Daddy."

"Can you move."

"I can move."

The train moved on. The coal cars clanked past the wreckage one by one, and the smell of the engine was the smell of coal smoke and of hot metal, and the wind across the flat field beyond the crossing was the same wind that had been blowing for six months and was going to blow for another eighteen. Albert got the driver's side door open — the door was bent but not crumpled — and helped Alice out through his side because her side of the car was pressed against the train. They stood on the frozen gravel beside the wreckage and Alice's face was half-covered in blood and Albert was shaking from the shoulders down, and there was nothing in the world on the horizon for as far as either of them could see except the flat grey fields and the low sun and the small black shape of the train disappearing south.

Albert put his arm around his daughter's shoulders.

They walked.

It was four miles to Eureka and the road was glazed and the wind was cold and Alice's blood dried on her forehead in the cold and cracked when she moved her eyebrows. They did not speak for most of the walk. Albert was holding Alice's shoulders and Alice was holding the side of her head where the gash was, and both of them were walking inside the small stunned silence that comes after the body has decided it is not going to die and has not yet decided what to do about the fact that it almost did.

They reached the edge of town at dusk.

---

Eva was in the kitchen when the door at the bottom of the wooden stairs opened.

She had been watching the clock since four. The doctor's appointment had been at two and the drive home was an hour and Albert and Alice should have been home by three-thirty, and it was nearly six now, and the wind outside the window over the sink had been picking up all afternoon, and Eva had been standing at the stove with her hand on the cast-iron rim, gripping it, the way she had been gripping it all her life when she was afraid of something and could not say so.

She heard the door open.

She heard the boots on the stairs — two sets, slow, one of them dragging slightly.

She heard Albert's voice, low: "Careful, Alice. One at a time."

Eva turned away from the stove and faced the kitchen door, and a moment later Albert came through with Alice under his arm, and Alice's face was a long red mask of dried blood from the hairline to the collar of her coat, and her dark hair was matted with it, and her eyes — her father's blue eyes — were open and were looking at her mother across the kitchen with the small brave exhausted expression of a fourteen-year-old who has walked four miles in the cold through a dusk and does not yet know she is still in shock.

Eva did not move for a half-second.

Then she crossed the kitchen in three steps and took her daughter from her husband's arm and lowered Alice into a chair at the table and put both her hands on Alice's face and tilted the face up into the lamplight and looked at the gash, and the gash was long and shallow across the left side of the forehead above the eyebrow, and the blood had clotted hours ago, and Alice was not in immediate danger of anything except the small secondary danger of a girl whose scarlet-fever heart had just been through a car accident and a four-mile walk in the cold.

Eva said: "Alice. Can you tell me your name."

"Alice Estelle Isaak."

"Can you tell me the day of the week."

"Saturday."

"Can you tell me what year it is."

"Nineteen thirty-five, Mama."

"Good girl."

Eva stood up. She went to the stove and put a kettle on for warm water and brought a clean cloth from the drawer and sat down beside Alice at the table and began to wash the blood off her daughter's face. She did it slowly. The cloth went pink and then red and then brown, and she rinsed it in the basin and wrung it out and wiped again, and Alice sat very still with her eyes closed and her hands folded in her lap and let her mother work.

Albert was still standing in the kitchen doorway with his coat on.

He was looking at the window over the sink. The glass was brown with dust from the afternoon storm. Outside, the street was empty and the wind was still blowing. He had not taken his hands out of his pockets and his hands were still shaking inside the pockets and he did not know how to make them stop.

Eva finished cleaning Alice's face. The gash was clean now. It was going to need stitches, and Eva knew the doctor would not come out in this weather and would not be needed in this weather because Eva had done this before — had stitched the small wounds on the girls before, with the small curved needle from the sewing basket and the silk thread Tante Frieda had left behind in the sewing tin — and she was going to do it tonight. She was going to do it in the lamplight at the kitchen table with Alice's head on a folded towel and Alice's hand in Edith's hand, because Edith was seven now and Edith was going to be in the kitchen holding her sister's hand whether anyone asked her to or not.

Eva looked up at Albert in the doorway.

"Albert."

"Yes."

"Come sit down."

He came and sat down. Eva put her hand on his hand on the table. His hand was still shaking. She held it until it stopped, and then she let go and went to get the sewing basket, and the kitchen was very quiet, and the wind outside the window over the sink was the sound the wind had been making for six months, and the apartment above the store in Eureka, South Dakota, in the winter of 1935 was the apartment the Isaaks had lived in for fifteen years, and the apartment was not going to be the apartment the Isaaks lived in for very much longer.

Albert did not say anything yet. But he was going to. The decision was already made, somewhere in the back of his head behind the shaking of his hands, and he was going to say it in a few days when the stitches were out and Alice was upstairs in bed and the wind had died down, and what he was going to say was going to change the lives of every person in the room.

For tonight, Eva stitched Alice's forehead in the lamplight at the kitchen table.

Edith held her sister's hand.

And the dust on the window over the sink was the color of weak coffee in the last of the evening light.
