## Chapter Two

# Frozen Ghosts

Duchy of Warsaw, December 1812

---

He had turned sixteen in August. Johann had turned fourteen two days later. Mother had baked a single Streuselkuchen with two candles in it, as she had every August for as long as Karl could remember, and Ernst had lunged across the table to blow out the candles before either of his older brothers could and had to be stopped, laughing, by everyone at once. The harvest had come in. The army had not come back. People had begun to say, cautiously, that perhaps it would not.

Now it was the second week of December, and the Kachelofen was roaring with split birch, and the whole Stube was warm. The cattle were wintering inside the byre on the far side of the wall, and their breath warmed the wall and the wall warmed the room. The pantry was full. November had been slaughter month — a smoked ham hung in the chimney, and the kitchen smelled of smoke and salt and good fat. Mother stood at the hearth rendering goose fat into Schmalz in a black iron pan, the white fat clarifying slowly to gold. Dorothea was at her elbow stirring. Ernst, almost three, sat on the floor banging a wooden spoon against an iron pot and laughing at the noise. Tasso slept against the warm tiles of the stove. Hexe lay flat on top of the stove like a thing she owned.

Outside, Karl was breaking the ice on the cattle trough with the back of an axe. His breath steamed; his fingers were dead inside his wool mittens. The sky was a flat grey-white that did not look like sky any more, only like ceiling. He chopped through and the cows pushed in. He hauled an armful of split birch from the woodpile to the basket by the door. The wind was from the east. The wind was always from the east in December. The wind brought a faint sound on it that he had been not-hearing for an hour.

He stopped on the path and listened.

---

It was the road again — not as it had been in June. There was no surf-roar this time. This was thinner: a dry rasping shuffling, with sometimes a single voice in it, sometimes a single high sound that was perhaps a horse, sometimes nothing at all. He went up to the rise. His feet broke through the crust at every step. He got to the top, and he had to put his hand on the gatepost to keep his knees.

They were not an army. They had not been an army in some time. They were a procession of dying men, with no formations, no banners, no officers any one could tell from the men. Their faces were wrapped in things that had not been made to be wrapped on faces — looted shawls, women's skirts torn into strips, strips of cured horse-hide. Their feet were wrapped likewise. Some had paper-stuffed boots that had once been blue leather and were now a cracked grey. Some were barefoot in the snow, and the footprints they left were not white but black, and the black was the colour of frostbite where the skin had gone past saving.

One man cradled an icon in his arms the way a man cradles a sleeping child.

As Karl watched, a man at the front of the section he could see fell down in the road. He fell forward, slowly, knees first, then hips, then face. He did not put his hands out. The men behind him stepped over him. Not around him — over him. None of them looked down. The snow began at once to drift up the side of him.

Twenty paces further on, a horse staggered out of the column and went down. Three men were on it before it had stopped kicking, kneeling around it in the road with small knives in their hands. For a moment Karl thought they were trying to help it — cutting the harness, perhaps, or putting it out of its misery. Then he understood that they were not. They were cutting strips of flesh from the haunch, fast, working with the practiced quickness of men who had butchered an animal many times before. The horse was still alive. One of them, with his knife in his teeth, said something to it in French — almost tenderly, the way one speaks to a hurt child — and the horse made a low sound, and the men kept cutting.

Karl had to look away. He stood with his hand on the gatepost and his eyes on the snow at his feet, and his breath was very loud in his ears. He would not be sick. He would not be sick. He waited a long moment for it to pass. The men were not cruel, he thought — he kept thinking it, almost helplessly — they were only hungry. They were only hungry. He kept saying it to himself.

Behind him, footsteps came up the path. He did not turn; he knew the step.

Johann came and stood beside him. Johann was fourteen now. He did not speak. He put both his hands deep in the pockets of his coat and watched, and his face was very pale, and his face was very still, and his face after a while was the face of a different boy than the one who had thrown grain at the rooster six months ago. They watched together.

After a while Karl said: “Go find Father. Tell him.”

Johann ran.

Within ten minutes Father and Michael were at the gate with axes — not raised, but carried at thigh-height, where they could be seen. A small group of perhaps six men had broken from the column and was stumbling up the path through the snow toward the gate. They had decided, by some unspoken signal among themselves, that the small thread of smoke from the chimney of a Lutheran farmhouse was their only chance.

They stopped, when they saw the axes, ten paces from the gate. The man at the front put both hands up, palms out. He had been crying, and the tears had frozen on his cheeks in a small white rime.

“Bitte,” he said. The German was bad. “Bitte. Brot. Wasser.”

Behind Karl the kitchen door opened. Mother came out in her shawl with a great brown loaf in one arm and an iron kettle of hot flour-water steaming in the other. She walked past her husband and her sons and the axes, out of the gate, into the snow, alone. She set the kettle in the snow at the men's feet. She broke the loaf in her hands — broke it in halves and quarters, fast, the way she broke bread at her own table — and gave a piece into each pair of hands. She did not give it to the strongest first. She gave it to the man who looked least likely to stand up. She moved among them the way she moved among her own children with bread.

There was a man at the back of the small group, older than the others, the only one wearing the rags of the blue. He had no hat. The end of his nose was a black knob. His right hand he held tucked into his coat against his chest as if it hurt to look at it. He took the bread from Mother in his left hand and chewed it very slowly — the way an animal that has only just remembered being a man chews.

He looked up. His eyes met Karl's, and lingered, and did not move on. Karl knew him then — the grey hair, the lined sun-darkened face under it, the missing teeth. It was the same man who had stood at the spring on the rise in June with a tin canteen on his hip and said Russland ist groß. The man, looking back at him now, did not know him. The man was looking at him the way a man looks at a wall.

Karl took a tin cup of the hot flour-water from his mother's kettle and brought it across. The man tried to take it with his left hand and could not; his hand shook too much. Karl held the cup to his mouth. The man drank. Some of the water ran down his chin and froze at once on his coat.

“Danke,” the man whispered. “Danke, Junge.”

Karl said, very quietly, “Was ist passiert?”

The man did not answer for a long time. He was looking at Karl's face and seeing something that was not Karl's face. After a long while he said one word.

“Beresina.”

Karl had never heard the word. The sergeant said it the way a man says a name. A river, Karl thought, looking at his face. A river, and every man he had marched east with in June. The sergeant would not say the word again to anyone.

“Alle tot,” the sergeant said. “Alle tot. Alle.”

Then he looked past Karl, and saw Mother, who had come up beside him with the kettle in her hand and was watching him. He looked at her white linen cap, and at her face under it, and Mother — forty-nine years old, with the strong calm face of a woman who had run a household and brought children up through fevers and bad winters — looked back at him as if he were one of them.

Something gave in his face.

“Meine Mutter,” he said. “Meine Mutter —”

And could not finish.

Mother set the kettle down in the snow. She lifted her hand — her bare red kitchen hand — and laid the back of it against the sergeant's frostbitten cheek, very gently, the way she might lay it against the cheek of one of her own when he had been sick all night.

“Geh nach Hause, mein Sohn,” she said. “Geh nach Hause.”

The sergeant wept. He wept without sound, the tears going down his cheeks and freezing in his stubble. He turned his face into Mother's hand and wept. After a moment, with the bread in his fist, he turned and stumbled back down the path toward the road. The other men went with him. They did not say goodbye. Within fifteen minutes they had been re-absorbed into the road, within an hour they were out of sight, and within a week, Karl knew without needing to be told, they would be dead in the snow somewhere west of here.

That night the family sat around the Kachelofen and did not speak for a long time. Ernst was asleep in his cradle. Dorothea had fallen asleep against Mother's side with her cheek on her mother's apron. Christian was on the floor with his back against Tasso and his hand idly in the dog's ruff. Johann sat closer to Karl on the bench than he usually sat; Karl could feel his brother's shoulder against his own.

Father, looking into the fire under the iron grate, said quietly: “This land will not stay quiet. The French will fall. The Russians will come. And then someone else. We are in the road.”

Mother said: “Where would we go?”

He did not answer.

Karl glanced sideways at his brother. Johann was staring into the fire, and there was something in his face that Karl had not seen there before — not fear, not grief, but a kind of wakefulness. Some small thing in him had opened tonight, looking at the men in the road, that would not close again.
