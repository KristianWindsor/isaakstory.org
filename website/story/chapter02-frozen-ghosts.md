## Chapter Two

# Frozen Ghosts

Pabianice, Duchy of Warsaw — January–February 1813

---

The first thing that came down the road was not a man.

It was a horse.

It was the third week of January. The smell had been on the wind for two days, and Karl had to know. He and Johann walked across the yard and along the cart-track in the early morning dark, dressed in everything they owned — two shirts, coats, scarves to the eyes — and it made no difference. The air was not air; it was a weight, a biting thing that froze the inside of the nose. The snow didn't crunch — it squeaked like grinding glass, packed so hard it took no footprints. They climbed the rise at the edge of the rye field and stood behind the hedge and waited.

The horse came from the east at a slow walk, alone, down the middle of the ice-rutted road, the bridle dragging behind it. A cavalry horse — a bay with good lines, the kind of animal Karl had watched the French officers ride past in June and privately admired. Now its coat was rimed white with frost. No rider. No saddle. Someone had opened it along the haunch: a long ragged cut, not clean work — sawed, back and forth, by something inadequate for the purpose. The wound was dark against the grey morning. The blood was freezing into black icicles down the animal's leg before it could reach the ground. The horse was still walking. Still bleeding, in the slow way of things that have lost so much blood the body has nearly stopped noticing. Its breath came in weak, ragged clouds that vanished into ice dust.

It passed below them and went around the bend toward Lask.

Johann did not speak. Karl did not speak. They stood at the hedge a long time after it was gone, looking at the empty road.

Karl began the Vater unser inside his coat, the way a hand reaches for a tool in the dark. He got as far as the daily bread and stopped.

They knew, more or less, what the horse meant. Word had come up from town at Christmas: the Emperor had gone back through Warsaw in the middle of December — in a plain sleigh, wrapped in furs, under a false name, running for Paris. And behind him, nothing. The army that had taken all summer to go east past the rye field was lost somewhere in the snow. No one at the market had quite believed it.

The horse was the first proof.

Karl went up to the rise every day after that, before the younger children woke and again at dusk, after the well ice was broken and the byre was done. Johann had come once and would not come again, and Karl did not blame him. The road was empty for an hour, for half a morning — long enough to believe it was finished — and then it was not. Two officers came by on one horse. The horse went down in front of the gate, slowly, folding at the knees first, and one of them shot it in the head, and they walked on. By nightfall it was stripped to bone — Karl watched from the loft as strangers came with bayonets and bare hands and ate some of it raw, their faces black with frozen blood to the eyes.

Karl told no one. His mother was keeping the younger children from as much as she could, and his job was to carry what he saw and not add to her weight. He had begun to understand that there are things a man carries alone.

---

The boy at the door was the size of Christian.

It was a baking day. The oven had been fired since before light, and the smell of bread must have carried to the road — that, or the smoke. Something had pulled him off the road and up the track to their door.

He was perhaps fourteen. He wore a French drummer's coat that reached his knees, so faded and fouled it was barely a color anymore, and there was nothing under it that Mother could see. His feet were wrapped in strips of blanket frozen solid around them. His face was the color of tallow — not a cold child's pale, but the waxy yellow of a candle.

He could not speak any language they knew. Karl — who had German and Polish and a little French from listening to soldiers — could not find a language in his sounds. Maybe sound was simply what remained.

Mother opened the door wider and brought him in before Father could stop her, and Father did not try very hard; one look at the boy and trying was breath wasted.

She sent Karl for warm water, and then did not send him away, so he knelt on the floor beside her and held the basin.

The rags did not unwind. They had frozen to the flesh, and where she worked them loose they came away with a dry tearing sound, like bark stripped off a dead branch. What was under them were not toes anymore. They were black — not bruise-black but the flat dead black of charred wood — shriveled and hard, the nails lifted and loose like fish scales. Where the black ended and the living foot began there was a line, red and angry and weeping. And there was a smell, sweetish and wrong: rot, in the middle of winter, in a room where everything else was frozen.

Mother eased the last wrap off the left foot and three of the toes came with it. They let go at the joints, with a small sound like a knuckle cracking, and there was almost no blood — a bead, two, no more — and one of them dropped into the basin between Karl's hands and turned over in the water, slowly, showing itself.

The boy watched it with mild interest. He could not feel it. That was the part Karl could never make anyone understand, after — the part that was worse than blood. The boy watched his own toes come away, and his face did not change.

Mother did not change either. She lifted the toe out of the basin and laid it on the rag with the others, as if this were a step in a task she had done before, and went on washing what was left.

The boy looked at the fire the whole time, with the fixed attention of a creature that has reduced its needs to one thing. Dorothea came and sat next to him on the bench, put her shawl over his shoulders — the good wool one — and sat with him while he looked at it.

He died in the night, on the bench by the stove, under Dorothea's shawl — the warmth he had followed for however many miles reaching him too late to do what warmth is supposed to do. He did not wake. He simply stopped.

In the morning Father and Michael carried him to the woodshed, and Mother stood at the threshold and said the Vater unser over him, so quietly Karl could barely make out the words — quiet because the younger children were inside. Karl heard. Johann heard.

Karl would hear it for the rest of his life — not the words, which were the ordinary words, but the specific sound of his mother's voice saying them in the cold, on a January morning, over a boy she had never met.

The boy was the first body in the woodshed. He was not the last.

---

The Polish steward came at the end of January.

They saw them from a long way off — three dark shapes on the white track, and the middle one walked like the steward.

"Cellar," Mother said.

"No," Michael said. He stood in the middle of the Stube with his chin up. "It's the steward. I've known him my whole life. I'm not going under the floor of my own house like a thief."

"He has two men with him," Father said. "He has never needed two men to talk."

"Then let him see me. Let him —"

"Michael." Father did not raise his voice. "The army is dead, and someone will be sent to clean up after it. It will not be my son. Down."

Michael went down with his face like stone, into the hole under the floor where the potatoes wintered — too low to stand in, smelling like the inside of the ground — and Mother pulled the rug over the boards and stood her loom on it and put Dorothea on the bench in front of the loom.

Dorothea was eight. She had been awake in the night when they carried the boy to the woodshed. She folded her hands in her lap and looked at the wall.

Father met the steward at the gate and brought him in out of the cold — you did not leave a man you had known a dozen years standing in that wind, whatever he had come for. A dozen years: markets, one change of government, winter pipes at this table. The steward was exhausted and ashamed, and he read the order because he had to.

A sled. Two horses. A man to drive them to Warsaw, for the army's hospitals. There would be a receipt.

He put the order down and told the rest plainly, looking at the table. Warsaw was drowning. The hospitals were full, the courtyards were full, the streets were full of men who had arrived dying and were finishing it in the road. Typhus was in everything. The teams that went in were kept — for the next load, and the next — and the men who drove them were kept with the teams, and after that what came back was word, or nothing. He had taken four teams off this road already.

The man who drove would go into that. Michael was eighteen. Father was fifty-four.

Father said his eldest was gone — three weeks now, north to a cousin in Cremzow, across the Prussian border, before any of this. He said it without blinking. He said it standing two paces from the rag rug, looking at a man who knew he had never told a lie in his life.

The steward looked at Father for a long moment.

Then he wrote something small in the margin of the order and said the office would find its own driver. The horses would come regardless. At the door he stopped and said: tell your son in Cremzow that the office will be counting heads when the thaw comes.

The two men harnessed the team in the byre. Father did not help them and did not stop them. Karl stood in the doorway and watched the sled go out of the gate behind the horses Michael had walked into the forest in June. There had been no forest this time. Foragers passed through; the steward knew the farm, knew the byre, knew the count. On the table he left a paper: Polish words, a number, a signature. No one picked it up.

When Michael came up out of the cellar an hour later, blinking, with dirt in his hair, he stood in the middle of the Stube and could not look at his father. He had heard every word through the floorboards. He had heard his father lie for him — this man who had not lied, who had bent but not broken through the French and the taxes and the foraging parties — and he stood in the warm room and the weight of it was visible on him, the shame of being the thing your father has staked his good name on.

Father said nothing. He went back outside.

---

The door came in four nights later.

Not a knock. The door itself — frame and all, kicked in by men who were past knocking, who had reached the far side of hunger where ordinary behavior becomes a kind of luxury that can no longer be afforded. Six of them, perhaps seven — Karl was never sure, because they were never all in his sight at once. The warmth of the Stube was sucked out in a single breath. The freezing wind poured through the shattered frame, instantly chilling the sweat on Karl's neck.

Deserters, Karl thought — from some unit that had broken somewhere east of Smolensk, men who had been on the road longer than the others and had survived by becoming something the road required of them. There was blood on two of them that was not their own.

Father stood up from the bench with his hands open at his sides.

Tasso went for the throat of the first man through the door.

He had been a large dog his whole life, enormous as a puppy, grown into a size that made strangers cautious, and he crossed the Stube in two strides and went for the throat without hesitation, without calculation, because that was what Tasso was.

The man killed him with a bayonet. It was fast. Tasso made a sound — one sound — and then Tasso was on the floor of the Stube and the man stepped over him and came further into the room.

The men took everything. The salt pork, the bread, the children's milk, the iron pot from above the stove, the smoked goose they found by smell in the chimney. One of them found Ernst asleep in the corner and pulled the wool blanket off him, and Ernst woke and screamed, and the man raised his hand, and Mother stepped between them. She did not say anything. She simply put herself there. The man stopped — not from mercy, but because she was in his way and he was too tired to push past a woman who had decided not to move.

They slept on the floor. All of them, around the stove, their weapons under their hands.

Karl climbed to the loft. He lay on his stomach and looked down through the gap in the boards at the men sleeping below, and he counted their breathing — the uneven breathing of men past exhaustion — and he counted the knives on their belts. Hexe came up into the loft without a sound and pressed herself against his side, and he let her. Somewhere under the counting the Vater unser was running, not because he had decided to pray but because the words went by themselves, like water moving under ice. And forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us. He watched the man who had killed Tasso, and he did not forgive him, and he did not stop praying. He lay in the loft for six hours and he thought, at some point in those six hours, that he could get down the ladder and across the room in the dark and cut the man's throat with the kitchen knife before the others woke.

He was fifteen years old. He was thinking clearly about killing a man and he knew it and the knowing did not make him feel young. He would never feel entirely young again after that night. He lay in the loft and watched and counted until the counting became familiar and the familiar became manageable, and somewhere in that process he slept without meaning to, and in the morning the men were gone.

The seed grain under the byre floor was safe. The candlesticks and the locket were safe behind the chimney stone. The iron pot was gone, and the food, and Tasso was on the floor of the Stube.

Christian buried him alone. The ground was iron and Christian was twelve and he had Tasso's paws wrapped in his old linen strips still from when the cold had cracked them, and it took him most of the day, pick and mattock in ground that did not want to open. Karl watched from the barn doorway once and then stopped watching, because there are things that belong to a person and this one belonged to Christian.

He came in at dusk with his hands bleeding from the pick handle. He ate his bread. He went to bed.

That night, through the wall, Karl heard his father weep for the first time in his life. Briefly — a few minutes — and then the sound stopped, and the house was quiet except for the wind and the road.

---

Three days after the door, a man came to the gate alone, on foot — thirty, maybe, starved down to wire, in a soldier's coat that was just a coat now. When Mother spoke to him in the German of the household — the rounded vowels of Brandenburg — he stopped as if struck, and answered in the same speech. He had not heard it in eight months.

He sat at the Isaak table and ate what was put in front of him. He was a colonist himself, from a village two days west. The army had taken him with his wagon in the spring — a driver, not a soldier. He had been at the Berezina.

Michael did not take his eyes off him.

Father asked him about it. Once, quietly.

The man looked up from his bowl and said he would tell them about the noise, and then he would not speak of it again. He spoke for perhaps three minutes. He described sound only — not the river, not what was in the river or what happened at the banks or how many or any of it. He described the sound of the bridge collapsing under the weight of the men on it, and the sound of the men in the water, which he said was a sound he had not known human beings could make, and which went on for two hours, and stopped not because the men were saved but because they froze. Then he stopped, and did not speak of it again, and no one asked him to.

In the morning Mother filled his pockets with what could be spared, and he walked west, toward home, and the road took him around the bend the way it took everything.

---

In the first week of February the road filled again.

Karl woke before dawn to sound — thin and far off, carried the way sound carries over snow in hard frost. Nothing like June, when the road had come up through the ground itself. Smaller. But coming. Father and Christian took the cow and the hens into the forest before first light. Karl went up to the rise, and Johann came with him, which Karl had not asked for and did not remark on.

There was no dust this time, and no music. The column came out of the east in the grey light with almost no sound — runners and axles, the blowing of horses, boots by the thousand squeaking on the packed snow — and went past the foot of the rise all morning, going west. Not stragglers this time — an army still shaped like an army, what was left of one, pulling itself out of Poland. Wagons with the canvas gone, heaped with men instead. Guns crusted white with frost, behind teams of four where there had been six. And walking, between everything and behind everything, the infantry: files of men wrapped to the eyes in whatever would wrap, heads down, muskets slung, keeping order the way exhausted men keep order — by being unable to imagine anything else.

They were speaking German. Not threaded through the column, the way it had been in June. All of it — low and constant, a whole river of it. Somewhere a driver roared "Vorwärts! Vorwärts!" at a team that had nothing left: the same word, on the same road, going the other way.

"Some of them talk like us," Johann said. He had said it in June, on this rise, as a discovery. He said it now the way a man says that water runs downhill.

"Where are they going?" he said, after a while.

"Home."

"Will they get there?"

Karl watched a wagon go by, heaped with men who did not move. "Some of them."

It went on until dark, began again before light, and by the second evening had thinned to the limpers and the led horses. Then the road was empty — not empty the way a road is empty, but the way a table is cleared between one sitting and the next.

Karl came down off the rise in the blue dusk, shut up the byre, and went in. That night, on the bench by the big stove with Johann's back warm against his, he listened the way he had learned to listen in June — through the bench, through the boards, through the packed earth — for the feel of an army in the ground.

There was nothing at all.
