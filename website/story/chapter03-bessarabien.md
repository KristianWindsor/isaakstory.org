## Chapter Three

# Bessarabien

Duchy of Warsaw, 1814

---

The Russians came through, and then the Prussians. A typhus winter took Pastor Hartmann's wife. Two harvests came in, both thin. Word reached the village at Easter that Napoleon had fallen, that he had been sent to an island called Elba, that a king had been put back on the French throne. The Duchy of Warsaw was collapsing, and no one in the village yet knew what they would be.

Karl was seventeen. Johann was fifteen. Michael was twenty. Father's hair had gone grey at the temples in two winters.

On Sunday morning the household put on its Sunday clothes. Mother fastened her white linen cap, took the Gesangbuch under her arm, and they walked up the dirt road to the colony's small wooden Lutheran church together — the whole German colony converging on the low tower from across the fields. The bell was already ringing as the Isaaks came over the rise. Pastor Hartmann — grey now, widowed — stood at the door with his hands folded, naming each of them as they came past.

Inside, the men sat on the right and the women on the left in the old way. The wooden benches were dark with sixty years of use. They sang Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott in four parts, the women's voices riding the men's underneath, no organ, only the precentor with his pitch-pipe and the congregation. Karl, between Father and Michael on the men's side, opened his mouth and sang and felt the hymn lift out of him.

---

After the service the colony spilled out into the spring sun and broke into knots in the churchyard. The men went to the wall under the linden. The women clustered around the church door. The young people drifted to the edges, and at the far end, in the dirt by the wall, the younger children had begun some game with sticks — a mock duel by the look of it, with Tasso (who had come along, as he sometimes did) chasing the thrown sticks and refusing to bring them back. Christian and Dorothea were among them. Ernst, four now, toddled between the bigger children, and one of them caught him neatly by the back of his coat each time he wandered toward the road.

The Leischners came out behind the Isaaks, the way they always did, and made for them across the trodden grass.

They came in their Sunday order. Mr. Leischner, square-shouldered, with the short grey beard and the deep careful voice he had always had. Katherina at his elbow, thin and sharp-eyed, who said something to Mother that Karl did not catch and got back one of Mother's rare returning laughs. Rosina, seventeen, Karl's age, tall and slim with thick chestnut braids; she said good morning to Karl from under her lashes in the way she had, and walked past him toward Johann.

And, last, Wilhelm. Thirteen, lit up at the sight of Karl, almost running across the grass.

“Karl. Karl. Come and see.”

Wilhelm caught Karl's sleeve and pulled. Karl let himself be pulled. From the pocket of his Sunday coat Wilhelm produced a small grey-brown stone, chipped on one edge to something that might have been an arrow point. He had found it on Wednesday in the south field, he said, and was almost certain it was old. Karl turned it gravely in his hand and asked the right questions in the right places, and Wilhelm, under his attention, went very slightly still.

Karl handed the stone back. Over Wilhelm's shoulder, against the church wall, he could see Father and Mr. Leischner standing a little apart from everyone else, talking low. Mother and Katherina had gone to the church door together, arm in arm. Katherina was laughing at something Mother had said, but the laugh was softer than her usual laugh.

He looked back at Wilhelm. He looked, past Wilhelm, at Johann — Johann leaning against the churchyard wall with his hands in his pockets, watching the younger children play and pretending not to. Rosina had drifted over to him. She was saying something to him about the sermon. Johann answered without quite looking at her, and Rosina did not move on. She lingered. Johann did not notice he was being lingered at.

Karl watched it for the time it took to draw a breath. He felt his mouth twitch into the first smile of the chapter. He said nothing, and he filed it away.

---

He drifted closer to the church wall, with Wilhelm at his elbow turning the stone in his hand, and caught the end of it.

Mr. Leischner was telling Father something quietly, slowly, as if the words had been in his mouth a long time.

“We are going east, Michel. Katherina and I have decided. The whole house. To Bessarabia.”

Father said nothing for a moment. Then he said, “When?”

“After the harvest. Late summer.”

Mr. Leischner went on, in pieces. The Russian Tsar Alexander had issued a Manifest — free land for German colonists in his new southern provinces, on the Black Sea. Tax exemption. Freedom from military service. Freedom of religion: they could build their own Lutheran churches and call their own pastors. A cousin of Mr. Leischner's had gone last summer, to a place called Kulm, named for the old Kulm in West Prussia. The cousin had written. The land was good, the cousin said. The wheat grew tall. There was no Napoleon there, and there would be no French sergeant on the road.

Father asked two practical questions only.

“How far?”

“Hundreds of miles.”

“All of you?”

“All of us.”

The two men stood together against the church wall. They did not speak for a long moment. They had stood together against this wall every Sunday for nearly twenty years.

Karl looked across the churchyard at Johann. Johann was no longer leaning casually against the wall. Johann was listening; he had heard. His face had the same fixed, awake, hungry look Karl had seen on it the night the sergeant came back — the look of a fifteen-year-old who had been waiting for something like this since he was thirteen, and who had at last been given its name. Bessarabia. Wagons. East. Rosina was still standing beside him, talking. Johann was no longer answering.

Karl looked at the boy beside him. Wilhelm had stopped turning the stone. He had heard. He was staring at the ground, and the lit-up thirteen-year-old who had run across the grass five minutes ago to show Karl the stone was gone. In his place was a small boy who had just learned, here in his own churchyard, from his own father, that he was leaving.

Karl put his hand on Wilhelm's shoulder. Just for a moment. He did not say anything. Wilhelm did not look up, but Karl felt the small body lean, very slightly, into his hand.

Across the churchyard, by the door, Mother had reached Katherina. They were standing close together with their heads almost touching. Mother's hand had found hers. Mother said, very quietly, only the one word: “Käthe.”

Katherina's eyes were wet but she was not weeping. She put her free hand over Mother's, so that her two hands held Mother's one.

“I know,” she said. “I know, Lieschen. I know.”

They had been each other's company at every birth and every death for nearly twenty years. They stood that way — their hands stacked together at the church door — and did not say anything else for a while.

---

The Isaaks walked home together across the fields in the late Sunday morning. The light was high and clean. Mother walked ahead with Father, Ernst on her hip with his small head against her neck. Michael walked with Christian. Dorothea was between them. Tasso had come out across the strip of common pasture and was trotting beside Michael's heel. Above them, slow and high and on the home wing, a stork glided over toward the chimney.

Karl and Johann fell behind. By twenty paces, then thirty. No one called them. Karl thought perhaps Mother had even slowed her pace a little to let them. He could not be sure.

They walked in silence for a long while. Karl knew what his brother was going to say before he said it.

Johann said it, finally, without looking at him. He said it to the road.

“Karl. We could go too.”

Karl did not answer at once. He looked at his father's back ahead of him — the slope of the shoulders, the steady way the man walked. He looked at Mother carrying Ernst. He thought of Mr. Leischner's careful voice against the church wall: We are going east, Michel. He thought of Wilhelm leaning into his hand.

He thought of the sergeant on the road in summer, with the canteen on his hip: Russland ist groß, Junge.

He thought of the sergeant on the road in winter, with the bread in his shaking left hand: Beresina. Alle tot. Alle.

In his pocket, his fingers closed around the French sou. He had been carrying it for two years now, and his thumb had worn Napoleon's profile almost smooth. The Roman wreath was still there, faintly. The man's nose was almost gone.

He looked at his brother.

“Maybe we will,” he said.

Johann nodded once. He did not say anything else. The brothers walked on. Behind them, the rye was green and not yet tall.

Ahead of them, their mother turned and called them home, and they came.
