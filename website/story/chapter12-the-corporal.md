## Chapter Twelve

# The Corporal

Eureka, South Dakota and the Western Front — 1917 to 1918

---

The night before Albert and Ed went to register for the draft, Hanna burned the bread.

She had not burned bread in twenty years. She had not burned bread since the year Anna died. She was at the stove with her back to her two grown sons, and the loaves were in the oven, and she had been standing at the stove for so long without moving that the smell of the baking had passed through done and into the next thing, and Albert at the kitchen table noticed it before she did.

"*Mutter.*"

"What."

"The bread."

Hanna opened the oven. She took the loaves out without a cloth and burned the side of her hand on the iron, and she did not say anything about it, and she did not put the loaves down. She stood at the open oven with the burned bread in her hands and her back still turned, and Albert and Ed sat at the table and looked at her back, and upstairs the floorboards creaked the small slow creak of Josie moving around the bedroom where their father had been spending his days for the last three months.

"*Mutter*," Ed said. "Put the bread down."

She put the bread down. She turned around. Her face had gone the color her face went when she was about to cry and was not going to let herself, and Albert understood — because he had been her son for twenty-nine years and he knew her face the way a man knows the face of the woman who raised him — that the bread was not the bread. The bread was every morning between now and the morning the army took him, and tomorrow morning was the first one.

"I do not want my boys to go," she said.

It was the first time anyone in the house had said it out loud. The newspapers had been talking for two months about the draft and what it would do and how it would work, and the kitchen had not talked about it once, because the kitchen was a Lutheran kitchen and Lutheran kitchens did not talk about the things they were most afraid of. Hanna had been keeping the not-talking together by herself, and tonight the bread had broken her, and the words had come out before she could stop them.

Albert got up from the table and crossed the kitchen and put his arms around his mother. He had not done this since he was a small boy. Hanna stood very stiff inside his arms for a moment and then leaned her forehead against his shoulder, and the two of them stood there in the smell of burned bread, and Ed stayed at the table because Ed understood that the moment was for Albert and his mother and that there was no room in it for anyone else.

After a while Hanna lifted her head.

"I am going to scrape the bottoms of the loaves and we will eat them anyway."

"Yes, *Mutter.*"

"Sit down, Albert."

Albert sat down. Hanna scraped the burned bottoms off the bread with a knife and brought the loaves to the table, and the three of them ate the bread in silence, and upstairs Josie was reading to David from the Bible because reading aloud was the only thing that helped, and the kitchen was quiet, and tomorrow was tomorrow.

---

Hank came for them at six in the morning.

He came up the wooden stairs at the side of the building two at a time and rapped twice on the kitchen door and walked in without waiting, because Hank had been walking into his cousins' kitchen without waiting since he was eight years old. He was in his good brown coat. His tie was crooked. His round friendly face was pinker than usual from the cold.

"Up. Both of you. We're walking together."

Hank Isaak was Solomon's eldest son, twenty-five years old, the only registered pharmacist in McPherson County. Two years ago he had taken his Bachelor of Science in pharmacy from the North Dakota Agricultural College and had come home and bought the Rexall drug store on the main street, and he had been running it ever since. He played the cornet in the Eureka band on Sundays. He had been Albert's bandmate for nine years.

Hanna came in from the front room. She saw Hank and her face did the small fond thing it did when one of Solomon's boys appeared in her kitchen. She walked over and put both her hands on his face and kissed him on the forehead — a thing she had not done to him since he was about ten — and then she stepped back and went to the stove without saying anything else, and Hank cleared his throat and turned back to his cousins.

"Let's go."

---

The line at the post office was already long when the three of them got there.

It stretched out the door and down the steps and along the sidewalk for half a block, and the men in it were every man in McPherson County between twenty-one and thirty-one — the boys from the grain elevator, the schoolteachers, the new clerk from the bank, the Norwegian dairy boys, two of the Schmidt brothers from the bakery, the Bessarabian-German farmers in their church coats. Nobody was talking. The line moved slowly.

Albert was the tallest of the three cousins. Five foot eleven, broad in the shoulders, with the dark hair he had gotten from his father and the blue eyes he had gotten from his mother. Ed was an inch shorter. Hank was an inch shorter than Ed. The three of them stood in line together with their hats in their hands, and the morning sun came over the roof of the post office and warmed the back of Albert's coat, and the line moved forward, and Albert watched the back of the man in front of him and tried not to think about what was coming.

Hank went in first.

The Selective Service Board had set up the registration in the back room of the post office, and Mr. Smolinsky from the bank was acting as the registrar because Mr. Smolinsky had the best handwriting in the county. He was a small precise man in his fifties — Polish, second-generation — and he had a voice that was very quiet and very clear, and he asked the questions in the order they were printed on the card and he wrote down the answers in his own hand because the men in front of him had calluses from working farms and were not used to writing in small spaces. He asked Hank his name. He wrote it down. Date of birth. Address. Occupation. *Pharmacist*, Hank said, and Mr. Smolinsky looked up from the card.

"You are the man at the Rexall."

"Yes, sir."

"Dependents?"

"None."

"Grounds for exemption?"

"I am the only registered pharmacist in McPherson County. There is no other within four thousand square miles, and the population the drug store serves is about five thousand people, including the Norwegian settlements to the north and the Bessarabian colonies to the south."

Mr. Smolinsky wrote this down. He read it back to himself once. He set the card on the small stack of completed cards on his right and motioned for the next man.

Hank stood up and walked out.

Ed went in next. Mr. Smolinsky asked him the questions. Mother dependent. Father sickly and dependent. Buyer and general manager of the family department store. Could not easily be spared. Trouble with his eyes. Mr. Smolinsky wrote down each answer in his careful hand, and then, after Ed had finished, he wrote a small additional note in the margin of Ed's card and set the card on the pile and motioned for Albert.

Albert sat down. Mr. Smolinsky asked Albert the questions. Same answers. Mother dependent. Father sickly. Necessary to manage the family business. Mr. Smolinsky wrote down each answer in the same careful hand, and then he looked up from the card and looked at Albert for a moment over the small steel rims of his glasses.

"Mr. Isaak."

"Yes."

"You and your brother work in the same store."

"Yes, sir."

"And the same father is sick."

"Yes, sir."

Mr. Smolinsky did not say anything else. He looked back down at the card and wrote a small additional note in the margin in the same careful hand he had used for Ed's. He set the card on the pile and motioned for the next man, and Albert stood up and walked out.

He never asked what Mr. Smolinsky had written, and Mr. Smolinsky never volunteered it, and Albert went to France not knowing how the registrar had judged his case. The card was filed in Washington with a small line of three words in the margin in Mr. Smolinsky's careful hand, and the three words sat on the card through the war and after the war and for the rest of Mr. Smolinsky's life and for the rest of Albert's life, and neither of them ever spoke of it again.

Hank was waiting on the sidewalk when Albert came out. The three cousins walked back to the store together along the same four blocks they had walked an hour earlier, past the same Schmidt brothers' bakery with the same butcher-paper sign in the window — *PLEASE SPEAK ENGLISH IN THIS STORE* — and Hank told them, in his loud cheerful Hank voice, the long complicated story of a man who had come into the drug store the day before and asked for *something for the head*, and Albert and Ed listened, and by the time they reached the store Albert was almost laughing, which was exactly what Hank had intended.

The numbers were drawn six weeks later in Washington. Albert's came up. Ed's did not.

---

Two nights before he left for Camp Funston, Albert was packing his trunk in the small bedroom above the store, and he had to choose.

The clarinet case was on the bed. The violin case was on the chair by the window.

The clarinet was the band — Hank and Walter and Edwin and Otto on Sunday evenings in the church basement, the Saturday night socials and the harvest dances, the polkas the older Bessarabian Germans had brought across the ocean in 1878. The clarinet was the instrument Albert was *known for*. The clarinet belonged to Eureka, and Eureka was where it would stay.

The violin was the instrument he played alone. His father had given it to him on his twelfth birthday in 1899, and he had taken lessons from a traveling music teacher for two years, and he had been playing in the parlor on winter evenings ever since — by himself, badly, the way a man plays an instrument he loves but does not perform on. Sometimes he played for half an hour. Sometimes for an hour. He played the German hymns from church and the folk songs his mother had taught him as a child, and he had kept playing through his twenties even after he had decided he was a clarinet player in a band, because the violin in the parlor was a separate thing and he was the only one who knew about it.

He took the violin.

Hanna came up to his room after supper and stood in the doorway and saw the closed trunk and saw which case was on the chair. She did not say anything. She walked over and put her hand on the clarinet case for a moment, and then she went back downstairs.

The clarinet stayed on the chair for nineteen months.

---

Albert came down to the kitchen at half past five on the morning he left for Camp Funston, and his father was already at the table.

This was not what Albert had been expecting. David had not come downstairs for breakfast in three weeks. David spent his days in the bedroom upstairs because the climb back up was the kind of climb that left him in his chair gasping for an hour, and the doctor had told him in May that the stairs were not worth what they cost him, and David had agreed and had been keeping to the bedroom ever since. Albert had assumed he would go upstairs to say goodbye.

But David was at the table.

He was wearing his Sunday suit. He was wearing the dark wool jacket and the white collared shirt and the polka-dot tie that Hanna had bought him for Easter four years ago, and his hair had been combed into the neat side part he had worn his entire adult life, and his handlebar mustache had been waxed and turned up at the corners the way David's mustache had always been waxed and turned up at the corners, and he was sitting in his chair at the head of the table the way he had sat at every meal of Albert's life, and if you had not known what was wrong with him you would have looked at him at six in the morning in his suit and his tie at his own kitchen table and you would have thought he was a man on his way to a wedding.

Josie was beside him. She was wearing her work apron and her hair was up and she had the small careful expression of a daughter who had spent the last forty minutes helping her father put on a suit and was now standing beside him in case he needed her to help him stand up.

David looked up at Albert in the doorway.

"*Guten Morgen, mein Sohn.*"

Albert could not speak for a moment.

"*Guten Morgen, Vater.*"

"Come and sit. Your mother has coffee."

Albert came across the kitchen and sat down in the chair opposite his father. Hanna brought him a cup of coffee without saying anything and sat down at her own end of the table with a cup of her own. Ed was already at his place. The four of them sat at the table the way they had sat at this table thousands of times in Albert's life, and Albert understood — looking at his father in the suit, looking at the polka-dot tie, looking at the carefully waxed mustache — that David had put on the suit because David was not the kind of man who let his son leave for the army from a bedroom upstairs. David had come down because the table was where the family ate together, and the family was eating together this morning whether David's heart agreed to it or not, and David had decided that his heart would have to manage.

Hanna had made eggs and dark bread and coffee. The bread was the bread from yesterday — the loaves she had burned and scraped and brought to the table anyway. Nobody mentioned it. They ate. David ate slowly, with the small careful movements of a man who knew that any sudden gesture might cost him, and Albert watched his father eat and tried to fix the picture of his father at the table in his mind, the way a man fixes a picture he knows he will need later.

When the coffee was gone, David reached into his coat pocket.

He took out a small book.

It was a Bible. It was new — Albert could see that immediately, because the leather of the cover was unworn and the gilt on the page edges was bright and the brass clasp on the front had not yet been opened — and it was small, the size of a soldier's pocket, the kind of Bible a man could carry inside the breast pocket of an army coat. David laid it on the table in front of him and put his hand on top of it.

"This is for you. I sent for it from the publisher in St. Louis last month. It is the Lutheran translation, the same one we read at home." David's fingers were trembling slightly on the cover. He pressed them down to keep them still. "I had it sent because I wanted you to have your own Bible. Not the family Bible — that one is for the family, and your mother and I will keep it, and you will have it when the time comes. This one is yours. To take with you. To carry."

He pushed the book across the table.

Albert took it. The Bible was light in his hand — much lighter than the family Bible at home, much smaller — and the cover was soft and the brass clasp was cool and the whole thing fit easily into the palm of his hand. He looked at his father.

"*Vater* —"

"It is a small thing, Albert. I know it is a small thing. But I wanted you to have one of your own to carry with you, and I wanted to give it to you myself, and I wanted to give it to you here, at this table, where you have eaten with us your whole life." David's eyes were wet but he was not crying — David did not cry, David had not cried that any of his children had ever seen — and his hand was on top of his other hand on the table, and the trembling had not stopped. "Read it when you can. Read the Psalms when you cannot sleep. Your mother will tell you which ones."

"Yes, *Vater.*"

"Come back, Albert."

"I will."

Albert stood up. He walked around the table. He bent down and put his arms around his father — carefully, because David was thin under the suit and the suit was hanging looser than it had hung at Easter — and David lifted one hand and put it on the back of Albert's neck and held it there for a long moment, and then he let go.

Albert kissed the top of his father's head. He had not done that since he was six years old.

He took his coat from the hook by the door and put the Bible in the inside breast pocket where it sat against his heart, and he took his hat and looked back once at the kitchen — his father at the table in the suit and the polka-dot tie, his mother at her end of the table with her hands wrapped around her coffee cup, his sister beside his father with her hand on his shoulder — and he went out the door and down the wooden stairs into the grey light of dawn, where Ed and Hank were waiting in the street, and the three of them walked to the train station together.

---

Hanna helped David back up the stairs after Albert had gone. It took them nearly fifteen minutes. David sat in the chair by the bedroom window when they reached the top, and Hanna tucked a blanket around him and went downstairs to wash the breakfast dishes, and David was alone in the room for the first time in three days.

He looked out the window. The bedroom window faced south across the main street of Eureka. The morning light had come over the roof of the Lutheran church across the way. Somewhere down the street, out of sight, was the railway station where his oldest son was now loading a trunk onto a train.

David had known for a month.

He had not told Hanna because Hanna had known before he had. He had not told Albert because Albert had been about to get on a train and go to a war, and a son going to a war did not need to carry the knowledge of a dying father on top of everything else. The Bible had been the closest he could come to the thing he could not say. He had ordered it from the publisher in St. Louis the day after the doctor's last visit, and he had been waiting for it to arrive ever since, and it had come in the mail two weeks ago, and he had kept it in the drawer of his bedside table and looked at it every night until tonight, when he had carried it down in the inside pocket of his suit jacket the way Albert was now carrying it in the inside pocket of his coat.

David closed his eyes.

He died on the morning of the twenty-first of December, 1917. Albert had been at Camp Funston for three months. Hanna sat beside the bed with David's hand in hers, and after his breathing had stopped she stayed there for a long time, and then she went downstairs and wrote a letter to her son about the weather and about the store and about the snow that had come early that year, and she did not mention David, because Albert was at Camp Funston and was about to be sent to France, and a son about to be sent to France did not need to know that his father had died.

She would write to Albert every few weeks for the next year and she would not mention David in any of those letters. She read Psalm 91 every night for both of them — the same Psalm she had told Albert to read, the same words about the shadow of the Almighty and the terror by night — and she folded each of Albert's letters into the wooden box on her bedside table where she kept them, and she lived through the war one letter at a time and one Psalm at a time, and she did not tell her son.

---

Camp Funston was on a flat plain in eastern Kansas where the wind came from a direction Albert did not know. He noticed the wind on the first day. It was the same wind, in some sense — the same prairie wind that had blown across his entire life — but it was coming from the southwest instead of the northwest, and that small change made everything feel slightly wrong, the way a familiar room feels wrong when the furniture has been moved an inch.

The 340th Machine Gun Battalion was being organized as part of the 89th Division. Most of Albert's fellow soldiers were South Dakota boys — German-Russians like himself, from Eureka and Hosmer and Leola and the small towns up and down the McPherson County line. He recognized half a dozen surnames the first morning at roll call. He nodded at the men who shared them. They nodded back.

The drill sergeants were not from South Dakota. They were from Kansas and Missouri and Iowa, and they pronounced the German names of their recruits the way men pronounce the names of things they intend to break. *Shrye-burr.* *Eye-zack.* *Shmit-y.* They mispronounced them the first time deliberately and then watched to see if the recruit would correct them, and the recruits learned by the second day that the correct response to a deliberately mispronounced name was *yes, sergeant* and nothing else. Albert answered to *Eye-zack* for nineteen months without complaint.

The slurs were worse. A man from Kansas urinated on a private's bunk because the private's name was Schreiber. A drill sergeant asked Albert in front of the company whether he planned to shoot the wrong way when they got to France. A boy from Eureka named Hauser was beaten in the latrine one night by men from another company who had decided the war needed a target and Hauser was within reach.

Albert learned the Hotchkiss M1914. Fifty-two pounds without the tripod, strip-fed, air-cooled, eight rounds per second. He learned to strip it and reassemble it in the dark by feel alone. He was promoted to corporal in the second month. He had not asked for the promotion. The captain had given it to him because the captain needed men who could stay calm when the world was exploding, and Albert at twenty-nine had a decade of life on most of the boys in his squad.

In the evenings, after lights-out, he played the violin. Quietly. The barracks was a long wooden building with a row of bunks along each wall, and the sound of a violin in a quiet barracks at ten o'clock at night carried further than Albert had expected. He played German hymns. *Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott.* He played folk songs his mother had taught him as a child. *Shenandoah. Down in the Valley.* He played the way a man plays at three in the morning when he cannot sleep, and the only thing keeping the dark away is the small steady sound of the bow on the strings.

The men in the barracks listened. By the end of the first week, the whole barracks was listening. By the end of the first month, men from the next building were sitting on the steps outside in the cold to hear better.

Sergeant Brown — the worst of the Kansas drill instructors, the one who had been calling Albert *Eye-zack* for two months — passed Albert on the parade ground one morning and stopped him.

"Eye-zack."

"Yes, sergeant."

"You the one playing the fiddle at night?"

"Yes, sergeant."

Brown looked at him for a long moment. "Don't stop."

He walked on. He never said anything else about the violin. Albert kept playing.

He read the Bible his father had given him in the evenings before he played. He read the Psalms. Every now and then he stopped on a verse and looked at it for a long time and thought about his father in the suit at the kitchen table on the morning he had left, and then he went back to reading. The Bible stayed in the inside pocket of his coat when he was not reading it. The leather of the cover began to soften from being held against his body, and the brass clasp began to dull from the same.

Hanna's letters reached him every few weeks. They were full of the small news of Eureka — the weather, the store, Hank's cornet practice, the Schmidt brothers, the price of wheat. She did not mention his father. Albert noticed at some point that she had stopped mentioning his father, and he thought about asking her about it, and he did not ask, because his mother was the kind of woman who left things out of letters when leaving them out was the kindest thing to do, and Albert was his mother's son and trusted her to know what to leave out.

---

On the fourth of June, 1918, Corporal Albert Friedrich Isaak boarded the SS *Khyber* at the port of New York and watched the Statue of Liberty get smaller as the ship steamed out into the harbor.

He had never seen her before.

His father had been in this same harbor in April of 1878, on the deck of the *Mosel* — seventeen years old, the second-eldest Isaak son — looking at the buildings of lower Manhattan rising up out of the water. The statue had not been built then. David had seen the harbor without her in it, with nothing but the tall city across the water and the gulls overhead and the smell of the docks rising up off the wharves, and Albert had heard his father describe it exactly once, in the parlor of the house above the store, years ago.

*It was very tall, the city,* David had said. *I did not know a country could have buildings like that.*

Albert had been ten years old. He remembered the way his father had said it — quietly, the way David said the things that mattered most to him, with the small economy of a man who did not waste words on anything important.

He thought about that now, at the rail of the *Khyber*. He thought about his father at seventeen looking at the tall city, and about himself at thirty looking at a green woman on a green island, and about how the Atlantic was the same Atlantic and how the boat was a different boat and how the direction was the wrong direction. He watched the Statue of Liberty until she was a green-blue point on the horizon, and then he watched the point until it was gone, and then he went below to find his bunk.

The Bible was in his coat pocket, against his heart. The violin was in his pack.

---

The forest in the Lucey Sector was older and darker than any forest Albert had ever known. He was on guard duty at the eastern edge of the American camp on a night in the second week of August, 1918. His partner had gone back to the latrine ten minutes ago. The moon was up but the canopy was thick, and the moonlight came down in small broken patches on the forest floor.

He heard the movement before he saw the boy.

It was not the random sound of an animal. It was deliberate — a footstep, a pause, another footstep. Albert raised his rifle and stepped behind the trunk of an oak and waited.

The shape came out of the shadows about thirty feet from him. It was a boy in a German army uniform that did not fit him — too big in the shoulders, the trousers rolled at the cuffs. He had no helmet. He was carrying a pack on his back. His face in the moonlight was the face of a child, maybe seventeen, maybe younger.

Albert's orders were clear. He did not shoot.

He lowered the rifle and stepped out from behind the oak. The boy saw him in the same instant — the small startled jerk of a deer that has just realized it is not alone — and froze, and his hands came up empty in front of him.

"*Raus*," Albert said.

The boy stared at him. He had not expected German.

"*Raus. Verschwinde.*"

The boy understood. He turned and ran — low and fast, back into the dark, back through the perimeter — and Albert watched him until the sound of his footsteps was gone.

He stood alone in the patch of moonlight for a moment with his rifle in his hands. Then he walked back to his post and waited for his partner to come back from the latrine. When his partner came back and asked if anything had happened, Albert said no, nothing had happened, and the two of them walked the rest of the post together until dawn.

---

Saint-Mihiel began at one in the morning on the twelfth of September with a barrage of three thousand guns along a forty-mile front. The ground dissolved. The sky turned white — not the white of daylight but the white of sustained explosion, a strobing percussive whiteness that seared the afterimage into the eyes so that when you closed them the landscape was still there, inverted, pulsing. The man next to Albert pressed his face into the mud and screamed, and the scream was inaudible.

Then the infantry went forward, and Albert gave the order, and the Hotchkiss hammered to life.

The salient fell faster than anyone had expected. The Germans had been holding it for four years and were tired and undersupplied and finished, and when the Americans came over the top in numbers the Germans broke. By the second day Albert's squad was moving forward almost faster than they could carry the gun, the four of them — Albert, a private named Lucas from Aberdeen who had attached himself to Albert at Camp Funston, and two other men whose names Albert would not remember by Christmas — humping the Hotchkiss and the tripod and the ammunition strips through the mud.

On the third day they came to the dugout.

It was an enormous stone structure — a deep underground chamber faced with cut limestone and reinforced with timber, big enough to shelter a German company from American shelling. The Americans had walked past it during the assault and had not even known it was there. Lucas found the entrance. He came back to where Albert was setting up the gun and said, "Corporal. There's a hole in the ground. I think there are men in it."

They took eight men down with bayonets fixed, and Albert called down into the dark in German to come out with hands up, and after a long silence the Germans came out.

They came out in a column of two. There were two hundred of them. Two hundred German soldiers in field-grey uniforms with their hands above their heads, walking up out of a stone hole in the middle of a French forest, and most of them were quiet but some of them were smiling — the relieved careful smiles of men who had been certain they were going to die in that hole and had instead been given their lives by the fact of being captured.

One of them looked over at Albert as he passed. He was an older man, maybe forty, with the lined weather-beaten face of a farmer and an ill-fitting uniform and the tired arms of a man who had been walking with his hands up for two minutes already. He saw Albert standing there with the Hotchkiss, and he must have read something on Albert's face — some small recognition, some flicker of the German that Albert had been carrying inside him since he was a child — and he called out:

"*Gottseidank ihr seid Amerikaner. Die anderen hätten uns nicht so gut behandelt.*"

Thank God you are Americans. The others would not have treated us so well.

Albert gave him a small nod and said nothing. The older man kept walking, and the column kept moving past, and Albert did not respond in German.

Lucas, beside him, said: "What did he say?"

"He said thank God we are Americans."

"You speak German?"

"My father did. It was his first language. I grew up with it."

Lucas nodded. He went back to counting prisoners.

That night Albert and Lucas slept inside the dugout. The Germans had built it for themselves and had vacated it in the morning, and the Americans took it over because a stone dugout was the best shelter against air raids any of them had ever seen, and Albert lay on a wooden plank shelf inside it and listened to Lucas snoring beside him and tried to imagine the two hundred men who had been lying on these same shelves twenty-four hours earlier, listening to the American shells walk across the ground above their heads, and waiting.

---

Three days later, in a quieter sector behind the front, Albert sat on a wooden ammunition crate with a regulation field postcard and a pencil and wrote to his mother.

*Dear folks:*

*Since I wrote you last we have moved around quite a bit and have had several days of fighting on the front line. They (the germans) were taken by such well planned surprise of the Sammies that they had no time to fight, they surrendered and retreated so fast that it took us a whole days travel to catch up. During our time up there our company only lost one man and so did B. company with a few more wounded.*

*No doubt you will get to read all about this drive because it was the most successful drive of any made by the Americans and that the date would go down in history, that was the morning we went over the top. moving pictures and things you hear about going over the top are not at all exaggerated because it sure is what Gen. Sherman said it was. It rained most all the time we were mud from head to foot which made it disagreeable.*

*The last two nights Lucas & I and quite a few others slept in a large stone dug out to make safe for air raids. Out of this dugout were captured 200 prisoners who went in there during our shell fire. It was fun to watch the prisoners come marching in, in a column of two waving their hands and smiling to think they were still alive and didn't have to fight any more. One spoke to me the other morning and he said they sure were glad that their enemy were Americans rather than the others.*

*Your loving son,*

*Albert*

He addressed the card, gave it to a runner, and went back to his squad.

---

Two weeks later, the 89th Division was redeployed to the Meuse-Argonne.

It had been raining in the Argonne for weeks before the Americans arrived, and it kept raining after they arrived, and it did not stop. The trenches that had been trenches when the war began had become winding rivers — slow brown rivers with sandbag banks, rivers that a man waded along instead of walked through, rivers that smelled like decay and wet wool and the particular sweet rot of standing water covering things that had been alive. The mud between the trenches was worse. It came up to a man's knees in the better places and to his waist in the worse ones, and the worst places swallowed men, horses, even tanks. A pair of British tanks that had tried to cross a clearing on the third day were still there a week later, half-submerged, only their turrets sticking out of the brown.

The mud could swallow tanks. The gas was worse than the mud, but only because the mud had already finished what the gas was about to start.

The men on the other side of the wire spoke German. The shouting at night across no-man's-land — the curses and the prayers and the cries of the wounded — came in the language Albert had grown up with at his father's table. He understood every word. He understood the German prayers. He understood the German pleading of a man in the wire who had been hit in the stomach and wanted his mother. The understanding was a knife that went in with every voice and stayed in.

The gas came on the night of the seventh of October.

He was in a shell hole with three other men — Lucas and two of the younger boys. They had set up the Hotchkiss in the rim of the hole and had been laying down intermittent fire across a clearing for most of the day, and the order had come down at dusk to hold the position overnight. The rain had been falling since noon. The hole was filling with water, and the four of them were sitting on their packs to keep the packs dry.

He smelled garlic.

It was the wrong smell for the place. There was no garlic in the Argonne. There was no kitchen for fifteen miles. He lifted his head and smelled it again, and he knew, with the slow cold knowledge of a man who has just realized something he should have realized thirty seconds earlier, what it was.

"*Gas.*"

He said it the way the manual said to say it — once, clearly, without panic. "Masks. *Masks.*"

They reached for their masks. Albert had his own out and over his head before the second breath, and the rubber sealed against his face and the canvas straps tightened, and his face was protected. But the two breaths he had taken before the mask went on had carried the vapor down into his lungs. He helped Lucas with his mask. The other two got theirs on. They sat in the shell hole in the dark with their masks on and the gas pooling around them invisibly in the water at their feet, and at first nothing happened, because mustard gas does not announce itself. It does not burn the way fire burns. It bonds. It bonds to wet skin and to wet wool and to the soft tissue of the lungs, and it sits there, and it begins to take the cells apart slowly, one at a time, over hours.

The first thing he felt was a tickle at his wrists where the cuffs of his coat were wet. Just a tickle, the kind a man would scratch absently and forget about. He scratched it absently. The tickle did not go away. After a few minutes the tickle was an itch, and after a few more minutes the itch was a burn, and the burn was the kind of burn that did not stop when he stopped scratching, because the thing causing the burn was still there in the wool against his skin and was still reacting with the skin and was going to keep reacting with the skin for as long as the wool was there, which was hours.

Then the same thing started at his neck where the collar was wet. Then in his armpits where the shirt had soaked through from the rain dripping down from the helmet. Then — worst — in his groin, where the skin was thinnest and the gas had pooled in the folds and the folds had held the gas like a cup. By the time the rain stopped the burning at all four sites was a single continuous fire, and Albert was sitting very still in the bottom of the shell hole because moving made the wool of his uniform shift against the burning skin, and shifting the wool made the burning worse.

He could feel the blisters rising. They rose slowly — over the course of the next hour, an hour and a half — small at first, then larger, then the size of grapes, then the size of plums. They rose under his clothes where he could not see them. He could feel them straining against the inside of his uniform. He could feel the skin on top of them stretching to its limit. He knew, in the way a man knows things about his own body, that the skin was going to start tearing soon, and that when the skin tore the fluid inside the blisters was going to come out, and that the fluid was going to be clear and yellow and was going to smell like garlic, because the gas that had made the fluid was still in the fluid.

The first blister broke at his right wrist around two in the morning. He felt the small wet release and the smell of garlic rising up out of his sleeve, and he understood that the worst of the night was just beginning.

His lungs were the worst of it. The two breaths he had taken before the mask had been enough. His chest felt at first as if he had run too far in cold weather — that small tight ache that comes with overexertion — and then the ache became a burn, and then the burn became the kind of burn no breath could clear, because the thing causing the burn was inside the lung tissue itself, bonded to it, taking it apart one cell at a time. Every breath after that was a small contract he had to negotiate with his own body. The body wanted to cough. Coughing made it worse. The body wanted to take a deep breath. A deep breath made it worse. The only thing that did not make it worse was a small shallow breath in and a small shallow breath out, and even those breaths were a labor, and even those breaths left him with less air than the one before.

He sat in the bottom of the shell hole through the rest of the night with the burning and the blisters and the lungs, and he did not move, and he did not speak, and he counted his breaths because counting was the only thing that kept him from panicking, and at some point he stopped counting because the counting itself had become too tiring to keep up with.

Stretcher bearers found them at dawn after the wind had shifted. Albert was still conscious. He walked out of the shell hole leaning on one of the bearers, with his mask still on. They did not let him take it off until they were a hundred yards back from the front and the air was clean enough to breathe.

When they took the mask off him, he saw the sky for the first time in eight hours.

---

The field hospital was a long canvas tent behind the lines, and Albert was in it for seven weeks.

The first three weeks were the worst, because the gas was still in him. Mustard gas does not finish its work in a single night. It keeps reacting with the tissue it has bonded to for as long as the tissue is alive, and the tissue at his wrists and neck and armpits and groin was alive and was going to stay alive, and the gas in it was going to keep working until the body had broken down every molecule of it — and the body could only break it down so fast.

The blisters that had broken in the shell hole were the smaller ones. The larger ones broke over the next two days, one by one, slowly, while he lay on his cot trying not to move. Each one was the size of a small orange by the time it broke. The fluid was clear and pale yellow and smelled of garlic, and it stained the sheets, and the orderlies changed the sheets three times a day for the first week and twice a day after that. The skin where the blisters had been did not heal. The skin came off in sheets. It came off in long strips when the gauze was unwrapped, and the orderly who unwrapped the gauze had to do it slowly, slowly, the way you peel a label off a bottle that does not want to come off, and the skin came with the gauze and the new pink skin underneath was so tender that Albert made a sound when air touched it.

The orderlies put fresh gauze on the new skin. The new skin tore. They put fresh gauze on the new new skin. The new new skin tore.

His wrists looked, by the end of the first week, as if they had been peeled. The skin from the cuff to halfway up the forearm was raw and weeping and bright pink and seeping clear fluid that the orderlies wiped away with cotton swabs that came back stained yellow. The skin at his neck looked the same. The skin in his armpits was worse — the folds had held the gas longest, and the skin there had come away in larger pieces, and the new skin underneath was the color and the texture of meat that has been left out too long. The skin at his groin was the worst of all. He could not look at it. He did not want to. The orderlies who changed the dressings there were French nurses who had been doing this work for three years and had stopped reacting to anything they saw, and they changed the dressings twice a day with the cool detached efficiency of women who had run out of horror and had only the work, and Albert stared at the canvas roof of the tent and counted the slats in the wooden frame above him until they were done.

The pain was not a single pain. It was four pains and then later it was five, and the four pains were at the four sites where the gas had bonded to his skin, and the fifth pain was inside his chest where the lungs were doing their slow chemical work of dying and rebuilding. Each of the pains was constant. Each of the pains was at a slightly different pitch. The wrist pain was a sharp insistent burning. The neck pain was a deeper throbbing. The armpit pain was an ache that made him want to hold his arms away from his body, except that holding his arms away from his body made the gauze shift, and the gauze shifting made everything worse. The groin pain was a thing he could not even describe to himself. The lung pain was the worst, because the lungs could not stop. The lungs had to keep moving every few seconds for the rest of his life, and every movement of the lungs was a movement of damaged tissue against more damaged tissue, and there was no position in which the lungs could rest.

He could not sleep for more than ten minutes at a time. Every ten minutes the body would shift involuntarily, and the shift would tear something, and the tearing would wake him, and he would lie awake in the dark in the canvas tent and listen to the small wet rattles of the men in the cots around him and try to find a position that did not make any of the five pains worse, and there was no such position, and after a few minutes he would shift again and the cycle would begin again.

He was thirsty all the time. The fluid loss through the broken skin was constant, and the orderlies brought him water every hour, and he drank it in the small careful sips that were all his lungs would allow, and he was always thirsty, and he was always sweating from the low fever the body was running to fight the chemical injury, and the sweat was making the gauze damp, and the damp gauze was making the new skin macerate, and the maceration was making the healing slower, and there was nothing anyone could do about any of it except keep changing the dressings.

He stopped speaking in the second week. There was nothing to say, and saying anything cost him a breath he could not afford to spend, and Madame Levert — the small dark-haired French nurse who had taken care of him from his first morning in the tent — understood that he had stopped speaking and stopped expecting him to. She asked her questions in French and he answered with his eyes — closed for yes, open for no — and she changed his dressings without small talk and brought him water without small talk, and she sat with him sometimes in the evenings on the small wooden stool beside his cot for a few minutes, not saying anything, just sitting, and her presence beside him in the dark of the tent was the only kindness in his world for those weeks, and he held on to it the way a drowning man holds on to a rope.

The armistice happened in the middle of his fifth week. The guns had been audible from the canvas walls the entire time he had been in the tent — a low continuous rumble from the east that became part of the air the way a heartbeat becomes part of the body. At eleven o'clock in the morning on the eleventh of November, the rumble stopped. It did not fade or taper. It stopped, all at once, the way a clock stops at the end of its winding.

The men in the tent heard it. One by one they turned their heads toward the canvas wall. Nobody said anything. A young English nurse came in through the entrance flap a minute later and she was crying, and she said, in English, *it's over, they've signed it, it's over*, and the men in the tent did not respond. Some of them closed their eyes. One of them, in a cot at the far end, said the word *Mutter* very quietly. Albert lay on his cot and listened to the silence outside the tent and thought it was the loudest silence he had ever heard.

He did not yet know that his father had been dead for almost a year.

---

The letter came in the second week of December. It was from his brother.

Madame Levert brought it. She sat down on the small wooden stool beside his cot the way she sat down most mornings, and she had a letter in her hand, and Albert could see by her face that the letter was something different from the letters she usually brought him.

"*Caporal. Vous avez une lettre. De votre frère.*"

Albert's eyes narrowed. Ed did not write. Ed had written him exactly twice in fifteen months, both times one paragraph long, both times with a small piece of practical news and his name at the bottom and nothing else. Ed did not write letters unless Ed had something to say that Hanna was not saying.

"Will you read it to me, Madame?"

"*Oui, Caporal.*"

She opened the letter. She read it through once silently, the way she read English letters, sounding the words out in her head, and her face did the small careful thing it did when she had understood and was deciding how to say so. Then she began to read.

*Dear Albert.*

*I am writing to tell you what Mother could not. Father died on the morning of December twenty-first, last year. It was his heart. He had been failing since before you went to Camp Funston, and the doctor told us in November that it would not be long, and we did not tell you because Mother could not bear to and I agreed with her. I am telling you now because the war is over and you are coming home and you should not walk in the door without knowing.*

*Mother has been reading Psalm 91 every night. She will be glad to see you.*

*Father's last words were to Mother and they were in German and they were about you. I will tell you what he said when you come home.*

*Come home, Albert.*

*Your brother,*

*Edmund*

Madame Levert folded the letter and laid it on the cot beside Albert's bandaged hand.

Albert did not move for a long time.

His father had been dead for almost a year. His father had been dead the entire time he had been in France. His father had been dead when he had stood at the rail of the *Khyber* and thought about him at seventeen looking at the tall city. His father had been dead when he had read the Psalms in his bunk at Camp Funston by the light of a lamp on a wooden crate. His father had been dead when he had written the postcard about the prisoners at Saint-Mihiel. His father had been dead when the gas had come in the shell hole. His father had been dead the whole time, and his mother had not told him, and his mother had written him every few weeks for a year and had filled the letters with the weather, and Albert understood — slowly, the way he understood most things — that she had not told him because she had been keeping him alive. A man in a trench in the Argonne did not need to know his father was dead. A man in a trench in the Argonne needed to believe there was a father waiting at home, and Hanna had given him that for as long as she could.

He thought about his father in the suit and the polka-dot tie at the kitchen table on the morning he had left.

"Madame."

"Yes, Caporal."

"My coat is on the hook by the door. There is a Bible in the inside pocket. Will you bring it to me?"

She got up and walked to the entrance of the tent and found his coat and brought him the Bible. She placed it carefully in his bandaged hand. He could not unfasten the brass clasp with the bandages, but he did not need to. He held the small book against his palm and closed his eyes.

*Gut, Albert. Spiel weiter.*

He tried to remember his father's face, and he could not quite. He could remember the suit. He could remember the polka-dot tie. He could remember the carefully waxed mustache and the neat side part in the dark hair, and he could remember the trembling fingers on the small Bible on the table. But the face — the face was already going, his father had been dead for almost a year, and Albert understood that this was something he was going to have to fight against for the rest of his life.

He did not cry. He held the Bible against his bandaged hand and listened to the small coughing sounds of the men in the cots around him, and at some point he fell asleep.

Six weeks later, in a casual company at Saint-Aignan, Albert found his pack on a wooden shelf in a quartermaster's tent. The violin case was inside it.

He carried it back to his bunk in the long wooden barracks of the casual company and set it on the bed. He sat down beside it and looked at it for a long time. Then he opened it.

The violin was inside, in the same condition it had been the last time he had closed the case in October. The bow was in its clip. The small block of rosin in its compartment. The varnish on the face of the instrument caught the grey afternoon light coming through the barracks window.

He took the violin out and tuned it slowly, his fingers stiff from the gas and the new skin on his wrists tender under the gauze, and he drew the bow across the strings once, and the sound that came out was the sound he remembered from the parlor of the house above the store in Eureka in the winter of 1905, when his father had sat in the chair across from him and had closed his eyes and listened.

He played the first four bars of *Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott.*

A mighty fortress is our God.

He played them once, badly, and stopped. He put the violin back in the case. He closed the lid. He set the case on the floor beside his bunk and lay down, and the small Bible was in the inside pocket of his coat where it had been for nineteen months, and his father was dead, and the silence outside the barracks was the silence that had been there since the eleventh of November, and Albert listened to it until he fell asleep.
