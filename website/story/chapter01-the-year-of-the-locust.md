## Chapter One

# The Year of the Locust

Pabianice, Duchy of Warsaw — June 1812

---

![Map of the Duchy of Warsaw, 1809-1813](/img/maps/1809-duchy-of-warsaw.png)

The byre was hot already, and the stink of it stuck in the back of Karl's throat.

Johann forked old straw into the barrow while Karl dragged it out through the mud toward the midden behind the shed. By the time he got back, Johann already had another load waiting.

Karl stopped beside the doorway breathing hard.

“You aiming to bury me under this thing?” he said.

Johann leaned on the fork a moment, considering the barrow seriously.

“Not yet.”

Then he added another forkful anyway.

Hexe the one-eyed grey barn cat watched from the rafters with the contempt of all cats. Sweat ran down Karl's neck into his collar. He was eleven, and he'd been helping in the byre on summer mornings since he was old enough to carry a bucket, and it had not got better.

In the yard Dorothea was feeding the chickens from her apron, scattering grain in careful little handfuls the way Mother liked. The hens darted around her feet in a frenzy of feathers and noise. The rooster — a vicious old red-and-black thing that ruled the chicken yard like a small bad king — strutted among them with his head high and his bad temper shining in his eyes.

Dorothea tried to shoo him away from the smaller hens.

The rooster spread his wings.

“Oh, leave off,” she said.

The rooster came at her at once.

Dorothea squealed and jumped backward into the feed bucket, scattering grain across the yard. The hens exploded after it. Tasso woke from his patch of sun and began barking furiously at everybody.

Johann leaned on his pitchfork laughing.

“You started it,” he said.

“I did not.”

The rooster came after her again.

Through the open kitchen door Karl could see Mother kneading dough on the long table, the morning's loaves taking shape under her hands. Father and Michael were off in the far field hilling potatoes, and Christian had been sent with them. The sun was already too high. The geese at the well had gone quiet.

Tasso growled.

It was a low growl, almost in his chest. He had stopped barking at the chickens. He was on his feet now, head turned toward the post-road beyond the rye field, ears forward.

“Tasso,” Karl said. The dog did not look at him.

Johann straightened slowly.

Karl set down the barrow handles. After a moment he felt it through the soles of his bare feet — a faint, even pressure. Not a sound. A shaking.

Dorothea looked between them. “What is it?”

Karl looked toward the road again. “Do you feel that?”

The shaking came again, faint through the ground beneath them.

Johann frowned. Then he straightened at once.

“Come on.”

They walked across the yard and along the cart-track to the rise at the edge of the rye field. Johann went ahead through the rye, pushing the stalks aside with both hands while Karl followed close behind. Behind them Dorothea remained standing near the chicken yard, one hand still clutching her apron full of grain.

The rye was up to Karl's chest, green and just beginning to gold at the tops, two weeks short of being ready. The shaking grew stronger as they walked. Johann opened his mouth to say something and then closed it again when he saw the empty sky.

They climbed the rise and looked east.

---

The first thing was the dust: a long yellow fog along the post-road as far as Karl could see in either direction, hanging in the still air at the height of a man's chest. The road, when his eyes found it through the dust, was full — not crowded but full, edge to edge, surface to bottom, going one way.

Then the sound came up to them on the rise: a rolling murmur made of a thousand kinds of noise at once. Hooves on packed earth, iron rims on stone, boots, harness, voices, the lowing of cattle, a single sharp distant whistle. Karl had never heard so many sounds together in his life, and they added up to something that was almost not noise any more but a kind of weather.

Blue coats. White crossbelts. Brass buttons catching the high sun and winking. Cavalry with lances longer than the men were tall, the pennons hanging limp in the dust. Wagons with green-painted canvas. Black squat cannon on their wheels. Behind it all, a shambling herd of cattle being driven with sticks toward slaughter.

The languages came up the rise in pieces. French first, sharp, snapped from somewhere in the column like a whip. Then a singing one he had never heard before, and that he later learned was Italian. Polish too, low and muttered. Dutch, which sounded like German with stones in its mouth. And then — he leaned forward — German itself, but with the vowels chopped short, a hard clipped German from somewhere he had never been.

“Vorwärts!” a voice was shouting somewhere down in the river of blue. “Vorwärts, vorwärts!”

His own word, in another man's mouth, marching for a French emperor toward a country Karl could not properly imagine. He felt the small jolt of it. Beside him, Johann had gone very still.

“French,” Johann said quietly.

But not only French.

Down in the column rode cavalry in crimson and dark blue with tall square-topped czapkas that even Karl recognized now: Poles. One of the old men in town had once called them Napoleon's favorite horsemen. Farther back someone shouted in German. Beyond that came another language Karl had never heard before at all.

A wagon halted in the road below the rise. One of its team-horses had gone down in the harness, and the driver — a small wiry man — jumped from the box and began to whip the horse where it lay, methodically, across the flank and the neck, as if he were beating a rug. The horse did not get up. After the third blow the driver took out a knife, cut the traces, and helped two other men roll the horse out of the road into the ditch on the far side, where it sank into the long weeds with one yellow eye half-open. The wagon moved on. A fly settled on the horse's eye, and another, and another.

Johann caught Karl's arm hard enough to hurt and pointed toward the yard.

Two soldiers had vaulted the low fence behind the kitchen garden. They were running low and quick across the chicken yard. One of them swept up a brown hen by the legs as easily as a man picking up his hat; the other got two. The hens screamed. Karl waited for Tasso to bark. Tasso did not bark — he had stayed back by the kitchen door. Karl waited for Mother to come out. She did not. She had seen the rifles too. The men ran back across the fence with the hens, and were absorbed into the column, and one of them was already wringing the first neck as he walked.

Johann drew breath to shout after them.

“No,” Karl hissed.

Johann looked at him.

“They have rifles.”

Johann's jaw tightened. After a moment he looked back toward the road and said nothing.

There was a small spring at the foot of the rise, a thread of clear water that came out of a flat rock and made a green pool before vanishing into the ditch. A French soldier had broken from the column and was kneeling at the spring with his shako tilted back, filling a tin canteen. He was older than the others, grey-haired under the shako, his face dark from a long sun. When he had filled the canteen he did not stand at once; he pressed his hand into the small of his back the way old men do. Then he looked up and saw the boys.

The soldier studied them a moment. Johann shifted first, wary but upright. Karl stayed still.

Then the man came up the rise.

“Jungen,” he said. “Brot?”

“Nein,” Karl said.

The sergeant nodded as if that were the answer he had expected. He drank from his canteen, deeply, his throat working. Water ran down his chin into the grey stubble. When he was finished he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked east, where the column was going. He looked east for a long time.

“Russland,” he said, “ist groß. Junge — Russland ist zu groß.”

He looked at Karl — the smaller of the two — and fished in his coat pocket for a small dull copper coin, almost the colour of a roof-tile, and held it out. Karl did not move. The sergeant made a small wry face, took Karl's hand by the wrist, and laid the coin in his palm. Stamped on it was a profile he had never seen before, a man with a Roman wreath and short hair, a stern profile, almost a god's. The metal was warm from the man's coat.

“Danke,” Karl said.

The sergeant gave a small nod that was not a smile. He turned and walked down the rise to the road and back into the column. Within thirty paces Karl could not pick him out of the river of blue.

Karl closed his hand around the coin. Johann was already starting down through the rye, glancing back once to make certain Karl was following.

They went down the back way through the field, the stalks whipping at their bare legs. Behind them the army continued to pass.

---

By dusk the household had pulled itself in. Father had taken the pig deep into the woods that afternoon and tied her to a young oak; the horses were standing behind the orchard, out of sight of the road; the granary door was locked, and the key was on Father's belt and no one else's. From across the rise, very faintly, like a millstream in another valley, the road was still going.

When the meal was put on the table Mother lit the candle without a word — the only one, on a Wednesday in June, which was unusual but which no one remarked on. The family sat down around it in the order they always sat. Father at the head, his huge scarred hands folded, the missing fingertip on the left ring finger catching the light. Mother opposite him with Ernst on her lap, his small head against her breast. Michael on Father's right — the heir's place, though no one would ever have said so. Johann beside Michael, Karl beside Johann. Christian at the foot. Dorothea on Mother's left, very small and very upright.

There was milk soup on the table with crumbled black bread and a knot of bacon-rind in it for the grease, and the hard cheese on its board, and a pile of green onions from the garden, and the stoneware jug of small beer Mother had brewed two weeks ago. Beside each plate, an egg. They had not had an egg apiece since Easter. Karl looked at his and knew what his mother had done. Sometime between his telling her about the road and her setting the table, she had decided that her family was not going to be hungry tonight. That was her answer.

Father bowed his head. The family bowed.

“Komm, Herr Jesu, sei unser Gast,” he said, in the voice he had said it in every night of Karl's life, “und segne, was du uns bescheret hast. Amen.”

“Amen,” the family said.

They ate. The boys told it: the road, the horse in the ditch, the woman with the silent baby, the two stolen hens, the sergeant at the spring. Russland ist groß. Karl laid the coin on the wood between him and his father, where it stayed for the rest of the meal. Father did not interrupt them. When they had finished he sat for a long moment with his hand resting beside the coin, not touching it.

It was Christian — twelve, gap-toothed, the world still small enough that any question could be asked at table — who broke the silence.

“Vater,” he said. “Why is there a French army on our road?”

Father chewed his bread. He looked at the candle. Then he said, “Because we live in the Duchy of Warsaw, and the Duchy belongs to the French. For now.”

Mother set down her spoon. She looked at Father a long moment. Then she said, low: “And our sons are old enough to die for it.”

Father did not answer.

Christian, with the unguarded curiosity of a twelve-year-old, asked: “Will they come back? The soldiers?”

Karl, beside Johann, watched his mother. He saw the small piece of bread she had been moving in slow circles in the milk soup in front of Ernst stop. He saw her bend her head, as if listening to a sound nobody else in the room could hear. He saw her look down at the small soft head of her youngest son against her breast — the round skull, the pale hair, the curl of the little ear pressed against the dark wool.

She did not look at Christian. She said, very quietly, into the top of Ernst's head:

“Some of them have mothers.”

And then she did not say anything else.

Karl felt something turn over in his chest, low down, the way a stone turns over when it's pushed. Mother was not at this moment thinking about the soldiers. She was thinking about her own boys, all five of them at the table with her right now, and she was holding the smallest one.

Beside her, Dorothea — eight, who did not understand the line, who understood enough — reached across and laid her thin small hand on the bare top of Ernst's foot where it dangled out of the swaddling of Mother's apron. Just her hand on his foot. A child's gesture. Karl saw it, and the stone turned over again.

After a while Father reached out and laid his great scarred hand briefly over Mother's on the wood. He did not squeeze. He laid it there, and then he took it back.

The candle burned down. Father read a short passage from the Gospel of Matthew, and the family knelt for the Vater unser. Ernst was carried already deep asleep to the cradle by the cold tile stove. Karl lay on the bench by the Kachelofen with Johann beside him. Through the wall the cows breathed. From the cradle Ernst breathed. From across the room two breaths went on, one slow and even, the other listening.

Outside, very faintly, the road was still going.
