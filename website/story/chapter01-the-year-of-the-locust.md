## Chapter One

# The Year of the Locust

Pabianice, Duchy of Warsaw — June 1812

---

![Map of the Duchy of Warsaw, 1809-1813](/img/maps/1809-duchy-of-warsaw.png)

The byre was hot and the stink of it stuck in the back of Karl's throat. He had pitched out three barrows of old straw with two still to go, his shirt already wet through, and for the third time that morning he wondered why Mother had sent Johann to the chicken yard instead of him. Johann was thirteen and Johann was useless. Johann could make feeding chickens take longer than writing a letter.

Karl pitched another forkful into the barrow. The wet straw came up heavy and sour. Hexe, the one-eyed grey barn cat, watched from the rafters with the contempt of all cats. He had done this byre on summer mornings since he was eight, and it had never once got better.

From the yard came the sound of his brother Johann not feeding the chickens. The hens were screaming, the rooster was screaming, and Tasso the yellow farm dog, who had been asleep in his patch of sun, was barking at the screaming. Karl set the pitchfork against the post and went to the byre door.

Johann was throwing handfuls of grain at the rooster's head and laughing. The rooster — a vicious old red-and-black thing that ruled the chicken yard like a small bad king — had decided the grain was war: he was running at the hens with his wings out and his beak open, and the hens were running at the fence, and Johann was bent double, pleased with himself.

“Johann.”

“What?”

“Feed them. Don't fight them.”

“He started it,” Johann said, of the rooster, with the absolute conviction of a thirteen-year-old. He scattered the rest of the grain in a wide arc, generously, and the chickens forgave him at once. Karl wished he could throw something at him.

Through the open kitchen door across the yard Karl could see Mother kneading dough on the long table, the morning's loaves taking shape under her hands. Father and Michael were off in the far field hilling potatoes, and Christian had been sent with them; their absence was the shape of the morning. The sun was already too high. The geese at the well had gone quiet. The stork on her wagon-wheel platform on the gable had her head tilted at the same angle as Tasso's.

Tasso growled.

It was a low growl, almost in his chest. He had stopped barking at the chickens. He was on his feet now, head turned toward the post-road beyond the rye field, ears forward.

“Tasso,” Karl said. The dog did not look at him.

Karl set down the pitchfork properly this time and stepped out into the yard. After a moment he felt it through the soles of his bare feet — a faint, even pressure. Not a sound. A shaking.

“Johann. Come here.”

“What is it?”

“Come here.”

Johann, hearing something in his brother's voice, came. They crossed the yard past the bread oven and the well, out through the gap in the wicker fence, and along the cart-track to the rise at the edge of the rye field. The rye was up to Karl's hip, green and just beginning to gold at the tops, a month short of being ready. The shaking grew stronger as they walked. Johann opened his mouth to say something — thunder, probably; the boy thought everything was thunder — and then closed it. The sky was empty.

They climbed the rise and looked east.

---

The first thing was the dust: a long yellow fog along the post-road as far as Karl could see in either direction, hanging in the still air at the height of a man's chest. The road, when his eyes found it through the dust, was full — not crowded but full, edge to edge, surface to bottom, going one way.

Then the sound came up to them on the rise: a rolling murmur made of a thousand kinds of noise at once. Hooves on packed earth, iron rims on stone, boots, harness, voices, the lowing of cattle, a single sharp distant whistle. Karl had never heard so many sounds together in his life, and they added up to something that was almost not noise any more but a kind of weather.

Blue coats. White crossbelts. Buttons and brasses catching the high sun and winking. Cavalry with lances longer than the men were tall, the pennons hanging limp in the dust. Wagons with green-painted canvas. Squat cannon on their high wheels, grimed dark from the road. Behind it all, a shambling herd of cattle being driven with sticks toward slaughter.

The road ran past the foot of the rise, close enough that pieces of it came up to them whole — shouts, a snatch of song, an oath and the laughter after it. French, snapped from somewhere in the column like a whip. And then, underneath it, German. Real German, roared at a wagon team not fifty paces below: “Vorwärts! Vorwärts!”

His own word, in a soldier's mouth, marching for a French emperor toward a country Karl could not properly imagine. He felt the small jolt of it. And the longer he listened, the more of it he heard — German hard and clipped, from somewhere north he had never been; German soft and rounded, like the Swabians he half-understood on market days — threaded through the Polish and the French and languages he could not name at all.

Beside him, Johann had gone very still.

“Karl,” Johann whispered. “Some of them talk like us.”

“I heard.”

“Are they ours?”

“No.”

A wagon halted in the road below the rise. One of its team-horses had gone down in the harness, and the driver — a small wiry man — jumped from the box and began to whip the horse where it lay, methodically, across the flank and the neck, as if he were beating a rug. The horse did not get up. After the third blow the driver took out a knife, cut the traces, and helped two other men roll the horse out of the road into the ditch on the far side, where it sank into the long weeds with one yellow eye half-open. The wagon moved on. A fly settled on the horse's eye, and another, and another.

Further along, where the road cut past the bottom of the long field, a party of cavalrymen had turned their horses into the rye. They had scythes — cavalrymen with scythes, swinging them like farmers — and they were cutting Father's rye, green as it was, and tying it in bundles behind their saddles for the horses. Nobody in the column looked at them. It was not stealing, the way they did it. It was grazing.

“Karl,” Johann hissed, pointing. “The chickens.”

Two soldiers had vaulted the low fence behind the kitchen garden. They were running low and quick across the chicken yard. One of them swept up a brown hen by the legs as easily as a man picking up his hat; the other got two. The hens Johann had fed an hour ago screamed. Karl waited for Tasso to bark. Tasso did not bark — he had stayed back by the kitchen door. Karl waited for Mother to come out. She did not. The men ran back across the fence with the hens, and were absorbed into the column, and one of them was already wringing the first neck as he walked.

Johann opened his mouth to shout. Karl clapped a hand across it, fingers spread over Johann's nose and chin. Johann's eyes went wide above his hand.

“No,” Karl said, very low. “They have muskets. Be quiet.”

Johann nodded against his palm. Karl held him a second more before letting go.

There was a small spring at the foot of the rise, a thread of clear water that came out of a flat rock and made a green pool before vanishing into the ditch. A French soldier had broken from the column and was kneeling at the spring with his shako tilted back, filling a tin canteen. He was older than the others, grey-haired under the shako, his face dark from a long sun. When he had filled the canteen he did not stand at once; he pressed his hand into the small of his back. Then he looked up and saw the boys, and came up the rise.

“Jungen,” he said. “Brot?”

His German came slow and easy, worn soft at the edges — a German from somewhere far west, the Rhine or past it.

“Nein,” Karl said.

The sergeant nodded as if that were the answer he had expected. He drank from his canteen, deeply, his throat working. Water ran down his chin into the grey stubble. When he was finished he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked east, where the column was going. He looked east for a long time.

“Russland,” he said, “ist groß. Junge — Russland ist zu groß.”

He looked at Johann — the smaller of the two — and fished in his coat pocket for a small dull copper coin, almost the colour of a roof-tile, and held it out. Johann did not move. The sergeant made a small wry face and gestured at Karl: take it for him. Karl took it. Stamped on the coin was a profile he had never seen before, a man with his hair cropped short like a Caesar's, a stern profile, almost a god's. The metal was warm from the man's coat.

“Danke,” Karl said.

The sergeant gave a small nod that was not a smile. He turned and walked down the rise to the road and back into the column. Within thirty paces Karl could not pick him out of the river of blue.

Karl closed his hand around the coin. “Home,” he said. “Now.”

They went down the back way through the rye, which whipped at their bare legs. Behind them the army continued to pass.

---

Father and Michael came in from the potato field at midday, Christian trotting behind, and Father did not stop to eat. He had seen the dust from the far field. He stood in the yard for a moment, reading the road the way he read weather, and then the afternoon went to a kind of work Karl had never been taught and understood at once. The pig went deep into the woods, tied to a young oak. Michael took the horses into the forest by the back way, walking them on the grass verge so the track would hold no prints. The granary was locked, and the key went on Father's belt and no one else's. Tasso was put on his chain by the byre door, and did not like it.

The soldiers came into the yard an hour before dusk.

Six men, a corporal, an empty cart, and a young officer the color of the road with a ledger under his arm. The steward who collected the estate's rents came with them — a Pole Father had known since the family had come south onto the estate — a dozen years of markets and winter pipes — and he stood inside the gate and turned the officer's French into German and did not meet Father's eyes while he did it.

Requisition, the officer said. For the army of His Majesty the Emperor. Grain, forage, cattle. There would be a receipt.

Father unlocked the granary himself, before any man could put a bayonet to the door. The officer looked at what the lean year had left on the boards — Karl watched his face do the arithmetic and come up short — and said something that was not a compliment. The men carried out the sacks. At a word from the steward, low, in Polish, two sacks of seed rye were set back inside, and the officer wrote in his ledger and did not argue. They forked half the hay down out of the barn loft into the cart. One of them looked into the pigsty, found it empty, and looked at Father; Father looked back at him with a face like a whitewashed wall, and the man moved on.

Then they took the red heifer out of the byre. Tasso came up against the end of his chain roaring, and Christian hung on his collar with both hands and both heels and talked into his ear until the roaring came down to a growl and stayed there, shaking. Mother stood in the kitchen doorway with Ernst on her hip and watched the heifer led out of the gate on a rope — calm, stupid, licking the corporal's sleeve as she went.

The officer tore a leaf from his ledger, wrote on it, and held it out. French words, a number, a signature. Payment, the steward translated — redeemable from the intendance of the Grande Armée. He said it the way a man reads the words off a stranger's grave.

Father took the paper. “Danke,” he said. It was the only word he spent on them.

They went back down the track toward the road, the cart groaning, the heifer trotting to keep up. Karl watched from the fence until they were small. Somewhere down there she would join the herd he had seen that morning, the one being driven east with sticks, and the road would eat her the way it was eating everything — a mouthful at a time.

By dusk the yard was quiet. The house held the last of the light in its lime-white walls, the way it always did, visible from the road for exactly what it was. From across the rise, very faintly, like a millstream in another valley, the road was still going.

---

When the meal was put on the table Mother lit the candle without a word — the only one, on a Wednesday in June, which was unusual but which no one remarked on. The family sat down around it in the order they always sat. Johann Michel Andreas Isaak — Father — at the head, his huge scarred hands folded, the missing fingertip on the left ring finger catching the light. Mother opposite him with Ernst half asleep in her lap. Michael on Father's right — the heir's place, though no one would ever have said so. Karl beside Michael, Johann beside Karl. Christian at the foot. Dorothea on Mother's left, very small and very upright.

There was milk soup on the table with crumbled black bread and a knot of bacon-rind in it for the grease, and the hard cheese on its board, and a pile of green onions from the garden, and the stoneware jug of buttermilk, cool from the cellar. Beside each plate, an egg. They had not had an egg apiece since Easter — not in this lean spring, the second after the harvest failed, and not on a day when the army had emptied half the granary. Karl looked at his and knew what his mother had done. Sometime between the soldiers going out the gate and her setting the table, she had decided that her family was not going to be hungry tonight.

Father bowed his head. The family bowed.

“Komm, Herr Jesu, sei unser Gast,” he said, in the voice he had said it in every night of Karl's life, “und segne, was du uns bescheret hast. Amen.”

“Amen,” the family said.

They ate. Over the food the boys told the part of the day the others had not seen: the road itself, the horse in the ditch, the rye cut green, the two stolen hens, the sergeant at the spring. Russland ist groß. Karl laid the coin on the wood between him and his father. Father looked at it a moment. Then he picked it up, tilted it to the candle, and read the worn letters around the rim the way he read everything — slowly, and to the end. “Napoleone,” he said, and set it back down. Then he took the officer's paper from inside his shirt and laid it beside the coin, and the two payments lay together by the candle for the rest of the meal.

It was a while before anyone spoke. Then Father looked at the paper by the candle, and at the coin beside it, and said, without looking up: “Locusts. And these are not done eating.”

Mother set down her spoon. She said, low, not to anyone: “Locusts don’t take sons.”

Father did not answer.

It was Christian — twelve, gap-toothed, the world still small enough that any question could be asked at table — who broke the silence. “Will they come back? The soldiers?”

Karl, beside Michael, watched his mother. He saw the small piece of bread she had been moving in slow circles in the milk soup in front of Ernst stop. He saw her bend her head, as if listening to a sound nobody else in the room could hear. He saw her look down at her youngest son, half asleep and still nursing at her breast — the round skull, the pale hair, the curl of the little ear pressed against the dark wool.

She did not look at Christian. She said, very quietly, into the top of Ernst's head:

“Some of them have mothers.”

And then she did not say anything else.

Karl felt something in him give. Not a loud thing — the small inward shifting of a beam under load. Mother was not at this moment thinking about the soldiers. She was thinking about her own boys, all five of them at the table with her right now, and she was holding the smallest one.

Beside her, Dorothea — eight, who did not understand the line, who understood enough — reached across and laid her thin small hand on the bare top of Ernst's foot where it dangled out of the folds of Mother's apron. Just her hand on his foot. A child's gesture.

After a while Father reached out and laid his great scarred hand briefly over Mother's on the wood. He did not squeeze. He laid it there, and then he took it back.

The candle burned down to a thumb’s width. Father read a short passage from the Gospel of Matthew, and the family knelt on the plank floor for the Vater unser. Ernst was carried to the low bed already asleep.

Karl lay on the bench by the big stove, Johann’s back warm against his, the coin folded in his fist. Through the bench, through the floorboards, through the packed earth under the house, he could feel it still — fainter than a heartbeat, and as regular — the road, going east in the dark.

He was asleep before it stopped.

It did not stop.
