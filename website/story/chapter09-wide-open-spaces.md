## Chapter Nine

# Wide Open Spaces

Parkston, Dakota Territory — April 1878 to June 1880

---

Johann's wife had made *Bierocks*.

Gottlieb ate three of them before he said a word, because he had been eating train food and ship food and emigrant-hall food for six weeks and his body recognized this meal the way a man recognizes his own language after months of listening to someone else's. The Schulzes were at the next table over, because Caroline's house was not big enough to seat twenty-one people at one table and so she had set two and pushed them close together.

"The soil," Gottlieb said finally. "Tell me about the soil."

"Black," Johann said. "Like Kulm. The real Kulm."

"Not like Sophiental."

"Nothing is like Sophiental. Sophiental was God's punishment for something we did in Poland, and I am not sure what we did but it must have been spectacular."

Gottlieb laughed. It was the first time he had laughed since Bremen and the sound surprised him. From the Schulz table, Johann the elder looked over and his face moved into something that was almost a smile.

"How deep?"

"Deep enough. You can push a rod in to your elbow and not hit clay. It has never been farmed. It has never been *anything*. Just grass, since the beginning of the world."

"And the rent?"

"There is no rent. There is no landlord. There is a hundred and sixty acres for any family that will live on it for five years and break it and not run away. The catch" — Johann pointed his fork at Gottlieb — "is that you have to live on it. Each family on its own quarter section. No village. No neighbors on the next wall."

"How far?"

"A half-mile, at the closest."

Wilhelm Schulz looked up from the next table. He had been quiet through most of the meal in the way Wilhelm was always quiet. "A half-mile between us?"

"At the closest."

Wilhelm thought about this for a moment. "Then we file next to each other. As close as the law allows. You and us, Gottlieb. The old people in their own cabin. We do not break this family up to satisfy the United States Congress."

"That was already my plan," Johann said. "I have spoken to the man at the land office in Yankton. He has promised to hold four quarter sections together along the section line west of mine. He thinks I am insane. He has never met a German before."

Old Johann Schulz set down his fork. He was eighty years old and he had not, in all that time, been the kind of man who made speeches at the table.

"In Pabianice," he said, "my father's farm and his brother's farm were separated by a wagon road. You could shout from one porch to the other and be heard. A half-mile is not a wagon road. But it is closer than an ocean."

Christiana put her hand on his sleeve. He covered her hand with his own and did not move it.

Caroline brought more *Bierocks*.

---

They broke ground three weeks later.

It was the second week of May. Gottlieb stood at the edge of his quarter section with his sons behind him — Jacob, twenty; David, eighteen; Daniel, fifteen — and Wilhelm Schulz beside him with his own oldest sons, and behind all of them the borrowed plow and the team of oxen Johann had loaned them until they could buy their own.

Nobody had said anything for a few minutes. They were looking at the grass.

"It's never been broken," Wilhelm said finally.

"No."

"Not once."

"Not in ten thousand years."

Wilhelm took off his hat, then put it back on. "Then we should pray first."

So they prayed, six men and three boys standing in a line at the edge of a quarter section in Dakota Territory in May of 1878, with their hats off and their heads bowed and the wind moving across the unbroken grass behind them. *Vater unser im Himmel.* The wind took the words and carried them west.

Then Gottlieb took the handles of the plow.

The first furrow was the hardest thing he had done with his body in twenty years. The prairie sod was not soil in the way Bessarabian soil was soil. It was a living mat of roots that had been knitting itself together since the last ice age, and the plow share went in two inches and stuck. He leaned. The oxen leaned. He and Wilhelm together put their weight on the handles and shouted at the team and the share moved an inch, then another, and then with a sound like cloth tearing the sod gave way and a long curl of black earth turned up behind them, glistening, alive, smelling of something Gottlieb had not smelled in nineteen years.

He let go of the handles. He walked around to the open furrow. He knelt down.

The dirt was black to a depth he could not see the bottom of. He picked up a handful and held it to his nose and smelled it, and the smell was the smell of Kulm — the real Kulm, the Kulm of his childhood — and he squeezed the dirt in his fist and did not say anything.

David came around the front of the team and crouched beside him. He did not say anything either. He put his own hand into the open furrow and lifted his own handful and held it up the way his father had held it, and the two of them knelt there in the broken ground for a moment without looking at each other, and then David, who was eighteen and quiet and had never been the brother who said the thing out loud, said: "*Vater. Es ist gut.*"

Father. It is good.

Gottlieb nodded once, hard, because his throat had closed.

From behind them, fifteen-year-old Daniel said, in a voice loud enough to carry: "If you two are going to kiss the dirt, can the rest of us go home? I would like to be home for supper."

Wilhelm laughed first. Then Jacob. Then Gottlieb himself, still kneeling, his hand still in the furrow, laughing into the open ground until his eyes watered. David was the last to laugh — he gave Daniel a look first, the long-suffering older-brother look — and then he laughed too, and he stood up and brushed the dirt off his knees and went back to his place behind the team.

They cut the second furrow.

---

That first summer's *Baschtam* was extraordinary.

*The likes of which we never again had,* Gottlieb would write thirty-six years later, and the sentence trembles with remembered abundance. The kitchen garden Louisa had planted in May went into ground that had never been asked for anything, and when she asked, it gave back with a generosity that bordered on the absurd. Melons the size of a man's head. Cucumbers longer than a child's arm. Pumpkins so heavy that Solomon, who was twelve and determined to be useful, dropped one on his foot and limped for a week.

Everyone hired out at the harvest. American wages came back in dollars and silver, paid in full on the day the work was done, and Gottlieb counted the money at the kitchen table by lamplight at the end of every week, carefully, suspiciously, the way he had once counted rubles. The first time he wrote down a week's total he had to recount it twice because he could not believe the number.

In September he walked into the post office in Parkston and mailed a money order for the equivalent of three hundred and thirty rubles to a man in Sophiental. It was the old debt — the money he had borrowed to pay the rent the Akkerman court had told him he owed. He had carried it across an ocean. He walked out into the September sunshine and stood on the boardwalk and felt something inside his chest unlock that he had not known was locked.

That night he opened the family Bible — the one they had carried from Sophiental — and wrote in the margin of the front flyleaf in his careful schoolmaster's hand:

*Den 14. September 1878. Die russische Schuld bezahlt.*

*September 14, 1878. The Russian debt paid.*

He closed the Bible and went to bed.

---

The first winter taught them what the bargain cost.

It came down on them in the second week of November, all at once, in a single afternoon. The sky went grey at noon and the temperature dropped thirty degrees by sundown and by midnight the snow was coming horizontally, driven by a wind that none of them had a word for. Gottlieb stood at the door of the half-finished sod house and could not see the Schulz house, four hundred yards away.

He shut the door. He banked the stove with the buffalo chips the children had spent all autumn gathering off the prairie. He sat down beside Louisa on the edge of the bed.

The roof was sod laid over a frame of poles, and where the chinking had not been his best work, the snow was coming through. Not a lot. Just a fine sift of it, drifting down through the gaps and settling on the blankets and on the children's hair while they slept. Louisa held Antonie under her own coat against her own skin. The baby was warm. Louisa was not.

There was a sound outside.

It was not the wind. The wind was a continuous low howl that you stopped hearing after the first hour. This was something else — a higher sound, intermittent, coming from somewhere out in the dark.

"What is it?" Louisa whispered.

"I don't know."

"Wolves?"

"Maybe."

"Coyotes?"

"Maybe."

"Or just the wind in something we have not learned to listen to yet?"

"Maybe."

They sat in the dark and listened. The sound came and went. Gottlieb did not know what was making it and he did not know how far away it was and he did not know whether the chickens were going to live until morning and he did not know whether old Johann Schulz, eighty years old and frail, was alive at this moment in his bed four hundred yards away in the dark.

Toward morning the wind dropped. The sound outside stopped. Gottlieb got up and went to the door and opened it.

The snow had drifted up to the windowsill on the north side. The air was perfectly still and so cold it hurt his face. Far across the prairie, through the new clean light of a winter dawn, he could see a thin column of grey smoke rising from the Schulz house.

They were alive. The smoke meant the stove was lit. The stove being lit meant someone had lit it.

He shut the door and went to wake the children for breakfast.

---

Emanuel was born on the eighth of May, 1880, in the sod house Gottlieb and David had finished re-chinking the previous October.

It was a long labor. Louisa was forty-two and this was her tenth child and the first one to be born in America. Caroline came from Johann's house with her midwife's bag. Susanne came from the Schulz house with hot water and clean linen. Regina sat at her mother's head and held her hand. The younger girls were sent over to the Schulz house to be out of the way.

Gottlieb sat on the bench outside.

Wilhelm came over from the Schulz farm at midmorning and sat with him for an hour without speaking, which was the kind of company a man needed at a time like this, and then went back to his own work. Old Johann Schulz came over in the afternoon, slowly, on a cane, and sat down on the bench beside his son-in-law and put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.

"I have done this nine times," Gottlieb said.

"Christiana did it eleven times. It is no easier the tenth time than the first."

In the late afternoon Caroline came to the door of the sod house and lifted both her hands and clapped them together once, softly, in the gesture that meant *come*. Gottlieb stood up.

Louisa was sitting up against the pillows with the baby in her arms — a small red wrinkled thing wrapped in linen, with a fuzz of dark hair and a face that was already starting to look like a face. She looked up at her husband and her eyes were very bright.

"A boy."

"I see."

"He is American, Gottlieb. He is the first one."

Gottlieb sat down on the edge of the bed. He put one finger gently against the baby's small fist and the fist closed around it with the unthinking grip of a newborn, and Gottlieb felt the small fingers tighten on his and he understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with words, that the journey was over.

"What shall we call him?"

Louisa thought about it. "Emanuel."

"Emanuel."

"*Gott mit uns*," she said. *God with us.* "Because He was."

---

A month later, the families gathered at Gottlieb's house for the noon meal on a Sunday in early June. It was Gottlieb's turn, and the new baby was old enough now to be passed from hand to hand without his mother panicking about it. Two long tables were set up outside under a stretched canvas because no house in the settlement was big enough to hold them all.

They came in from the section road in twos and threes — Johann and Caroline and their five from the east; August and his Louisa and their four from the north (August had arrived in November and built his house through the winter, and his youngest, Nathaniel, was now seven months old and the size of a small turnip); the other Johann, the cousin from Kulm, who had stepped off the train in Yankton three weeks earlier with his wife and a single trunk and had filed his claim before he had unpacked it; Wilhelm and Susanne and the eight Schulz children walking the four hundred yards from the next quarter section; old Johann Schulz on his cane with Christiana on his arm, carrying their grandchildren's youngest between them.

Twenty-nine people. Eleven of them under the age of twelve. Five sod houses on a half-mile of section road, all of them within shouting distance, none of them more than a brisk walk from the next.

Old Johann Schulz said the grace. He said it slowly, in the German of Pabianice, which was almost but not quite the German of Kulm, and the differences in his pronunciation reminded Gottlieb that this old man had been born in another century in another country and had carried his trunk down from the loft and had crossed an ocean at seventy-eight years of age in order to be sitting at this table on this Sunday saying grace over his daughter's husband's new son.

After the *Amen*, Louisa held Emanuel up so that everyone could see him.

"He looks like a potato," Solomon said.

"He looks like Father," Magdalena said firmly.

"Father looks like a potato."

"*Solomon.*"

"What? It is a compliment. Potatoes are very useful."

Louisa told both of them, in the voice, to be quiet and eat their bread. They were quiet. They ate their bread. The wind moved across the prairie. The grass leaned. The canvas above the tables snapped softly.

Gottlieb sat at the head of the table with his wife on his right and his father-in-law on his left, and he looked down the long row of his family and his wife's family and his brother's family, and he ate his bread.
