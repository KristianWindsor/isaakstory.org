## Chapter Five

# The Capping

Kulm, Bessarabia — February 1816

---

The smell of the Erdhütte never truly left the back of the throat. It was a thick, dark soup of earth, damp wool, the sharp tang of dung-smoke from the clay stove, and the yeast of rising bread. For five months, ten of them had drawn it into their lungs , and soon, it would belong to eleven.

Karl stood on the dugout steps, his shoulder pressed against the sod-brick casing, watching the weak afternoon light leak down into the floor. From here, his eyes traced the line where the roof meet the earth. In September, there had been nothing but empty grass and two surveyor’s pegs sticking out of the steppe. He remembered the skin peeling from his palms as they hacked out the first sod-bricks, digging down into the dirt while the earth hardened against them week by week. They had raced the frost, hauling timber and river-reeds, working until their fingers cracked in the wind. The night the roof finally went on—the first night they slept below the wind instead of in it—Karl had lain awake listening to the silence of the earth. Every foot of the packed-earth wall had a price he knew by heart. It wasn't a picture to be admired; it was a fortress built against a clock.

"Karl, move your boots," Mother said, not looking up from her stool near the stove. She shooed him with the back of her hand, her fingers grey with flour.

He stepped back up a riser, his head nearly touching the timber joist. The dugout had become a workshop, and he was entirely in the way. Dorothea sat beside Mother, her twelve-year-old hands mimicking the sharp, rhythmic tucking of fabric. Every flat surface in the single room was spoken for. A grown man, nineteen years old and three days from heading his own household , he was being herded from corner to corner like a loose hen.

The fact of it returned to him in a sudden, cold jolt: *married*. It didn’t feel like doubt; it felt like a heavy piece of iron he had to carry but couldn't quite find the handles for.

He looked across the crowded room toward the Strümpske parcel a few hundred yards away. He and Elisabeth were not strangers. They had shared a thousand miles of trail dust, mud, and the grey terror of the road from Poland. He had seen her with her hair matted by rain, her face grey with exhaustion, hauling water from frozen ditches. There had been no dances, no stolen glances across a well-lit parlor ; they simply knew exactly what the other looked like when the world was stripped to its bones. When Father and Mr. Strümpske had sat together over a pipe in late autumn, their old heads nodding in agreement, Karl had felt only a quiet, steady willingness. It was arithmetic, and everyone in the dugout knew it : the Isaaks would gain a strong, capable pair of hands before the spring planting , and Elisabeth would have the shelter of a large, many-handed family. The survival of one depended on the strength of the other, and that made the ground beneath them solid.

Through the low doorway, the sky looked thin and silver. The sun at midday was a weak, glareless thing, shedding grey shadows across the snow. It had been wrong since autumn, a visual riddle without an answer, and it left a cold unease resting behind his eyes.

---

Karl did not see the assembly of the crown, not directly. Custom kept him outside the circle of women , but he found the evidence of it on the small split-log table when he returned from the sheepcote.

The women had gathered in the dim light by the clay stove. In Poland, a bride’s hair was bound in fresh green myrtle ; here, the steppe was a vast sheet of iron and white crust, barren of any living leaf. Instead, the colony’s older women had reached into the bottoms of their travel chests.

On the table lay the *Schappel*. It was a fragile, strange skeleton of wire, bent into a curve. Katherina Leischner had brought two glass beads, clear as river ice. Another neighbor had offered a scrap of faded red silk flower; Mrs. Strümpske had contributed a short length of blue ribbon. The crown was being built from the hoarded losses of a dozen families’ journeys. Dorothea watched from the edge, her eyes wide as Mother’s rough, calloused fingers twisted a bit of wire around a bead.

One elderly neighbor woman paused, her thumb lingering over a small tinsel star before she pressed it into Mother’s palm. She said nothing, but her hand stayed still for three heartbeats, holding a memory from a road that had taken her own daughters.

Karl stepped toward the table, his fingers reaching into his vest pocket. He pulled out a short length of green wool thread—something he had saved from a bundle on the wagon months ago. He laid it clumsily beside the wire.

Mother looked up. Her face remained flat, weathered, and unromantic—the road had burned the sentimentality out of her long ago —but her eyes softened as she looked at her second son. She reached out, her hand resting briefly against his knuckles. The skin of her palm was dry and warm. Then, without a word, she picked up the green thread and wove it into the base of the crown. Karl watched it disappear into the knot of hoarded scraps, his own small offering anchored to the collective weight of the colony's past.

---

The morning before the wedding, the cold was a dry needle in the nostrils. Karl stood inside the Strümpske Erdhütte, the atmosphere quiet and weighted.

Mr. Strümpske shifted his weight, his large, ruined hands dark against the knees of his trousers. He was of Father’s generation, his shoulders permanently sloped from the plow. With a slow, deliberate movement that cost him something in the throat, he reached into a small wooden box and drew out his daughter's dowry.

The road had swallowed chests, linens, and featherbeds. What remained could fit inside a single fist. He laid it out on a wool cloth: a carved wooden spoon from their village in Poland, a brass thimble, and a single silver coin, worn smooth at the edges. It had survived the trek only because it was too small to be worth losing.

Karl watched the old man’s face darken with a silent apology he couldn't bring himself to speak ; a man who should have given his daughter cattle and a full wagon was reduced to offering a handful of trinkets.

Elisabeth stepped forward. She did not look down, nor did her face show shame. She closed her fingers over the spoon and the thimble, meeting her father’s eye with a steady, level gaze that spared him the need for words. "It is enough, Father," she said softly.

Beside them, Father and Mr. Strümpske exchanged a brief, dry look. They had buried children within miles of each other on the trail; they did not need to make speeches. A short nod passed between them—the final closing of a contract they had agreed upon before the first snow.

When the old men turned to speak of the rye rations, Karl and Elisabeth stepped back into the shadow of the storage sacks near the door. Because they were formally betrothed, the rigid rules of the colony had unbent slightly over the winter. They were allowed this closeness.

Karl reached beneath her wool shawl, his fingers finding her hand. Her palm was rough with work, her grip firm and steady.

"Tomorrow," he whispered.

Elisabeth squeezed his fingers, her face split by a tiny, wry smile that showed the sharp edge of her teeth. "Tomorrow you must stand still, Karl. The whole valley will be looking."

He felt the small heat of her breath against his cheek before she withdrew her hand. The realization settled into him like a stone into a well: by this time tomorrow, the responsibility would be his alone. He would no longer be a son following in the tracks of his father's wagon ; he would be the man who had to keep Elisabeth alive.

---

The dugout woke long before the first grey slit of dawn appeared under the door. The clay stove hissed as Christian threw on a fresh block of dried Mistholz, the smell of burning dung sharp and dry in the cold room.

Karl sat on the low bench, dressed in his dark wool wedding coat and his good shirt. By the yellow flicker of a tallow dip, he used a dull bone knife to scrape the frozen crust of mud from the heel of his high boots. He worked with obsessive care, digging the brown ice out from the seams until the leather stood clean. It was the only dignity available to him, and he would not stand before the pastor with the dirt of the sheepcote on his feet.

Father walked over, his heavy boots creaking on the packed floor. He didn't offer a speech. He reached into his coat and produced a small wedge of tempered iron—a spare tooth for a harrow, scarce and valuable. He dropped it into Karl’s open hand, then placed his massive palm on Karl’s shoulder. He pressed down once, hard, with the exact same slow, calculating weight he used to judge the muscle of an ox before the yoke. Karl held his ground, tightening his back against the pressure. Father nodded once, a brief, dry movement of the chin. He had weighed him, and Karl had held.

Mother stepped in then, her hands smelling of wild thyme soap. She brushed the lint from his collar, her fingers tugging the wool straight with a fierce, possessive sharpness. Her face was set like flint; she did not weep. This was the final mothering of a boy before he became a husband, and she performed it with the efficiency of a soldier bracing a comrade for battle.

Karl stepped up the stairs and into the open morning.

The steppe was a white wilderness under a dead sky. At the eastern horizon, the sun was rising as a flat, blood-red orb. The glare had been stripped clean out of it; Karl could stand on the frozen crust and look directly into the red center of the light without blinking. It looked like a rusted iron coin hanging in a milky, stagnant pond. It cast no brightness, only flat grey shadows across the drifts. The sight of it sent a cold shudder through his belly. He had no name for it, but he knew the sky had been broken since the previous autumn.

"Come on," Michael said, his own breath freezing into a thick white cloud as he led the way down the valley track toward the mayor's dugout.

---

There was no church in Kulm, nor would there be a proper house of prayer for years to come. The wedding was held in the communal dugout—the largest hole in the valley, usually reserved for town meetings.

Karl stooped as he entered, his shoulder brushing the low joists. The air inside was an almost solid wall of heat and moisture—sixty families had packed themselves into the earth, body to body. The frozen dampness of the log ceiling had begun to thaw from the heat of their breathing, forming tiny, clear beads of water that clung to the bark.

At the far end of the room stood the altar. It was nothing but a split oak plank laid across two flour barrels, but the women had covered it with a single white linen cloth, ironed smooth, which had been carried all the way from Poland inside a family Bible. Two tallow candles—a luxury that had cost Christian three days of bartering with a Russian teamster —burned against the dark sod wall, their little flames sputtering in the heavy air.

The room went quiet as the door at the top of the steps groaned back.

Elisabeth came down the steps. She did not wear white; she was dressed in her finest heavy black wool, her bodice reinforced with stiff linen that made her shoulders straight. Her eyes were fixed on the dirt floor three paces ahead of her, her chin tucked into her collar in accordance with the modesty expected of every young woman in the congregation.

But on her head sat the *Schappel*. The glass beads caught the yellow candlelight, and the single scrap of green wool Karl had given her flashed against the dark fabric of her hair. She looked small under the weight of the crown, yet entirely unbreakable. Karl watched her approach, his throat tightening until he could scarcely swallow.

Mr. Strümpske guided her by the elbow, his great boots heavy on the earth as he brought her to Karl’s side. Behind them, little Samuel stood among the other children, his eyes wide and bewildered as he watched his sister join another family’s circle.

---

Pastor Hartmann stood behind the oak plank, his old black cassock green at the cuffs from years of trail dust. He was the same man who had blessed their departure from Poland, who had buried their neighbors' children by the roadside, and who had prayed over Mother when the marsh fever nearly took her.

He opened his book and began the old German Lutheran rite, his voice deep and steady, skipping not a single syllable of the ancient liturgy. The colonists stood rigid, their heads bowed beneath the low timber ceiling. They clung to every formal word precisely because they were standing in a hole in the dirt; the old language was the only house they had left.

"Anna Elisabeth Luise Strümpske," the pastor read, his voice filling the corners of the dugout. The full name had an immense, metallic weight in the small room—the only time it would be spoken aloud on this day.

Karl tried to listen to the Latinate German, but his mind became hyper-focused on the physical details of the room. He watched a single bead of water gather on the log directly above the pastor’s head. He watched it swell, heavy and clear, reflecting the tiny flame of the tallow candle. He counted the rough marks of the adze on the timber. He focused on the smell of damp earth an arm's length behind the altar. He was the watcher; it was how he survived things that were too large to contain.

Then Elisabeth spoke her response. Her voice was low but perfectly clear, without a tremble.

The weight broke through his defenses all at once. The physical details vanished. As he took the plain iron wedding band from the pastor’s tray, he realized the terrifying magnitude of the moment : he was promising before God and sixty watching households to protect this girl, to find bread where there was none, and to keep her alive against the expanse of the steppe. He could no longer look through a window; he was inside the room.

The congregation began to sing. There was no organ within five hundred miles ; their voices rose a cappella, packed shoulder to shoulder. The old wedding hymn was a wall of harmony that shook the low room until Karl could feel the vibration through the soles of his boots. It was the valley’s true voice, dense and heavy.

*Plip.*

A single drop of ice-cold meltwater fell from the ceiling, striking the bare skin at the back of Karl’s neck. He flinched slightly, the sudden cold snapping him back down into the reality of the dirt room, the smoky tallow, and the solid weight of his wife standing beside him.

---

The moment Pastor Hartmann lowered his hands, the rigid silence of the dugout evaporated. The solemnity did not fade; it was blown backward by a roar of voices and the stamp of feet. The colony had lived through five months of grey frost with nothing to celebrate ; they took hold of this wedding with a desperate, hungry joy.

The planks were cleared of the linen cloth, and the feast arrived. The two families had pared their winter stores down to the bone for this hour. Mr. Leischner had slaughtered a young shoat—a real, bleeding sacrifice from a thin winter herd.

Steam rose from the large earthenware bowls as they were set down. Thick chunks of salted pork fat sat in pools of grey sauerkraut that had been put down in the autumn ; there were loops of coarse dark garlic sausage and loaves of heavy Russian rye flour bread bartered from the soldiers down at the post. It wasn’t an abundant table, but every dish showed the careful, deliberate sacrifice of people who loved them.

Karl sat beside Elisabeth, the noise pressing into his ears until his head rang. Grease shone on his brothers' chins as they tore into the bread. The small room grew hot from the press of bodies, the smell of roasted fat, and the sharp sourness of the cabbage.

A clay jug of harsh grain schnapps went round, burning the throat and loosening the tongues of the older men.

Karl looked down at Elisabeth’s hand resting near his plate. They had never sat like this—side by side before the world.

"Are you eating?" he asked quietly, leaning his shoulder into hers so she could hear him over the din.

Elisabeth turned her head, her face flushed from the heat of the stove. She reached out and subtly dropped a thick slice of sausage onto his plate. "If I eat any more cabbage, Karl, you will have to sleep outside the blanket tonight."

He let out a short, startled laugh—a sudden bubble of relief that broke the tension in his chest. They were still themselves, wry and steady, even under the eyes of the entire valley.

---

Old man Roloff pulled a battered fiddle from a greasy wool sack. The instrument had survived the road inside a flour barrel, its wood scratched but intact. He drew the bow across the strings, and a sharp, screeching Polish village tune leapt into the rafters.

The dugout was too small for anyone to turn a proper circle. The dancing was therefore a matter of stamping and clapping in place—boots coming down hard on the packed dirt until the floor seemed to drop an inch under the weight.

As the heat rose, the moisture on the ceiling liquefied completely. Large drops of cold water began to rain down regularly on the dancers’ heads and shoulders. No one stopped. Michael wiped a drop from his forehead and shouted a joke into the crowd ; it became the great jest of the evening—the steppe winter was literally weeping because it couldn't stop their joy.

Then came the *Brauttanz*. Roloff stood by the altar plank, shouting out the prices. The men of the colony formed a line to dance a few stamping steps with Elisabeth, each paying for the privilege. Mr. Strümpske threw down a handful of iron nails; another neighbor dropped three copper copecks into a wooden bowl; Wilhelm Leischner offered a small twist of black tobacco. It was the colony's practical contribution to the survival of a new household, an investment made in nails and flour.

Karl stood against the wall, watching his wife being swung from hand to hand. He saw her dark wool coat fly out, saw the glass beads of her crown catch the light, and felt a strange, thick pride rise in his throat—a feeling he would spend twenty years learning the true name for.

In the corner, Johann was laughing, his arm brushing against Rosina Leischner’s shawl as they watched the dancers. Karl noticed it with a quiet, knowing look, saving the ribbing for a later day.

---

By nightfall, the air in the dugout had grown thick enough to choke a mule. Karl was sent up the steps to fetch a fresh jug of water from the drift, and as the door swung shut behind him, he heard the light rustle of wool.

Elisabeth had followed him.

They stood together on the dugout steps, just beneath the rim of the earth. The roar of the fiddle and the stamping boots became a dull, low thumping beneath their feet. They had ninety seconds alone, hidden by the dark and the immense cold of the steppe.

Out here, with no elders watching, the public bride vanished. Elisabeth let her chin drop from her collar and leaned her back against the frozen sod casing. Her shoulders slumped with an unglamorous, honest exhaustion.

"My feet feel like they have been hammered into the dirt," she said, her voice dropping its formal cadence.

Karl looked at her profile against the pale snow. "We can go back to our own corner soon."

"Good." She turned her face toward him, her eyes steady and clear in the starlight. "We have the planting to think of when the crust breaks, Karl. We cannot spend all our strength on one night."

It wasn't a romantic sentence, but to Karl, it was better than poetry. It was the speech of a true teammate. She wasn't a girl to be sheltered from the winter; she was a partner who was ready to help him dig through the dirt until they found the spring.

He took her hand, his thumb tracing the callus on her palm, and they stood in the silence for a few remaining heartbeats before turning back toward the light.

---

At midnight, the music died. Roloff laid his fiddle across his knees, and the laughter faded into a heavy, domestic silence. The solemnity returned, but it was no longer the formal weight of the church; it was the quiet, ancient grief of the women.

The older wives of the colony formed a tight circle around Elisabeth in the center of the floor. Mother and Mrs. Strümpske stood at the front, their weathered faces dark under their own linen caps.

Mother reached up, her rough fingers moving with unexpected delicacy as she untwisted the wire pins that held the ribbon crown. She lifted the *Schappel* from Elisabeth’s head. Girlhood ended with that single upward movement.

In its place, Mrs. Strümpske brought forward the *Haube*—the close-fitting white cloth cap of a married woman. Together, the two mothers smoothed Elisabeth’s hair and tied the linen strings securely beneath her chin. From this hour until her death, her hair would be covered from the eyes of men. She had crossed the line.

A low, collective sniffling rose from the circle of women. Mother’s face broke then; two heavy tears leaked from her eyes, tracing the deep wrinkles in her cheeks. It wasn't grief for the match itself, but a shared mourning for the end of a girl's freedom and the beginning of a working wife’s long, unsparing labor. Every woman in that hole had made that same hard crossing. Dorothea watched from behind Mother’s skirt, her twelve-year-old face pale as she realized the true cost of the day.

Karl watched the linen cap go on and felt a sudden, sharp ache of understanding. The day was not equal. He had gained a household and a partner ; she had laid down her girlhood in the dirt.

"Elisabeth," Michael said from the bench, his voice dropping the playful diminutive the family had used for her all winter. The girl-name was left on the table with the wire crown.

---

The walk back to the Isaak Erdhütte took less than three minutes, but the cold made it feel like a league. They walked under an immense, blue-black steppe sky, the crust of the snow crunching like broken glass beneath their boots. It was the first time they had walked anywhere as husband and wife.

When Karl pushed the door open, the reality of their situation met them in the dark. There was no separate cottage for them ; the earth was frozen to the depth of three feet, and no new dugout could be hacked out until May. Elisabeth walked directly into the room that already held nine other people. Eleven of them would share this air.

But the family had given them what privacy the world could spare. Across the far corner, near the storage bins, they had strung a heavy, grease-stained woolen blanket and two old shawls on a length of hemp rope. It enclosed a single yard of packed earth and a straw pallet. That was their entire house.

The rest of the family was already lying down on their pads, their backs turned deliberately toward the corner. Michael was snoring softly with theatrical loudness; Johann was tucking Ernst under a sheepskin without looking up. They were establishing the ironclad peasant contract of the night: they would hear nothing, see nothing, and know nothing.

Karl lifted the corner of the heavy blanket, and Elisabeth stepped behind it into the pitch dark.

---

Behind the blanket, the air was freezing. There was no candle; the stove had been banked low for the night, leaving only a faint, red eye of light that could not pierce the heavy wool weave.

They didn't take off their clothes ; no one on that steppe stripped to their skin in February. Karl could hear the rustle of Elisabeth’s heavy wool coat as she laid it aside, her breath coming in short, shallow puffs in the blackness.

They lay down together on the straw pallet under the immense weight of three sheepskins and two old blankets. They pushed their rough linen nightshirts up, the severe cold dictating the clumsy geometry of the act.

There was no modern elegance to it. It was a quick, fumbling business in the dark, driven by biology and the ancient expectation of the wedding night. For Elisabeth, sixteen and exhausted from the long hours of the celebration, it was awkward and briefly painful. Karl felt her stiffen against him, her fingers digging hard into the meat of his shoulder.

He stopped, his forehead resting against her temple. "Elisabeth?" he whispered.

She let out a low, ragged breath that smelled faintly of the rye bread. In the dark, her knee bumped his with a dull thud. "Karl, your nose is like an icicle."

A short, broken whisper of a laugh came from his throat, and he felt her own ribs shake against his chest. It was the saving grace of two people who actually liked each other ; they were embarrassed together in the dark, three feet from a family pretending to be asleep, and they had survived it. The fumbling cleared away. When they moved together again, it was with a quiet, grounded warmth.

The act itself was brief , but the holding-on afterward was long. Karl lay awake in the true black, his arm beneath her head. Elisabeth’s breathing gradually slowed, her chest rising and falling in steady rhythms against his ribs. Her rough fingers found his hand beneath the sheepskin and locked there, staying.

Beyond the blanket wall, the low murmur of the family’s breathing filled the dugout. It was awkward, yes, but as Karl listened to the heavy rasp of his father's sleep and the soft shifts of the children, he felt a strange sense of security. The new marriage wasn't alone in the dark ; it was cradled inside the breath of a whole family that would help them hold the line.

He let his thoughts turn toward the spring. He thought of the iron harrow tooth in his coat pocket , of the dugout they would cut into their own parcel when the frost broke , and of the children who would eventually fill the space. The red sun at dawn still troubled him , and the winter stores were thin , but the dread remained the size of an ordinary farmer’s worry.

He tightened his grip on Elisabeth's fingers. He held her the way he had once held the old French sou on the road—a small, warm, solid thing that proved the world wasn't entirely empty. Except this time, the coin was alive, and breathing, and his, and the future was hidden inside its breath.
