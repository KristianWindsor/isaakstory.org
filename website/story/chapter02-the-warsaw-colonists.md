## Chapter Two

# The Warsaw Colonists

Blacka, Duchy of Warsaw — June 1812

---

![Map of the Duchy of Warsaw, 1809-1813](/img/maps/1809-duchy-of-warsaw.png)

The wheat was not yet ripe when the soldiers came.

Karl August Isaak was nine years old. He was in the east field with his older brother Johann Michael, pulling weeds from between the rows, when they heard it — a low sound, felt more than heard at first, like distant thunder on a clear day. Johann Michael straightened up and shaded his eyes, looking south toward the road. Karl August stood beside him, the pulled weeds still in his fist, and followed his brother's gaze.

The road was half a mile away, visible as a pale line cut through the green where it crossed the low rise beyond the Kowalski farm. Something was moving on it. At first it looked like a dark stain spreading slowly across the landscape, too wide to be a wagon train, too steady to be a herd. Then the stain resolved into shapes — men on horseback, men on foot, wagons, cannon on wheeled carriages — and the low sound sharpened into something recognizable: the rhythmic crunch of thousands of boots on packed earth, the jangle of harness, the distant snap of pennants in the wind.

"Get down," Johann Michael said. He was fourteen, old enough to know what an army looked like. He put a hand on Karl August's shoulder and pulled him into a crouch between the wheat rows.

They watched from the field as the column passed. It took a long time. The cavalry came first — lancers with red and white pennants, their horses kicking up clouds of dust that drifted across the fields like smoke. Then the infantry, regiment after regiment, their blue and white uniforms smudged grey with road dirt, muskets on their shoulders, faces blank with the particular exhaustion of men who have been marching for weeks and know they will march for weeks more. Behind them came the artillery — heavy guns on iron-wheeled carriages, each one pulled by teams of horses that were already too thin. And behind the artillery, stretching back as far as Karl August could see, came the supply wagons, the ambulance carts, the sutlers, the women and children and dogs that trailed behind every army like a second army of their own.

Karl August had seen soldiers before. Small patrols, couriers, the occasional squadron of cavalry passing through the village on their way somewhere else. But he had never seen anything like this. The column filled the road from edge to edge and spilled over into the fields on both sides, and still it kept coming.

"How many?" Karl August whispered.

Johann Michael shook his head.

"Will they take our wheat?"

"They'll take everything."

They stayed in the field until the column had passed and the dust had settled and the sound had faded to a hum on the eastern horizon. When they walked back to the farm, they found their father standing in the yard with their grandmother, Anna Elisabeth, watching the last of the wagons disappear. She was seventy-two years old, and she did not turn her head when the boys came up behind her. She had seen armies before.

Their father turned to the boys. "Did they come into the field?"

"No," Johann Michael said.

"They will. Tomorrow, or the next day. They'll send foragers. We need to move the stores."

That night, the family buried half their grain in the root cellar beneath the barn, wrapped in oilcloth and covered with straw. It was a crime. Requisition officers had the authority to search farms and seize supplies, and hiding food from the army was punishable by imprisonment or worse. Their father did it anyway. He had been a Prussian subject and then a subject of the Duchy of Warsaw, and he knew that governments came and went but winter came every year, and if you did not have grain in the ground come November, your children starved.

The foragers came two days later, as he had predicted. They took a third of the visible stores, two of the family's four horses, and the good wagon. They left a requisition receipt stamped with Napoleon's eagle — a piece of paper that promised future payment from a government that would not exist in eighteen months.

---

Six months passed. The Grande Armée did not come back. What came back were rumors, then refugees, then the wreckage.

By December, the survivors began staggering west through the Duchy — gaunt, frostbitten men in rags, many of them barefoot in the snow, their eyes emptied of everything except the animal desire to keep moving. Of the 615,000 who had crossed the Neman in June, fewer than 100,000 came back. Behind them came the Russian army, advancing steadily westward, occupying the Duchy of Warsaw without resistance.

Karl August watched them too — the defeated men shuffling through the village in ones and twos, begging for bread, sleeping in barns, some of them dying where they fell. He was nine years old, and he understood, in the way that children understand catastrophe, that the world had broken and would not be put back together the same way.

By the autumn of 1813, in the German colonists' villages, in the churches and farmhouses and meeting halls where they gathered to speak their own language, a new word was circulating.

*Bessarabien.*

---

Tsar Alexander I was calling for German settlers to colonize a vast territory between the Danube and the Dniester rivers that Russia had taken from the Ottoman Empire the year before. The terms were astonishing: sixty-six hectares of free land for every family. Freedom of religion. Exemption from military service. A ten-year tax holiday. Money for building a house.

The German colonists in the Duchy listened with open ears. They had been promised security by Prussia and abandoned by Prussia. They had been promised independence by Napoleon and crushed by Napoleon. The land they farmed was about to change hands for the fourth time in twenty years. More than fifteen hundred German families would accept the Tsar's offer between 1814 and 1815 alone. They would leave behind farms they had spent decades building, because they had learned — in the hardest possible way — that a farm means nothing if the government above you does not want you there.

The recruiters came to Blacka in October. The older men talked in low voices after church. Karl August did not understand most of what they said, but he understood the look on his father's face when he came home from those meetings — the look of a man who had already decided and was working out how to say it.

Anna Elisabeth sat by the stove and said nothing. She had heard offers like this before, and she knew what came next.

One evening Karl August sat down on the bench beside her. She was mending a sock by the firelight, her hands moving without her eyes, the way old women's hands do. He watched her for a long time before he asked.

"Grandmother. Have you ever moved before?"

She did not look up from the sock. "Twice."

"Was it hard?"

She pulled the thread tight and bit it off with her teeth, the way she always did, and only then did she look at him. Her eyes were the pale blue of winter sky.

"You take what you can carry," she said. "And then you go."

She went back to the sock. Karl August sat beside her until the fire burned low.
