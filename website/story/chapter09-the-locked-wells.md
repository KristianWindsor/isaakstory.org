## Chapter Nine

# The Locked Wells

Kulm, Bessarabia — September 1860

---

Gottlieb had the rope around the chest twice when Louise lifted the lid again.

“The scissors,” she said.

“They're in.”

“The little ones.” She dug under his Sunday shirt, leaving white fingermarks on it, found the little scissors, looked at them, and put them back exactly where they had been. “Now they're in.”

He started the knot over.

Their room at the end of the house had never been big. Now it was mostly floor. The bed was down to its boards, stacked against the wall, and the straw sacks were rolled and tied. On the windowsill sat a spoon, one small shoe, and an egg that nobody would admit to putting there.

Louise went back to her dough, humming *Befiehl du deine Wege*, the hymn that tells you to hand your ways over to the Lord. All week she had never made it past the second line.

Their daughter, Regina, sat on the straw sacks and watched him pull the rope tight. For three days her eyes had followed every object out of the room.

“Are we taking the door?”

“The door stays.”

She thought about that. “Then how do we get in?”

“I'll make us one.”

“Out of what?”

He didn't answer. He had seen Sophiental once, in June: a long slope of grass running down to a hollow, the estate's sheep pens up on the rise, and not one tree between there and the sky.

Regina went to look at the door they were leaving, as though to decide whether it would mind.

Their little boy Jacob came in backward, dragging the water pail.

“Water,” he said.

“The well's dry,” said Gottlieb.

“Water.”

He turned around and walked the pail into the doorframe. It boomed like a drum.

In his basket, baby David woke and began to cry. Louise had dough to her wrists, so Gottlieb picked him up and put him on his shoulder. David stopped crying. He went red, and still, and thoughtful.

Warmth spread through Gottlieb's sleeve. It ran down the inside of his arm to the elbow and kept going. When he held the baby out to look, the cloth sagged, yellow and dripping, and something the color of mustard dropped onto the floor he had swept that morning.

Louise laughed so hard she had to put her floury hands on her knees. “He saved that for you.”

“Where's the water?”

They both looked at the crock by the door. At the bottom lay a finger's depth of water, with a drowned fly turning slowly on top.

“Most of it's in the dough,” said Louise.

He cleaned David with the dry end of the cloth and a twist of straw, which David did not like. The smell stayed on his sleeve.

“Tell me about the rent again,” said Louise.

“It isn't high.”

“You always say it the same way.”

He could have said more. He knew the figure the estate's man had given him, a ruble and a half for every dessiatine, and he knew what that came to in work: five or six days of a hired hand's pay for each one. He had done the sum with a nail on the whitewash of the shed wall, and in his head in church when he should have been praying. It came out right every time. It came out right if the rain came.

“It's the part I know,” he said.

Louise looked at him over the dough for a moment. Then she went back to kneading, and started the hymn again from the beginning.

He took both pails down from their nails.

“Leischner's,” said Louise. “And don't stand there all morning helping some girl with her pails.”

He was still smiling when he reached the street.

---

At their neighbor Leischner's well there was a line: women with pails, girls with pails, a boy hugging a clay jug to his stomach. Someone's grandmother sat on an upturned bucket in the shade of the wall with her eyes shut, holding a place.

Leischner sat on the well itself. A chain ran from its wooden lid through the handle of the crank and was fastened with a padlock as big as a fist. The key hung on a string around his neck.

“Two pails a house,” he said to each one who reached him.

At the front of the line, Gottlieb's brother August was being charming.

“They're not big horses,” he was saying. “They'd hardly drink anything.”

“Then they won't miss it,” said Leischner.

August laughed with the others in the line. He saw Gottlieb and waved him forward. “Tell him they're small horses.”

Gottlieb had seen their horses. He said nothing.

“Last week the bucket came up full at twenty turns,” said Leischner. “This morning it took twenty-six, and it came up half. When it's mud, it's mud for the whole street. Take them to the river. Everyone else has.”

The woman ahead of Gottlieb had a feverish baby on her hip. When she reached the front, Leischner wound up her two pails, and then a third, and did not count it.

“The schoolmaster prayed for rain again on Sunday,” someone said.

“Then the Lord has heard him three Sundays running,” said the grandmother on the bucket, without opening her eyes. “He'll answer when He's ready.”

Gottlieb was winding up the second pail when his younger brother Johann came up the street with the wagon. Both barrels were already in the back, with the long trough, and the brown cow and the heifer were tied on behind.

“Who told you to bring the barrels?”

“Nobody.” Johann never said more than he had to. “I looked in our well.”

The brown cow stretched her neck over the lid and licked the wet boards. Leischner pushed her nose away. She put it back.

Their youngest brother, Martin, riding on the back of the wagon, was sent home with the two full pails.

They went down the hill with the sun already hot on their necks. Below the last yards the fields were gray stubble. The weeds along the track had died standing and rattled when anything brushed them. Grasshoppers sprang up ahead of the horses. Dust hung in the still air, and Gottlieb could taste it.

Far below, the Kogälnik had shrunk to a chain of brown pools in a bed of cracked mud.

Halfway down, the cows smelled it. The brown cow jerked her rope loose from the wagon and broke into a trot. Johann caught the end and dug in his heels, and she dragged him ten steps with the rope burning through his fists. The heifer strained after her, pulling her own rope taut.

“Let her go!” Gottlieb shouted.

Johann let go.

Half of Kulm was already there. Horses stood knee-deep in the one pool that still had a hard bottom, flicking their tails. The air stank of mud and dung, and under that, of something sweeter.

The sweeter smell was a cow.

She lay at the edge of the upper pool, where she had gone in and not come out. Only her head and her swollen side showed above the mud, the hide stretched tight, and the crows had been at her eyes. Flies crawled in and out of the holes.

The brown cow went past her at a lumbering run, straight at the water, and straight into the mud. At the first step she sank to her knees. At the second she was in to her belly. She bellowed and heaved and went deeper, and green dung sprayed out behind her across the cracked mud.

*Lord,* Gottlieb thought, *not the cow.* She was the children's milk.

He was in the mud before he had decided anything. It took him to the knee, warm on top and cold underneath, and each foot came out with a long sucking noise and a black stink.

“Not by the horns,” said someone above him. “You'll break her neck.”

Daniel Schulz, Louise's older brother, stood on the hard bank with a coil of rope over his shoulder. He had been there since dawn with his father's horses, and his trousers were perfectly dry.

“Get the rope behind her front legs.”

“I know,” said Gottlieb, who had been reaching for her horns.

Daniel threw the rope. It landed in the mud beside him.

“Behind the front legs,” said Daniel.

“I heard you.”

Gottlieb worked the rope down behind the cow's legs, his cheek against her hot, heaving side. Johann was in the mud on her other side before anyone called him. He passed the end back under her chest and knotted it without a word. Daniel unhitched the near horse and tied the loose end to its collar.

“When she heaves, you lift and the horse pulls,” Daniel called. “Not before. August! Not before!”

August, at the horse's head, had already started.

The rope went tight. The cow bawled. Gottlieb put his shoulder under her ribs and sank to his thighs. Then she heaved, and he lifted, and the horse leaned into its collar, and the mud let go of her all at once with a long, sucking gulp. She came up the bank black to the shoulders and shaking.

She had been out about as long as it takes to say amen, and already she was looking at the water again.

They set the trough on the hard ground near Daniel's horses. Johann went down to the pool along the edge, stepping on the old reed roots, and he did not sink. Gottlieb followed in the same steps.

Johann filled. August carried. Gottlieb poured. The horses drank in long, loud swallows, and each pail was gone before the next one came. Sweat ran into Gottlieb's eyes. The mud on his legs dried gray and cracked and pulled at the hairs. He thought of Louise with the children, and the morning gone.

The brown cow would not touch the trough. She wanted the river. In the end Johann filled a pail and held it under her nose, and she drank from that, slobbering over his wrists, while he stood with his arms shaking and let her.

“Right before the grapes,” said August, going past with a full pail. “You always did know when to leave.”

Gottlieb didn't answer. He poured so hard that water jumped out of the trough and ran off into the dust, and he had to stand there and watch it go.

In two weeks the whole family would be in the vineyard from first light to dark. If he stayed to pick, he would reach Sophiental with the plowing not begun and the winter wheat still in its sacks, and that ground would give no bread next summer. He had told himself all summer that they could spare him. Now he pictured the full tubs waiting at the ends of the rows.

They worked a while without talking. Then August slipped off the roots, went in to the knee, and said a word their mother would have washed out of his mouth.

“When we pick,” he said, dragging his leg out, “you take the rows along the—” He stopped. “Martin can take them.”

“Martin eats half of what he picks,” said Johann.

Gottlieb laughed. The rows along the top had been his since he was old enough to carry a basket. This year Martin would pick them, and eat half, and Gottlieb would be three days away and never know which half.

A little later, bending to the pool beside him, Johann said, “Is there much land down there?”

“More than anyone could plow.”

Johann nodded and filled the next pail, and did not ask anything else.

When the horses lifted their dripping muzzles from the trough, the sun stood straight overhead. Gottlieb turned toward the harness.

“Not yet,” said August. He reached under the wagon seat and brought out a watermelon as big as a lamb, wrapped in his coat.

“We don't have time for that.”

“We have time for this.”

Daniel sat down in the shade of the wagon and stretched out his dry legs, and that settled it.

Johann folded his hands. August left the knife standing in the rind while Johann prayed, “Come, Lord Jesus, be our guest, and bless what you have given us. Amen.” Then August leaned on the knife, and the melon split with a crack, red all the way through.

August cut out the heart of it, the sweet middle where there were almost no seeds, and put it in Gottlieb's hands without looking at him.

It was warm from the sun and so sweet it made Gottlieb's jaws ache. Juice ran down his chin and dripped off his elbows into the dust.

August spat a seed at Johann, missed, and hit Daniel on the knee. Daniel looked at the pink spot on his dry trousers. Then he spat one back.

Gottlieb sat with his back against the wheel. The horses dozed in the heat, a lark went up somewhere over the stubble, and for as long as the melon lasted there was nothing to do but eat it.

They filled both barrels after that, pail by pail, and started home with water sloshing out at every rut. It left dark spots in the dust behind them.

---

Louise was not at home when the barrels came into the yard.

“At her mother's,” said his mother from the cooking shed. “With all three.”

There was no water to spare for washing. He scraped his legs with a stick and walked up the street to the Schulz yard as he was.

The Schulz table stood under the acacia, crowded with bowls and elbows. Mrs. Schulz took one look at him and set a bowl at the empty place.

“Sit. Eat. Don't touch anything.”

Daniel's wife, Dorothea, lifted their sleeping boy's feet out of the space on the bench.

“Slowly,” she said. “He's only just fallen asleep.”

Gottlieb lowered himself beside her. The boy stirred, and they both waited until he settled. Across the table, Louise was nursing David. Regina and Jacob shared a stool beside her.

There were potatoes boiled in their skins, sour cream with dill, cucumbers, and bread. Gottlieb split a hot potato with his thumbs and dragged it through the cream.

Beyond the table stood Daniel and Dorothea's wagon. They were going to Sophiental too. It had been half loaded for a week, and Mrs. Schulz walked the long way around it to fetch another loaf.

Louise's brother Wilhelm Schulz leaned back. He had shared Gottlieb's school bench and never let him forget a thing. “Daniel says you went swimming with a cow.”

Wilhelm and Daniel laughed, and Mr. Schulz laughed with his mouth full. Louise did not. She was looking under the table at Gottlieb's trousers.

“And who's going to wash those?” she said. “With what?”

Mr. Schulz swallowed and held up a finger. “Rain by Sunday. I feel it in my knee.”

The air was as dry as flour.

Louise reached over, took the cucumber out of Wilhelm's hand, bit it, and gave it back. “Careful, Vater, or it'll be the goose again.”

The whole table burst out laughing. Wilhelm put his head down on his arms. Mr. Schulz turned the color of a beet.

“What goose?” said Gottlieb.

Nobody told him. Louise patted his arm. Nearly six years married, and there was still a goose.

Louise's younger brother Simon had stopped eating to listen.

“Is it true you can smell the sea from there?”

“When the wind comes from the south.”

“What does it smell like?”

Gottlieb had to think. “Like the brine barrel,” he said. “Only cold.”

Simon glanced toward the loaded wagon before going back to his potato.

Daniel came round with the wine and filled Gottlieb's cup.

“Father thinks we'll be back within the year.”

Gottlieb looked up the table. Mr. Schulz had Regina on his knee and was explaining to her exactly how Sunday's rain would arrive.

Daniel almost smiled. He looked out through the gate at the long street and the yards along both sides of it, all the way down to the last white wall.

“Fifty-four on each side,” he said. “I've counted them too.”

Gottlieb had counted them in every season. Behind every one of those gates was a farm the Crown had given out forty-five years ago, to be held, the paper had said, *in perpetual and hereditary possession*. A farm passed to one heir. Seven sons had sat at his father's table. For six years Gottlieb had bid for strips of orphans' land at the yearly leasing and lost them to men with more money. He had hired himself out at twenty-five kopecks a day. He had watched for a farm to fall vacant, and none had.

Wilhelm was pestering his sister. “So what's in Sophiental that isn't here?”

“My own oven.” Louise glanced at Gottlieb. “And my own door, that I can shut.”

“And what's here that isn't there?”

She didn't answer. She shifted David to her other breast, and he took it with a small grunt, one hand opening and closing on the neck of her dress.

Mrs. Schulz leaned toward her. “When his teeth come, rub his gums with—send for me. I'll come and—”

She stopped.

She cut a slice of bread that nobody had asked for and put it on Louise's plate.

“I'll remember how,” said Louise.

“You won't. You'll send for me.” Mrs. Schulz looked at the bread a moment longer. “And I'll keep the little grave.”

Louise nodded. The boy they had lost lay in the children's rows at the cemetery, under a cross that did not reach Gottlieb's knee.

At the head of the table Mr. Schulz was still explaining the rain to Regina. Simon slipped over the back wall to find his friends, was called back, and slipped over again. The smoke from a dozen cooking sheds stood straight up in the still evening air.

---

Four days later the wagon stood loaded in the yard before sunrise.

The chest was roped for the last time. Louise had not opened it again, though Gottlieb had seen her look at it. The straw sacks and feather covers were packed around it, with the flour, the big pot, two spades, and the sacks of seed wheat. A bundle of roof poles ran the length of the load. The plow rode on top, lashed on its side, and a basket of hens complained until they gave up. The brown cow was tied on behind, and the heifer stayed.

His eldest brother, Gottfried, was already at the horses, his sleeves wet to the elbows. On the side of the wagon, lashed on with new rope, rode the small barrel, full to the top and beaded with water. Two empty pails stood beside the wheel.

It was the clean well water they had fetched for the house. Nobody had asked Gottfried to set it aside for the journey.

“That's for the children,” Gottfried said. “Not the horses.”

“I know.”

“You'll give it to the horses.”

“I won't.”

“By noon,” said Gottfried.

Gottlieb fitted the collar on the near horse. The buckle would not go. He pulled the strap free and tried again, his fingers as clumsy as if it were January. Gottfried watched him try twice.

“Hold it steady,” he said.

Gottlieb held the buckle. Gottfried drew the strap through.

Their mother had David first. She held him against her shoulder with her nose in the soft fuzz on his head and breathed in, as though she meant to keep some of him. He caught her cap string in his fist and would not let go, and she had to open his fingers one at a time. She gave Regina a loaf wrapped in a cloth, for the road and not for now, and she kissed Jacob on both cheeks.

Then she came to Gottlieb.

“Don't stand in the road looking at the sky,” she said, “and let the horses walk you into a ditch.”

“No, Mutter.”

For a moment she seemed to wait, the way she had always waited at the table for someone else to begin. Then she put her hand on his head, and he bent so she could reach.

“The Lord bless you and keep you. The Lord make his face shine upon you, and be gracious unto you. The Lord lift up his countenance upon you, and give you peace.”

“Amen,” said the yard.

August handed up the last watermelon. Johann went along every rope once more, as if the knots might have come undone in the night. Martin held the horses' heads. Across the wide street, Leischner had come out to his wall with the key on its string, and he lifted a hand.

“After the grapes,” said Gottfried, “August and I will bring the second load, the rest of the seed and the flour. I'll have a look at this ground of yours.”

“Bring Mutter.”

“If she'll sit three days on a wagon.”

“I've sat longer,” said their mother.

Louise climbed up with David bound against her in the striped carrying shawl, and Gottlieb shook out the reins. The wheels turned.

The street in Kulm was long and wide. For a long time they could see the gate, and the gate could see them: his mother, small and dark, and Gottfried beside her with a hand on her shoulder. Neither of them waved. They stood there getting smaller and did not go in.

At the end of the street Daniel's wagon was waiting, with Dorothea on the seat and their boy in her lap.

“Jacob's holding his shirt up,” said Regina.

“Now?”

“Now.”

They stopped in full view of half of Kulm. Gottlieb lifted him down. Jacob stood by the back wheel with his shirt held up in both fists and gave the job his whole attention.

Gottlieb straightened and looked behind them. The gate was a long way back. He could still make out the two of them, one small and dark, one taller, neither moving. He lifted his hand. He could not tell whether they lifted theirs.

He lifted Jacob back up. He looked at the gate a while longer. Then he looked up at the sky. There was not a cloud in it.

---

It took three days, with Daniel's wagon following theirs through the dust. Before noon on the first day, the horses had the first of the barrel.

On the third afternoon the breeze came round from the south, cool against Gottlieb's face, and it smelled like the brine barrel. Louise lifted her head and breathed it in and said nothing.

They came down into the hollow as the sun was going.

In June there had been nothing in it. Now there were four humps of earth along the valley side, with smoke coming up out of the ground, and a few wagons standing about with their shafts down. Up on the rise were the estate's long sheep pens and its haystacks and the steward's house, the only walls in sight.

A woman climbed up out of one of the humps, wiping her hands on her apron, and called to them. She had come from the Swabian villages beyond the Dniester, they learned later, and her German was so different from theirs that Gottlieb caught one word in three. The word was *water*. She pointed down the hollow. Daniel answered her slowly and loudly, as if she were deaf, and she answered him slowly and loudly back.

Louise sat on the wagon seat with both hands on its edge and looked at the four humps of earth for a long time.

Then she began untying the shawl. “The wagon there,” she said, “with its back to the wind. The flour stays up off the ground. The children sleep in the wagon.”

Gottlieb did what she said.

The well stood at the bottom of the hollow, a long pole balanced on a forked post, with a bucket hanging from one end.

“Is it locked?” asked Regina, who had followed him down.

It had no lid and no chain.

“No.”

He let the bucket down. It struck water sooner than he expected and came up full, slopping over his boots. He drank from the edge. The water was cold and hard, with salt at the back of it, as if someone had used the bucket for pickling. He drank again. When he brought the cow down, she put her nose in, blew, and drank the bucket dry.

Then he drew another bucket and washed the dust of three days off his face and neck, and let the water run off his elbows into the grass.

“Is it good?” asked Regina.

“It's water,” he said.

Before the light went, he climbed out of the hollow to the land the estate's man had shown him and Daniel in June, knelt, and dug his fingers in.

He knew what he was looking for. On the hill at Kulm the best fields were black, and a handful squeezed hard would keep the print of your fingers. This was paler. It was still warm from the day, and when he squeezed it, it held for a moment and then crumbled. He dug down with his knife to see how deep the dark went. A hand's depth, perhaps. Then paler still.

Two horses could pull a plow through that all day, and there was more of it than he could see the end of. Whether it would hold water through a dry summer, he did not know. Nobody here knew. Nobody here had had a summer yet.

He stayed on his knees. Five people would have to live on what came out of this ground. He pressed the loose handful together once more, trying to make it hold.

He said the hymn to himself, the whole first verse, past the line where Louise always stopped: the part about the One who steers the heavens and gives the clouds and the winds their paths, and will find a path for your feet as well. Then he said it again, because the ground in his hand had not changed.

He wiped his hand on his trousers, where it hardly showed among the dust of the road, and went back down to his family.

Louise had a fire going. She had sent Regina up the slope with a basket for dried cow dung, with Jacob stumping along behind her, and Regina had carried it back at arm's length. It burned low and red and smelled like the cowshed. Gottlieb milked the cow by the wagon wheel. Louise warmed the milk in the big pot and broke the last of his mother's loaf into it. The children ate from one bowl on the ground, and Gottlieb and Louise ate what they left.

They knelt beside the wagon for the evening prayer. “Abide with us, for it is toward evening, and the day is far spent,” Gottlieb said, and they said the Lord's Prayer together. Jacob fell asleep on his knees before the Amen, with his forehead on a straw sack.

Later, the children slept across the wagon bed, and Gottlieb lay beside Louise under the cover. It moved a little in the wind. He could hear the three small breathings, a sheep bell far up on the rise, and somewhere down the hollow the Swabian woman singing a baby to sleep in words he did not know.

Louise was awake. He could tell by her breathing.

“When we came over the top,” she said, “I wanted to sit down in the road and cry.”

“I know.”

“I didn't.”

“I know that too.”

She was quiet for a while.

“Say it,” said Louise.

“Say what?”

“You know.”

“The rent isn't high.”

She laughed into his shoulder, very quietly, so as not to wake anyone.
