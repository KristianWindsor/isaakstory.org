## Chapter Six

# What the Summer Took

Kulm, Bessarabia — Summer 1831

---

The horses had been circling since before dawn, and they knew it.

Karl could feel it in the lead mare's gait — that particular sullenness that meant she had decided to give less. He walked behind the team, switch in hand, and tapped her flank. She flicked an ear back at him but did not quicken. Behind the horses the threshing stone dragged over the cut wheat with a slow tearing sound. Chaff hung in the air and settled on everything: Karl's neck, Gottfried's hair, the dark sweat spreading across the horses' withers. Every breath scraped.

Gottfried was eleven and managing the second horse better than he had on Monday. The boy had stopped yanking the lead and started reading the animal, adjusting before it slowed. Karl noticed and said nothing. There would be time later.

The bell rang.

Not noon. Noon came in its pattern, two strokes and then three. This was single strokes, evenly spaced, each one landing flat in the hot air. The toll for a death.

Karl stopped walking. The mare stopped too and swung her head toward the trough.

"Papa," Gottfried said.

"I hear it." Karl handed him the lead. "Keep them moving. Don't let her drink hot."

He crossed the yard and stepped into the street. The August glare struck him full in the face. Across the lane, Leischner's wife had come out to her gate and was shading her eyes toward the prayer house. The new stone walls stood pale and raw in the light.

The bell rang again.

Karl already knew. He had known since July, when they buried Roloff's wife without the three-day wait, without hymn stops, without anyone walking ahead with the cross. The Schulz had given word then: cholera dead went in the ground fast.

He had told himself it would not reach his family.

A man had to tell himself something.

He was already walking toward Michael's house.

---

He smelled vinegar before he reached the gate.

Not death. Not yet. Vinegar-soaked rags hung near the doorway, sharp enough to sting the eyes, and underneath was a sour bodily smell that made the skin rise on Karl's arms.

The gate stood open. Anna was in the yard between the house and the summer kitchen, wrapped in her dark shawl despite the heat. She was looking at the door as if it might still give back a different answer.

Karl went past her.

The kitchen was dim and worse — low thatch overhead, a swept clay floor, walls of mudbrick that held the August heat like a shut oven. Michael lay on the floor on the straw mattress dragged down from the sleeping room. He was alive. That was the first thing. Karl had expected the white cloth already over him. But Michael was alive, and the difference was so large Karl stopped in the doorway.

His brother's face had gone the color of unbleached linen. His eyes were sunken. The skin of his hands looked loose, as if it had come free of the man beneath it.

Three days ago they had argued in this yard about the threshing schedule. Michael had said Karl drove the horses too hard. Karl had said Michael drove them like pets. They had parted irritated, which now seemed impossible and ordinary at the same time.

Karlchen stood against the far wall with Christian beside him. Christian held his brother's sleeve in both hands. He was not crying. He was watching his father the way a child watches a wounded animal when he understands something has passed beyond repair.

Karl went to his brother.

Michael's eyes found him. He tried to speak. No words came.

"Water?" Karl asked.

Michael's mouth moved.

Karl took the clay cup from the floor and held it to his lips. Michael drank greedily, desperately, then twisted sideways and lost all of it. Karl held him through the cramp, one hand on his chest, one on his back, feeling the muscles seize with a force that should not have remained in a body so emptied.

Michael made a sound Karl had never heard from any man.

Anna came in and knelt on the other side. She took Michael's hand in both of hers and held it in her lap. No one spoke for a while. The afternoon heat pressed through the small window and lay over them.

The cramp passed. Michael's breathing thinned.

Karl stayed on the floor beside him. The boys stayed against the wall. Anna's thumb moved once over Michael's knuckles and then stopped.

Michael died in the early evening.

Quietly, after all that.

Karl sat still until his knees ached. Then he stood and went to the shelf above the bed. The three books were there — Bible, Catechism, Hymnbook — the ones Michael had carried from Pabianice in a cloth bag when they were boys walking into Bessarabia. The spines were worn smooth.

Karl took down the Hymnbook, opened it, and began to read because Michael had been owed a watch-night, and there would not be one.

He read until the light failed.

---

Women came after dark.

Karl heard the gate, low voices, a pot being set down in the summer kitchen. Elisabeth had come from their house. Louisa came from Johann's. Maria Jeske came with cloths. They did not ask what had happened. They saw the mattress, the boys, Anna's face, and went to work.

Water was heated. Linen was sorted. The room was cleared of what could be carried out and washed. Grief would wait its turn. The body had to be cleaned before morning.

Karl stood once in the doorway and watched Anna kneel beside the basin. Elisabeth had one hand on Anna's shoulder, not soft, not sentimental — just there, steadying her while Maria wrung out a cloth.

Then Karl went back to the Hymnbook.

Outside, the fire in the summer kitchen caught. Inside, Michael lay still.

---

The carpenter came at first light with boards already cut.

He measured Michael anyway. Respect, or habit, or because no one wanted to say the boards had been cut for any body of a similar size. He drove the nails straight except one near the foot. It went in crooked. He looked at Karl.

Karl took the hammer and knocked it flat.

The grave had been dug by two men with cloth tied over their mouths. At the cemetery, Karl saw three new mounds since he had last been there, each with a plain wooden cross. The paint was still bright on two.

Father stood very straight in his black coat.

He was seventy-three. He had buried infants in the first years, when fever ran through Kulm and took children before they had learned the shape of their own names. But he had not buried a son of thirty-seven. His face had the look of a man staring at a sum that had come out wrong.

Mother's lips moved without sound. She prayed when the men lifted the coffin, prayed when the ropes lowered it, prayed when the dirt began to fall. She did not cry. Karl thought perhaps crying belonged to people who still expected the world to revise itself.

The pastor from Tarutino was not there. No one had waited. The sexton read from the Psalms, his voice steady enough. Twenty people stood well apart, the way people had learned to stand since June.

Karl stood behind Christian and put his hand on the boy's back.

Christian was ten. He had not said a word since Karl entered the house the day before. Through the thin linen shirt, Karl could feel each knob of his spine.

The coffin reached the bottom.

The first dirt struck the lid.

Christian flinched once, then leaned back a fraction into Karl's hand.

The wooden cross went in at the head of the mound. Michael's name was burned into it. The dark paint had not dried and caught the morning light too brightly.

Michael had not gotten the singing. He had not gotten the three days. He had gotten a board coffin, a quick psalm, and twenty people standing at a careful distance.

Karl picked up a handful of soil and dropped it onto the mound.

The dirt fell apart before it landed.

---

Four more died in Kulm that August.

Karl kept his head down and worked. The harvest did not stop for cholera. A spoiled harvest was its own kind of dying, only slower.

At the threshing floor, the horses circled. At the well, women talked with their mouths close to one another's ears. At the tavern, the officials drank the afternoons through, and the village's business waited on them. At the cemetery, new crosses appeared faster than the grass could be cleared around them.

A farm could not run on one adult. Karl had known this all his life. Fields, animals, heavy work on one side; house, dairy, garden, children on the other. A person could do it wrong for a season. Not into winter.

He was near the mill one afternoon when Graumann fell in beside him. Graumann's sister Susanna had lost her husband to cholera in July, two weeks before Michael.

"Anna," Karl said.

Graumann did not ask what about her. They walked a few steps while the mill wheel turned with its slow, grinding patience.

"Roloff has three children and no wife," Graumann said. "And a farm."

"I know."

"Then what are you thinking about?"

Karl looked toward the road home.

"How to say it to Father."

---

Father was in the parlor, sitting in the good chair no child had ever been allowed to touch.

From the kitchen came the sound of Mother working. She had been working since the burial: bread, mending, preserves, anything that let one task follow another without silence.

Karl laid it out plainly. Anna. The boys. The farm. Winter. Georg Roloff with three small children and no woman in the house.

Father listened without interrupting.

"Michael is six weeks dead," he said.

"Yes."

Outside, a cart went by. One wheel squeaked every other turn.

"Roloff is a Kulm man," Karl said. "The boys would know where they came from. I would see to the inheritance. Whatever else changes, that stays Isaak."

Father rubbed a bit of lint from his coat and flicked it away.

"Your mother," he said.

"I know."

"She'll say it is too soon."

"It is too soon."

Father looked at him. His eyes were pale and hard. "Then why are you here?"

Karl thought of Christian's spine under his hand at the graveside. He thought of the farm going into November with no man on it, and two boys of twelve and ten. He thought of winter coming down from the north, as it did every year, without asking permission.

"Because too soon is better than too late."

Father looked at the wall again. Then he nodded once and reached for his coat.

---

On the third of October, a boy came to say Karl's sister Dorothea had been delivered safely. A son. Mother and child both well. A child come into the world in the same season it had emptied so many houses; Karl could not tell whether it was a mercy or only the world insisting on itself.

Elisabeth was setting bread near the stove. She stopped, closed her eyes for a moment, then finished covering the bowl.

"Lobe married Graumann's sister last week," Karl said.

Elisabeth sat across from him. She was thirty-two, heavy with the child due in February, her hands rough from work and warm when she put one over his.

"You've spoken to Father," she said.

"Yes."

"She will say yes."

Karl looked at her.

"Anna," Elisabeth said. "She said yes in her mind the week Michael died. She has only been waiting for the rest of you to arrive there."

"It does not make it easier."

"No."

That was all she said. It was enough.

---

Anna was splitting kindling when Karl came through the gate the following week.

Small, precise strokes. The axe head flashed, fell, split, rose again. Her dark shawl was wrapped tight though the air was not yet cold. The yard looked held together by will: the woodpile too low, the trough needing scrubbing, the barn door propped shut with a stick where the hinge had pulled loose.

Two-person jobs. Strong-man jobs.

Karl waited until she drove the axe into the block and left it there.

"Roloff came Tuesday," she said.

Karl had not known. "How was it?"

She gave a short sound. Not quite a laugh. "He stood in the street with his hat in his hands. Said he was sorry for my loss. We both knew that was not why he had come."

She picked up kindling and stacked it against the wall.

"I told him I needed two weeks."

"Do you?"

She turned. Her face had thinned since August. The softness that had been there before was gone. He doubted it would come back.

"No," she said. "I don't."

Karl looked at the barn door with the stick against it.

"The boys keep their name," he said. "What Michael left them stays theirs. I'll stand for that before Schimke."

"I know you will."

"Anna."

She stopped.

"Are you all right with this?"

For a while she did not answer. Then she said, without turning around, "No. But I'll do it anyway."

She picked up another piece of wood.

"That is all right can mean, sometimes."

Karl left her to the work.

He was almost through the gate when she called after him.

"Karl."

He turned.

"Tell Christian to come home for supper. He's been going to your mother's. I don't want him thinking he needs to disappear."

"I'll tell him."

---

Christian was at Mother's table, as Anna had said, sitting very still while Mother set out the evening bread.

Karl sat across from him.

"Your mother says come home for supper."

Christian nodded.

"She misses you."

The boy looked down.

Karl took the French sou from his pocket and set it on the table. He had carried it since Pabianice, a useless old coin worn smooth by years of being kept.

Christian looked at it.

"Keep that on you," Karl said.

"Why?"

"So you remember you're an Isaak."

Christian picked up the coin and turned it in his fingers.

"You'll live in Roloff's house," Karl said. "You'll eat at his table. That's all right. But you're Michael's son. Your grandfather is my father. Your name is the same as mine."

Christian's hand closed around the coin.

"What if I forget?"

"Then I'll remind you."

Christian looked at him with Michael's eyes.

"That's what I'm for," Karl said.

The boy put the coin in his pocket.

Mother set the bread down without comment.

---

The eighteenth of October came with rain.

The hill was mud to the ankle. Horses slipped pulling the cart to the prayer house. Women lifted skirts and still arrived with hems fouled brown. The new plaster inside smelled of lime and damp wool.

The sexton married them.

There was no music. There had not been music at a Kulm wedding in years, the elders seeing to that, and no one would have danced at this one anyway. Georg Roloff and Anna stood at the front in dark clothes and said what was required of them.

Karl stood beside Father and watched his brother's wife take another man's name.

It was right.

It was also a grief.

Both things stood together, refusing to cancel each other.

Afterward they walked to the Roloff house for the meal, because it was larger and there were now seven who needed to fit inside it.

The table was tight.

Karl Ludwig, six and already solemn, planted himself at the far end and dared anyone to move him. Michael Ferdinand wanted to sit next to Georg and kept climbing off the bench until Georg put a hand on his shoulder and held him there. Christine sat on Anna's lap, grabbing for bread with both fists.

Karlchen sat as far from Georg as the bench allowed, which was not far.

Christian sat beside him. His hand went once to his pocket, touching the coin through the cloth.

Georg poured wine and said a short grace. No flourishes. Then they ate.

Georg's children ate fast and without looking around. Michael's boys ate slowly and looked at everything.

At some point Karl Ludwig reached across Christian and took the heel of bread from beside his plate. Not snatching. Just taking, with the confidence of a child who knew whose table this was.

Christian looked at the empty place where the bread had been.

Then he looked at Karl.

Karl held his gaze and did not move.

Christian looked at Georg's boy. Then he looked back at his plate and ate what he had.

Small thing.

Not pretty.

But Karl thought: good. Learn the house. Learn when to fight. Learn when not to spend yourself on bread.

Across the table, Elisabeth had Christine's dropped spoon in one hand and was telling Michael Ferdinand to keep his elbow out of the cabbage. Louisa cut meat for one child and slid a piece toward another without asking whose child was whose. Anna ate with Christine on her lap, one-handed and tired. Georg reached for the bread, hesitated, broke his own piece in half, and put part of it beside Christian's plate.

Christian looked at it.

This time he took it.

Outside, the rain came back, softer than before. It fell on the hill, on the mud, on the new graves and the old ones, on the threshing floor where the wheat was finally done.

Karl looked down the table at the five children who were not yet a family.

Winter was coming.

There was still work to do.
