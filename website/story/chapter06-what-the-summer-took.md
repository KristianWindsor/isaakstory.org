## Chapter Six

# What the Summer Took

Kulm, Bessarabia — Summer 1831

---

The sickle was a brutal, uncompromising tool. It demanded that a man bend himself double, bringing his face close to the baking black earth to hook and sever the stalks of wheat.

Karl August Isaak straightened up, his breath coming hot and ragged. He wiped the stinging sweat from his eyes with a dirt-caked wrist and looked across the simmering field. Beside him, his thirty-seven-year-old brother, Michael, worked steadily, his broad shoulders rising and falling in the suffocating, motionless heat. A few paces ahead, Karl's eleven-year-old son, Gottfried, gathered the cut stalks. The air was a heavy blanket. The steppe was baking dry, the dust rising in choking clouds with every step.

From the center of the village, a bell tolled.

Michael paused, leaning heavily on his sickle. The tolling drifted over the golden stubble, heavy and flat in the dead air. They did not need to ask who it was for. The cholera had been crawling up the valley from the lower colonies for weeks, a quiet, invisible tide. Two weeks ago, it had taken Anna Dorothea Roloff. The bell was ringing entirely too often now. It was the sound of the summer being dismantled.

"It is the heat," Michael muttered, his voice suddenly thin. He dropped his sickle, clutching his stomach. 

Karl turned just as his brother’s knees buckled. 

The sickness did not arrive with a long, wasting fever or a dignified decline. Cholera was a violent, humiliating thief. By the time Karl half-carried, half-dragged his brother back to the village, the violent, uncontrollable purging had begun. The smell filled Michael’s small house instantly—a foul, sickly-sweet, rotting stench of watery sickness that clung to the back of the throat and settled permanently into the packed earth walls. 

Karl sat on a low wooden stool beside the bed. The heat inside the room was absolute, compounded by the horrifying odor. Michael lay on the mattress, his face already becoming unrecognizable. The water was pouring out of him, stripping the life from his tissues in a matter of hours. The flesh was sinking tightly against the bones of his skull, his skin turning a terrible, ashen grey. His eyes were hollowing out, staring blindly at the thatched ceiling. A man built to break oxen and cut the earth was being reduced to a husk.

There was no doctor, no medicine that could touch it. Karl sat in helpless, suffocating silence. He looked at the wooden shelf above the bed, where the three sacred books from Pabianice sat perfectly aligned: the heavy family Bible, the Catechism, the worn *Gesangbuch*. They were the anchors of their faith, but today, they offered no water to a dying man.

Anna, Michael’s wife, knelt on the other side of the bed. She was constantly wiping her husband's face and cleaning the fouled linens with a damp rag, ignoring the horrific smell, her movements frantic and precise, fighting a battle she had already lost.

Karl reached out and placed his heavy, calloused hand over Michael’s cold fingers. He did not speak. There was nothing to say. Just before sunset, Michael took a shallow, rattling breath, and did not take another.

---

Father and Mother received the news in the yard of the main house.

Karl stood by the gate and watched the unnatural order of the world fracture. Father, seventy-three years old and weathered like ancient oak, seemed to shrink instantly, his broad shoulders bowing under an invisible, crushing weight. He turned away, bracing both hands against the wooden fence, his knuckles turning white as he stared out at the barren steppe.

But it was Mother who terrified Karl. She did not scream or collapse. She stood perfectly still, her face turning to stone. She walked past Karl without a word, heading straight for Michael's house to wash her firstborn son's body.

Karl followed her inside and stood in the shadows. He watched his mother systematically strip the soiled linens, her hands moving with a grim, shattering efficiency. This was the woman who had carried the infant Michael across freezing Polish mud, who had starved to feed him, who had shielded him from French muskets and Russian drafts. Now, she was preparing him for the dirt. Karl saw the absolute, hollow devastation in the rigid line of her jaw, and he realized for the first time the terrifying, unshielded vulnerability of being a parent.

---

The burial was hurried.

The heat demanded it. There was no ornate coffin, only a plain pine box hammered together by the neighbors. The parish pastor was eleven kilometers away in Tarutino, overwhelmed by the dying in his own streets. The village schoolmaster stood at the head of the open grave, reading the ancient liturgy from the *Gesangbuch* in a voice that trembled slightly in the dry wind.

Behind the cemetery, the unfinished stone walls of the prayer house stood like a ruin.

Karl stood at the edge of the pit. Beside him stood Christian, Michael’s ten-year-old son. The boy was dressed in a woolen jacket that was too heavy for the heat, his face pale and set in a mask of rigid, desperate stoicism. He stared down at the pine box, refusing to cry in front of the village men.

Karl stepped closer to his nephew. He raised his hand and brought it down firmly against the small of Christian’s back, pressing his palm between the boy's shoulder blades. He held it there—a heavy, silent anchor. Christian flinched once, then leaned a fraction of an inch backward into Karl’s hand, anchoring himself to the weight.

---

By September, the practical horror of the world had set in.

Karl watched Anna hauling a massive wooden bucket of water from the village well. She was a strong, capable woman, but the dark circles under her eyes were bruised and deep. She had two boys to feed—Christian and twelve-year-old Karl—and a harvest that still needed processing. The brutal arithmetic of the colony was perfectly clear: a farm required a man's back and a woman's hands to survive the winter. One adult could not hold the line against the frost.

The village was already adapting, re-knitting its torn fabric with grim, necessary speed. On the fourth of October, Heinrich Lobe, a widower, married Susanna Graumann, a widow. It was not romance; it was survival. 

Karl felt the desperate pull to simply take Anna and the boys into his own house. But the reality stopped him cold. His own house was full. His wife, Elisabeth, was heavily pregnant, their next child due in February. They had Gottfried and little Andreas. He could not feed three more mouths on his own harvest without risking his own children to the winter hunger.

---

A week later, the match was proposed.

Father and Mayor Schimke sat in Karl's kitchen, the mood heavy and pragmatic. Georg Roloff needed a mother for his three tiny children. Anna needed a man to plow her fields and chop her winter wood. Two halved houses could be driven together to make one whole.

Karl recoiled, a surge of hot, visceral anger rising in his throat. "Michael is barely cold in the ground," he said sharply, glaring at the mayor. "It is an insult. It hasn't even been ten weeks."

"It is the frost, Karl," Father said quietly, his old voice thick with exhaustion. "The frost does not care about Michael's memory. The boys must eat."

Karl stood up, his chair scraping violently against the packed earth, and walked out into the dusty yard. He could not stomach it. He could not watch another man step into his brother's house, sit at his brother's table, and command his brother's sons.

---

On the third of October, Karl's sister Dorothea gave birth to a child.

The sharp, sudden cry of new life echoing down the village street cracked something open in Karl. He sat on the edge of his own bed that evening, his head in his hands.

Elisabeth sat heavily beside him. She placed her hand on his knee. "She is not replacing him, Karl," she said, her voice grounded, carrying the quiet, absolute authority of a woman who understands the brutal mechanics of keeping children alive. "She is surviving. Michael would want his sons fed. He would want them warm when the snow comes."

Karl closed his eyes, the anger draining out of him, leaving only a hollow, aching clarity. Elisabeth was right.

The next morning, Karl walked down the street to Michael's house. He found Christian chopping kindling in the yard, wielding the heavy ax with too much force, trying to be the man of a house that was about to be given away.

Karl knelt in the dirt so he was eye-level with his ten-year-old nephew. He reached into his vest pocket and pulled out the smooth, worn French sou he had carried since 1812. He took Christian's small, rough hand and pressed the heavy coin firmly into his palm, folding the boy's fingers over it.

"You are an Isaak," Karl said, his voice low and fierce. "No matter whose roof you sleep under, or whose bread you eat this winter. Your father’s name is in your blood, and it is in this dirt. I will watch you. I will stand for you. Do you understand?"

Christian looked at the coin, his jaw tight, his eyes shining with unshed tears. He nodded once, sharply.

Karl stood up. He walked into the house, took the heavy family Bible, the Catechism, and the *Gesangbuch* from the shelf, and carried them out to the boy. He placed them in Christian's arms. The weight of his father's faith, passing into his keeping.

---

The wedding took place on the eighteenth of October.

The brutal summer heat had finally broken, replaced by a freezing, driving autumn rain that turned the broad village street into a river of thick, clinging mud. There was no dancing. There was no loud celebration or flowing schnapps. The mood in the prayer house was sober, rain-soaked, and heavy with the ghosts of the summer.

Georg Roloff and Anna stood together at the front. They looked tired, two battered survivors agreeing to pull the same cart.

Behind them stood the newly blended family. Five children in a line. The three tiny Roloffs—Karl Ludwig, Michael Ferdinand, and baby Christine—stood next to Michael’s sons.

Karl stood at the back of the room, his jaw set, holding his hard-won peace. His eyes bypassed the couple and locked onto Christian.

The ten-year-old boy stood perfectly straight in his dark wool coat, the heavy French sou heavy in his pocket, the three sacred books waiting back at the house. He was stepping into a new life, under a new man's roof, but his chin was raised with a fierce, unmistakable pride. Karl watched his nephew step forward into the muddy, uncertain future, carrying the quiet, unspoken weight of a line that would endure.
