## Chapter Seven

# A Wolf in the Hollow

Kulm, Bessarabia — April 1849

---

From the top of the Kulm hill, a wolf hunt looked a great deal like standing still.

Gottlieb had been standing still for most of an hour. The mare had been eating for all of it. Now and then she lifted her head, looked down the empty slope, and put it back down.

Below them the ground fell away into burian, the dead weeds of last summer, grey and taller than a man. They grew all the way down to the reeds in the hollow. The dry stalks rattled in the wind. Two hundred paces to his left, Wilhelm Schulz sat on his father's horse. Two hundred paces to his right, cousin August waited on his own. Beyond them, more riders stood along the ridge. Wilhelm Leischner had put them there one by one and told each of them to stay.

The bailiff had come down the street at dusk the night before, calling at every gate. Riders, beaters, hunters, at the village office at eight. Two lambs gone in two nights, and a colt found in the hollow with its belly opened. At supper his father had said, "Gottlieb takes the mare," and nothing more.

This morning in the yard, Gottlieb had lifted the saddle onto her back. She had walked him halfway to the shed while he tried to buckle the strap beneath her belly.

"She thinks it's breakfast," his little brother August had said from the fence.

"She thinks everything is breakfast."

His mother had come out and straightened his cap, then straightened it again. It had been Gottfried's cap.

"God go with you," she had said.

His father had tested the strap with two fingers. Once Gottlieb was on her back, he had handed up the stick from beside the door, the one for the night watch. It was oak, as thick as Gottlieb's wrist, worn dark where hands had held it. It was heavier than it looked.

"You're not there to catch it. You're there so it can't get past you. If she runs, let her run. And bring her home on the same legs she left on."

Outside the village office, cousin Christian had been turning his tall chestnut in tight circles to show what she could do.

Louise Schulz had pushed between the horses with the bread her brother had forgotten. She had looked up at Gottlieb and said, "Bring me back its wool."

"Wolves don't have wool."

"Then bring me whatever they have."

Now he sat with the stick across his knees. The wind found the sweat under his cap and turned it cold. Down in the hollow, three vultures rose from the reeds and circled slowly. That was where the colt was. By the mare's feet a ground squirrel sat up, whistled at him, and dropped out of sight.

Far below he could hear the beaters: a shout, an answering shout, the knock of sticks on dead weeds. It went on a long time and came no closer.

*Lord,* he thought, *let it come to someone else.*

That seemed a poor thing to ask. He added: *Or let me hold.*

A cry went up on the far side of the hollow and passed along the line until it reached him. The mare's head came up with grass hanging out of her mouth.

Below him, the burian was moving.

---

The tops of the stalks parted and closed in a line coming up the hill. Then something yellow-grey came out onto the open grass. Gottlieb's first thought was *dog*. His second was that no dog ran like that: low and level, its head thrust forward, its tail straight out behind. It was smaller than he'd expected, no bigger than a sheepdog. It ran as though the hill belonged to it.

It was heading for the gap between him and Wilhelm.

Wilhelm saw it too. He raised his stick and opened his mouth to shout. Then his horse saw it, turned, and bolted. It went over the ridge toward Kulm, Wilhelm flat along its neck with both fists full of mane. His stick bounced down the slope behind him.

The gap was twice as wide as it had been.

The mare spun.

The turn threw Gottlieb sideways. For a moment he hung off her shoulder, one arm around her neck, the grass passing close beneath his face. *She's going home,* he thought. *Like Wilhelm's horse. My father will hear it from half of Kulm before I'm back.* He caught a fistful of mane and hauled himself up.

She wasn't facing home. She was facing the wolf.

Her ears were flat. She stretched her neck out low and gave a squeal like a door on a bad hinge. He had never heard that sound from her, in all the years she had carried sacks and children and him. Perhaps she had faced a wolf before, out with the village herd.

The wolf was still heading for the gap. Gottlieb turned the mare downhill to meet it. She pulled against the rein.

*If she runs, let her run.*

He let her run.

She charged down the slope, faster than he had ever known her to go. Gottlieb let out a yell and swung the stick over his head. The wolf stopped short. It was close enough now for him to see its pale eyes. They were fixed on the mare's feet.

It turned across the slope toward cousin August, who was already riding down to meet it with his stick out.

"Down!" August shouted. "Keep it down!"

The wolf turned again, downhill, back toward the beaters and the dirt road where the wagons waited. Gottlieb and August went after it side by side. The mare's hooves slid on the wet clay, caught, slid again. Mud spattered his trousers. He had both hands in her mane and the stick clamped under one arm, and he could not have hit anything if the wolf had stopped and asked him to.

Behind them, the other riders came pouring down the slope. Beaters burst out of the weeds with their clubs raised, shouting to one another. The vultures flapped away over their heads.

On the road below, Mr. Schulz, Wilhelm's father, stood on his wagon seat and lifted his long gun to his shoulder.

The shot cracked across the hollow. Smoke leaped from the barrel, and a clod of earth flew up three horse lengths behind the wolf. It did not so much as flick an ear. By the time Mr. Schulz had his powder horn out, the hunt had swept past his wagon. Gottlieb caught the sharp smell of burned powder.

A horse passed him at a full gallop with nobody on it at all.

It was cousin Christian's tall chestnut, the stirrups swinging and the reins flying. She rushed between the riders and right past the wolf without a glance. Then she swerved around a beater and went back up the slope toward Kulm.

They drove the wolf back and forth along the hollow until the shouting grew hoarse. Each time it tried to break uphill, a rider turned it back. At last it came back toward the wagons with its head down and its mouth hanging open.

Beside the road, the wolf dropped into a ditch half full of dead leaves. The bank beneath the road rose steeply behind it. Beaters were running along the ditch from one end. At the other, Gottfried and two riders were turning their horses across its path.

The wolf turned back toward the slope. The mare was coming down at it. It stopped in the leaves, its head low and its lips drawn back from its teeth.

Gottlieb slowed the mare to a walk. Her sides heaved beneath his legs. He could hear her breathing and his own.

---

Gottfried, his eldest brother, climbed down from his horse and walked toward the ditch with the club in both hands. He did not hurry.

The wolf lunged at his legs. Gottfried stepped back and brought the club down.

There was a dull crack. The wolf fell on its side. Gottfried struck again. One hind leg scraped through the leaves, then lay still.

Men were shouting. Gottlieb slid off the mare and stood holding her rein. His knees shook.

There were burrs in the wolf's coat and ticks in its ears as fat and grey as peas. Its tongue lay in the dirt with grit on it.

Gottfried wiped the club on the grass. He came over, put his hand on the back of Gottlieb's neck the way their father did, and shook him once.

"It's dead," he said. "You can stop looking at it."

---

They hung it by its hind legs over the side of Schulz's wagon. Mr. Schulz sat up straight beside it, his gun between his knees, as though the clod of earth had been part of the plan.

Then they came back over the ridge and down into Kulm, shouting. The horses steamed in the cool air. As they reached the long street, the men began to sing. Two guns went off, one after the other, and every dog in Kulm went mad. Children ran alongside to look at the wolf, ran away screaming whenever its head swung toward them, then ran back to do it again.

Louise was at the Schulz gate. She looked straight at the wolf as it went past. Then she searched the riders until she found Gottlieb.

He brought the mare alongside the wagon, leaned over, and pulled a tuft of fur from the wolf's neck. It felt coarse and greasy between his fingers. He held it down to her.

She took it and rubbed it between her finger and thumb.

"It isn't wool," she said.

"I told you."

She closed her hand around it. "It'll do."

Behind her, Wilhelm stood holding his horse, which had run exactly as far as its own yard and then stopped to be caught.

"You screamed," Wilhelm said.

"I shouted."

"I heard you from the other end of the hill."

"You *were* at the other end of the hill. You were halfway home."

Cousin August, riding past, said without turning his head, "He closed the gap," and rode on.

Wilhelm opened his mouth, then shut it.

At the village office they pulled Gottfried off his horse and threw him in the air three times, with a roar each time. Then the mayor climbed onto the wagon and sold the hide. Old Graumann bought it and sent a boy to his cellar for wine.

Cousin Christian came down the middle of the street on foot, mud to his knees and his cap in his hand. The chestnut was already tied at the hitching rail, eating someone else's hay.

"Christian! Your horse beat you home!"

Christian looked at the chestnut. The chestnut did not look at Christian.

"She wanted to tell it first," he said.

They threw him in the air too, for that.

---

His father was at the gate.

Gottlieb had known he would be. It still caught in his throat to see him there, hands behind his back, watching the street for a brown mare. He looked at Gottlieb once. Then he looked at the mare.

"How is she?"

"She ran."

Gottlieb slid down.

His father took the rein and ran a hand down each of her legs, feeling for heat. The mare put her nose against his shoulder and left it there.

"Hm," his father said. "Same legs." He straightened and laid a hand on her neck. "Leischner came by. He says you closed the gap."

"She did," Gottlieb said. "I stayed on."

His father nodded and handed back the rein.

"Rub her down. All of her. Then you can eat."

His younger brothers came running from the house as he pulled a handful of straw from the stack.

"Did you see it?" August asked.

"Did you hit it?"

"Gottfried did."

"Was it as big as the camel?" Johann asked.

"Not quite."

Gottlieb went to work on the mare's shoulder, where the sweat had dried in white lines. The straw rasped over her coat. He told them as he worked, beginning with the wolf coming up through the weeds. He left out the part where he had hung off the mare with his face over the ground. Johann made him tell the bit about Christian's horse twice.

The mare leaned into his hand and let out a long sigh. Then she swung her head toward the shed and went back to thinking about breakfast.
